The synthesis · what repeats across all 13

The patterns

Line up everyone the attention economy touched, and one theme dominates: the fight isn't for market share — it's for the same finite minutes, and the algorithm decides who gets them.

Each page asks one question: what did the attention economy make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions keep showing up. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

Content became infinite and free, so the only scarce thing left is your attention — and the only real power is the algorithm that decides what fills it. Value flows to whoever captures minutes; it drains from anyone who assumed a captive, paying audience. And because winning finite attention means optimizing for engagement, the whole system quietly tilts toward outrage, addiction, and impulse — not truth or wellbeing.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

The algorithm beats the content

When anyone can make anything, making it is worthless and surfacing it is everything. So the recommendation engine — not the show, the song, or the post — became the scarce, valuable asset. Whoever controls the feed controls the attention and the money.

Pattern 02

The fight is for minutes, not market share

Everything competes with everything for the same fixed hours. Netflix's rival isn't Disney+ — it's TikTok, gaming, and sleep ("we compete with sleep"). The pie of attention doesn't grow, so every product is really fighting every other product for your time.

Pattern 03

Engagement is not truth — or wellbeing

Optimizing for "what holds attention" rewards outrage, conspiracy, addiction, and impulse, because those hold attention best. Nobody designed this; it falls out of maximizing one number. The cost — polarization, loneliness, worse focus — lands on users, not platforms.

Pattern 04

The captive-audience model died

The old giants assumed you'd keep showing up — for the bundle, the newspaper, the movie. When attention could go anywhere, that captive audience walked, and the businesses built on holding it hostage collapsed.

Pattern 05

The winner re-becomes what it killed

Streaming beat cable by being cheap and ad-free — then, out of growth, added ads, bundles, price hikes, and lock-in, re-creating cable. Kill the incumbent, become the incumbent. The bundle everyone fled is quietly reassembling.

streaming warsthe bundle
Pattern 06

The business is misaligned with the user

When the metric is engagement, the company profits from keeping you hooked — sometimes from your failure. A dating app earns when you stay single and swiping; a shopping feed earns from the impulse you'll regret. Your goal and theirs point opposite ways.

dating appssocial commercethe algorithm
Pattern 07

The format reshapes the art itself

The container changes the thing inside it. Made for the skip button and the 15-second clip, songs got shorter and front-loaded; vertical video became the default of the whole internet. Distribution didn't just move the art — it rewrote it.

Pattern 08

Discovery replaced search — the thing finds you

You stop looking; the feed decides. The song, the product, the opinion, the match arrives mid-scroll, chosen by an algorithm reading your behavior. It collapses the gap between entertainment and action — and quietly removes your intent from the loop.

Eight forces, one method

The point of doing all eight

This is the eighth collection built the same way — after AI, Ozempic, the decoupling, the end of cheap money, the energy transition, the demographic cliff, and the climate bill. Each force has its own signature: AI's is "you can only trust what you can verify," Ozempic's is "one force, many industries," the decoupling's is "the policy backfires and the walls leak," cheap money's is "the tide went out and revealed who was swimming naked," the transition's is "a fuel you rent forever becomes a machine you build once," the cliff's is "for 200 years there were always more people coming — that just stopped," the climate bill's is "a stable climate was a hidden subsidy — the bill is coming due," and the attention economy's is "attention is the last scarce resource — everything competes for the same minutes." But the method is identical: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group by the answer, and read the patterns. Point it at anything big and the stories fall out.
