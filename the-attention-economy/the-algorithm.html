Group A · The attention machine

The algorithm — when content went infinite, the feed became the power

Once anyone can make anything, the scarce, valuable thing is deciding what you actually see.

This is the engine under the whole thing. When making content became nearly free and infinite (anyone with a phone), the bottleneck flipped from making it to surfacing it. So the recommendation algorithm — the thing that decides what you see — became the scarce, valuable asset. Whoever controls the feed controls the attention and the ad money.

The shift: from your friends to "For You"

Feeds moved from chronological and social ("posts from people you follow, newest first") to algorithmic ("For You" — whatever keeps you watching), roughly 2015–2022. The algorithm's single goal: engagement = time-on-app = ad revenue.

2018
Facebook whistleblower Frances Haugen's later leaks (2021) showed the company's own research: the engagement-ranked feed amplified divisive content and harmed teens — and it shipped anyway, because engagement made the most money.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — engagement isn't truth

Optimizing for "what holds your attention" is not the same as optimizing for what's true, good, or good for you. An engagement-maximizing feed structurally rewards outrage, conspiracy, extremes, and addictive design — because those hold attention best. It's not a bug; it's what the optimization naturally produces.

The surprising part

People assume the algorithm is a neutral piece of math. It isn't — it's a value system written as math, reflecting one choice: maximize engagement above all else. And the uncomfortable part is that the platforms' own internal research showed this was harming users (mental health, polarization, misinformation) — and they kept it, because engagement is what pays. The algorithm wasn't an accident. It was a decision.

In one sentence

The algorithm in a nutshell

When content became infinite, the power moved from making it to surfacing it — so the feed became the most valuable thing in media, and the choice to optimize it for engagement rather than truth quietly reshaped what billions of people see and believe.
