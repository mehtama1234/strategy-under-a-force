Group E · The big picture

The attention economy — the whole force in one page

One idea ties every page together: attention is the last scarce resource, and everything now competes for the same minutes.

Step back from the twelve stories and one shift explains them all. For most of history, the scarce things were physical — land, oil, labor, money. Now making content and connection is nearly free and there's an infinite amount, so the one thing that stays fixed is human attention (about 24 hours a day, a fixed set of eyeballs). The whole economy reorganizes around capturing it.

The one idea: attention is the bottleneck

The whole force in a sentence

Two consequences follow. One: because content is infinite, the power moves from making it to surfacing it — so the algorithm beats the content. Two: attention is zero-sum for time — every minute on TikTok is a minute not on Netflix, the news, a book, or sleep — so the fight isn't for market share, it's for minutes, and everything competes with everything. Value flows to whoever captures and holds attention; it drains from anyone who assumed a captive or paying audience.

Where the frame holds — and where it bends

The idea isn't new — Herbert Simon named the "attention economy" back in 1971; what's new is the scale. And it has honest caveats. Attention isn't perfectly fixed — screen time can grow by stealing from sleep or books. It isn't perfectly zero-sum minute for minute. Curation and trust still win loyal time (the best shows, papers, podcasts). And regulation is arriving (TikTok bans, Apple's privacy change, the EU's Digital Services Act).

The scale of the shift

~4.6 hrs/day
Average global screen time (2024), up from ~3 hours in 2012 — roughly a third of every waking day claimed by the feed.
~$600B+
Global digital ad market (2024), nearly triple 2010 — the biggest companies on earth are, at heart, attention businesses.

Who wins, who loses

Win

Platforms, the recommendation algorithms (the gatekeeper is the product), the top sliver of creators, and advertisers who master targeting.

Lose

Old media, anyone with a captive-audience model — and, arguably, users' focus, sleep, and shared sense of reality.

The biggest tension

The open question

Engagement is not the same as wellbeing or truth. To win finite attention, everything is optimized for the triggers that hold eyes longest — outrage, extremes, addiction loops — creating a cost (polarization, misinformation, worse mental health) that users pay and platforms don't. Regulation is starting, but the money in capturing attention still beats the pressure to stop.

The surprising part

Here's the paradox: the more infinite content becomes — especially with AI generating it for free — the more valuable scarce human attention and the gatekeeping algorithm become. AI made creation free but made attention more precious. The only things that stay real in a flood of machine-made slop are human judgment, human voice, human time. So the winners won't be the content-makers; they'll be whoever controls which content reaches you. In the system we built, humans became the rarest resource.

In one sentence

The attention economy in a nutshell

Attention is now the only truly scarce thing in an infinite-content world — so whoever controls the algorithm that decides what you see holds enormous power over what you think, buy, believe, and become.
