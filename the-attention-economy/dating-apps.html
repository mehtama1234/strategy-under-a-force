Group D · Attention eats everything

Dating apps — a slot machine that profits when you stay single

Finding a partner got turned into an endless, gamified swipe. And the app makes money when you fail.

Dating apps turned finding a partner into an endless, gamified, swipe-based attention machine. The uncomfortable core: they earn from engagement and subscriptions, so their incentive is to keep you swiping and paying — not to help you succeed and leave. The app's revenue and the user's goal point in opposite directions.

The model, and the crack in it

  • The game: swipe, match, dopamine — plus paywalls on the good features (see who liked you, unlimited swipes, boost your profile).
  • The turn: after a long boom, growth stalled and fatigue set in (2023–24). Tinder's paying users declined; Bumble's stock fell from a ~$43 IPO to ~$10–15; Match Group's stock roughly halved from its 2021 peak.
  • Gen Z reports "swipe fatigue," burnout, and frustration with paywalls — even as loneliness rises.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the model is misaligned with you

The business makes the most money when you stay single, engaged, and paying. The moment you find a partner, you leave and the revenue stops. So the algorithms and paywalls are quietly tuned to keep you swiping, not to match you well — even if that means wasting your time.

The surprising part

Dating apps promised to cure loneliness by removing the friction of meeting people. Instead they created a new kind of friction — infinite choice, paywall gates, and low-quality matches optimized for engagement rather than connection. The result: users report being lonelier and more burned out, and paying more, while the app companies post falling growth. The technology meant to democratize dating became a machine for monetizing loneliness.

In one sentence

Dating apps in a nutshell

Dating apps turned finding love into a slot machine built to keep you swiping and paying — a business that quietly profits from your failure to find a partner, and is now cracking as users burn out.
