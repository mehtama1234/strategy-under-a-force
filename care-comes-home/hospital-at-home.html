Group A · The care that moved home

Hospital-at-home

IV drugs, daily doctor visits, and telemetry — for pneumonia or heart failure — in your own bedroom.

A newer model delivers actual acute, hospital-level care in the home — IV meds, monitoring, and daily nurse and doctor visits — for conditions that used to require a hospital bed (pneumonia, heart failure, infections). Medicare's "Acute Hospital Care at Home" waiver, launched during COVID, let hospitals bill for it, and Mayo, Kaiser, and Mass General Brigham scaled it. Studies show lower cost and fewer readmissions — but the waiver keeps needing renewal.

The core bet

Deliver genuine acute-level care in the home instead of a hospital bed, for the same conditions that used to demand a 4–5 day stay, at 20–30% lower cost with fewer readmissions. The medicine works; the reimbursement is the fragile part.

Lives on a fragile waiver

The entire reimbursement hinges on repeated CMS waiver renewals — there's no permanent billing authority. A hospital can't confidently build a 50-bed home-acute program or hire stable home-based nurses when payment might vanish in 12–24 months. So only the largest integrated systems can absorb the risk, narrowing access and blocking smaller entrants.

Who ends up ahead

Big integrated systems (Kaiser, Mayo) and monitoring vendors — and Medicare, which pockets the per-episode savings.

Who's exposed

Smaller hospitals frozen out by policy risk, and the model itself if the waiver lapses.

The single biggest worry

Without permanent statutory authority, the service line is one policy lapse from collapse — the margin is wafer-thin under normal payment, so the whole thing depends on continued government blessing that arrives only in short, uncertain extensions.

The surprising part

Counterintuitively, hospital-at-home often doesn't free up beds — most cases are admissions that would have happened anyway, now delivered at home. Hospital census stays flat, so the cost savings accrue almost entirely to Medicare, not the hospital, which still deploys nurses and monitoring. That's exactly why policy support matters so much: remove the waiver, and the thin margin makes the whole line collapse.

In one sentence

Hospital-at-home made the hospital bed unnecessary for many acute cases at 20–30% less cost and fewer readmissions — but none of it survives without continued government reimbursement.
