Group A · The care that moved home

Home health & senior care

Nearly nine in ten seniors want to stay in their own home — so a $140B industry brings the care to them.

As boomers age and refuse to move into facilities, a huge industry cares for them at home: skilled home health (nurses and therapy after a hospital stay, Medicare-paid) and non-medical home care (aides helping with bathing, meals, and companionship, mostly paid out of pocket or by Medicaid). Companies like Addus, Optum, Amedisys, and thousands of local agencies run it — bottlenecked by a shortage of low-paid aides.

The core bet

Whoever captures home-care supply — the aides, scheduling, billing, and compliance — wins a $140B+ recurring revenue stream, because most seniors refuse institutions and the demographics are relentless. The scarce input isn't demand; it's the worker.

Two industries in one

The two halves have completely different economics: skilled home health is Medicare-code-driven at ~8–12% margins; non-medical personal care is roughly half Medicaid (at state-capped rates often below $20/hr) and half private-pay (which can bear $20–40/hr). One is a reimbursement business; the other is a labor-and-cash business — but both run into the same wall: not enough aides.

Who ends up ahead

Agencies and payers who lock up home-care supply — and affluent seniors who can privately fund it.

Who's exposed

The aides (near-minimum wage, no benefits), and low-income seniors on Medicaid's rate-capped care.

The single biggest worry

The labor trap is unsolvable within current economics: aides do physical, intimate, exhausting work for near-minimum wage with no security, so turnover runs 40–60% and continuity collapses. Raising pay to retention levels (~$22–25/hr) would erase margins under Medicaid's ~$18–22/hr caps — a doom loop of low pay → workers leave → quality drops → hospital admissions rise.

The surprising part

The industry's growth has little to do with seniors getting sicker — it's demographic math and family change. Boomers aren't sicker than their parents; they're older, more likely to live alone, and their care once fell to daughters and female relatives as unpaid labor. As women entered the workforce, that free care vanished — so the "market" for home care is really the monetization of care that used to be free, now done by mostly immigrant women for $15/hr.

In one sentence

Home care is a structural arbitrage — the care families used to give free is now paid work done by low-wage, mostly immigrant women, letting affluent seniors age privately while public-pay systems strain.
