Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The limits

Home care is cheaper and often better — but it trades auditable oversight for rampant fraud and quiet denial.

The honest counterweight. Fraud is rampant — home health and hospice are among the most fraud-prone Medicare areas (ghost patients, upcoding). Quality is hard to oversee across thousands of homes. Medicare Advantage insurers face scrutiny for denying care and upcoding. Value-based-care startups burned huge money and some collapsed (Cano Health, Babylon). And "home" can mean isolation and burden dumped on families.

The core bet

Home care promised cheaper, faster, more humane care — but the model works only if fraud stays rare, quality oversight reaches thousands of homes, and families can absorb the burden. None of those bets are holding cleanly.

The dark side of "home"

Home care's economics make fraud structurally attractive: billing is remote, visits are quick to document or fabricate, and verification is slow and costly, so high-fraud hospices can bill for years before being shut down. And the incentive is perverse — the more you bill the more you earn, and the auditing lag means the money is gone before the fraud is caught.

Who ends up ahead

Fraudulent agencies and cost-minimizing insurers, in the near term.

Who's exposed

Legitimate patients denied care, taxpayers footing upcoding, and families absorbing the shortfall privately.

The single biggest worry

The most damaging dynamic isn't agency fraud — it's the legal denial of legitimate care by Medicare Advantage plans, which have every incentive to keep capitated costs low. They deny expensive home interventions that fee-for-service Medicare would cover automatically, forcing families to pay privately, do without, or spend weeks appealing — while the same plans are investigated for upcoding to be paid more.

The surprising part

The dark irony: the startup pitch — "we'll deliver more care, cheaper, at home" — became the insurance industry's excuse to deliver less care and pocket the difference. The plans simultaneously deny care and ask Medicare to pay them more for sicker-looking patients, so the humane-sounding shift home doubles as a mechanism for under-serving borderline cases quietly and legally.

In one sentence

Home care trades auditable institutional oversight for unmeasurable fraud, and lets insurers deny legitimate care while billing Medicare for sicker patients than they actually serve.
