Group E · The big picture

Care comes home

When the money rewards keeping people out of the hospital, the hospital came to them.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. Healthcare flipped from a centralized institution — the hospital — to distributed care where people actually live: the home, the phone, the retail clinic. The engine is payment: Medicare Advantage and value-based contracts now pay providers a fixed amount to keep people healthy and out of the hospital, reversing the old incentive where volume meant revenue. An aging population that wants to age in place, and technology that makes home monitoring feasible, filled in behind that financial flip.

The core bet

It's not idealism — it's arbitrage, and it's working. When profit comes from prevention rather than procedures, the expensive hospital ward moves to the living room. But the cost got pushed down to the lowest-wage workers — home aides — and onto families doing the caring for free.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Insurer/PE conglomerates (Optum, CVS) capturing margin across the chain; Medicare Advantage enrollees; wealthy seniors aging in place; scaled agencies; and telehealth/monitoring vendors.

Losers

Home-health aides (wage-stagnant, no benefits); unpaid family caregivers (mostly women); low-income seniors denied higher-acuity care; and traditional hospitals losing admissions.

The single biggest worry

The shift home is real and often cheaper and better — people recover faster at home and avoid hospital infections. But the money saved gets harvested by consolidators, not reinvested in the people doing the work: aides are chronically underpaid, and family caregivers sacrifice careers and health. And when an insurer decides a patient "can't afford" continued home care within its capitated budget, the denial is quiet and legal. The same payment flip that rewarded keeping people out of hospitals also created incentives to under-serve borderline cases and squeeze labor to subsistence — a two-tier system where the rich get seamless home care and the poor face denials dressed up as "appropriate discharge planning."

The surprising part

The hospital didn't disappear — it inverted. Instead of patients traveling to the building, hospital-grade acute care now travels to the home, at 30–40% less cost with equal or better outcomes. But that inversion makes the home-care infrastructure critical and fragile: one IV-pump shortage or one bad aide can tip a patient back to the ER. And insurers quietly want that fragility, because it pushes families to buy supplemental private-duty care outside the insurance system — a shadow market billed straight to the family.

In one sentence

The economics of value-based care moved the hospital into the home, making home care the new profit center — but only for the consolidators, not for the aides and families doing the actual work.
