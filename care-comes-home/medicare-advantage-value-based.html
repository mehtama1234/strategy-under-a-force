Group B · The payment engine

Medicare Advantage

Flip the payment from "do more, earn more" to "keep them healthy, keep the money" — and care follows the cash home.

This is the engine behind the whole shift. Traditional Medicare pays "fee for service" — providers earn more the more they do, which rewards hospital use. Value-based care (and Medicare Advantage, where private insurers get a fixed payment per member) flips it: the provider profits by keeping the patient healthy and out of the expensive hospital, so it invests in cheaper home and preventive care. Medicare Advantage now covers over half of all Medicare enrollees.

The core bet

Under a fixed, per-member ("capitated") payment, the insurer or provider absorbs all cost above that amount — so it profits by not spending on unnecessary hospital visits. That single incentive swap is why care is migrating home.

Prevention or denial?

The same flip that rewards prevention can reward denial disguised as efficiency. If a patient needs an MRI, a specialist, or a hospital stay, but the insurer's incentive is to minimize spending, what stops it from saying no? Under fee-for-service the doctor over-treats; under capitation the insurer under-treats — and the patient sits in the middle, hoping the appeals process moves fast enough. It often doesn't.

Who ends up ahead

Integrated providers and insurers that profit from keeping members well and out of the hospital.

Who's exposed

Patients who need expensive care the plan is incentivized to deny, and taxpayers overpaying for upcoding.

The single biggest worry

The incentive inverts cleanly and dangerously: capitation gives insurers a reason to under-treat and to inflate risk scores at the same time — denying care while billing Medicare more. Investigations have already flagged both, and the guardrails (appeals, external review, regulators) are slow while the patient waits.

The surprising part

The real revolution is that, for the first time, someone profits from doing it right. Under fee-for-service, a doctor who spends an hour preventing your next heart attack loses money and the hospital gains when you're admitted. Under capitation the integrated network gains millions by keeping you out — which is why you suddenly see home visits, free rides to appointments, and "food as medicine." Not charity; the math finally works.

In one sentence

Medicare Advantage flipped the lever from "do more, earn more" to "keep them healthy, keep the money" — and care follows the cash home, for better (prevention pays) and worse (so does denial).
