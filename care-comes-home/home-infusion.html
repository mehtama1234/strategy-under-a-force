Group A · The care that moved home

Home infusion

Expensive biologic drugs must be infused — and doing it at home costs a tenth of a hospital chair.

A booming niche: delivering IV drugs and complex therapies at home instead of a hospital or clinic — antibiotics, chemo, immunoglobulin, and the wave of expensive biologic drugs that must be infused. Companies like Option Care Health and Optum Infusion run it; it's far cheaper and more convenient than a hospital chair, and the explosion of biologic and specialty drugs is fueling the demand.

The core bet

Home infusion moves hospital-grade IV therapy into the living room with visiting nurses and pharmacists, at a fraction of the hospital cost. The bet won on economics; logistics — nurses, sterile supplies, scheduling — became the moat.

The payer keeps the savings

The real winner isn't the home-infusion company — it's the insurer and the pharmacy-benefit manager. Home infusion saves $1,000–2,000 per patient per month versus a hospital, and that delta is captured almost entirely by the payer. Option Care runs ~16–18% margins doing the operational heavy lifting; the insurer, on 2–3% margins across billions, pockets most of the savings.

Who ends up ahead

Insurers and PBMs (who keep the savings) — plus patients getting comfort and nurses getting steady work.

Who's exposed

The home-infusion company (thin margin, all the operational risk), and patients exposed to home IV errors.

The single biggest worry

Medication errors, missed infusions, and line infections happen at home with no ICU nearby — a contaminated PICC line can go unnoticed for 48 hours. The data is murkier than in hospitals (patients don't report errors), liability is fragmented across agency, pharmacy, and manufacturer, and one preventable death rewrites the cost math.

The surprising part

The company doing all the work makes pennies per dollar saved: it's essentially a low-margin logistics provider running the patient experience so the insurer banks the difference. Patients get genuine comfort and nurses get stable jobs — but the economics are inverted, with the operational risk-bearer capturing the least of the value it creates.

In one sentence

Biologic drugs got so expensive and so numerous that the hospital chair became a luxury — so home infusion is now the default channel, and the whole economics of specialty pharmacy depends on it.
