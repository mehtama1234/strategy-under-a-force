Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who does the caring

The whole boom rides on two invisible groups: aides paid like gas-station attendants, and 53 million unpaid family caregivers.

The uncomfortable truth beneath the boom: the direct-care workers who do the physical, intimate work of caring for the elderly and disabled are among the lowest-paid in the economy — mostly women and immigrants, with brutal turnover. And a vast amount of care is done for free by unpaid family caregivers — tens of millions of Americans, whose labor is worth hundreds of billions. "Care comes home" quietly shifts the cost and burden onto these two groups.

The core bet

The home-care boom sits on labor valued at roughly what a gas-station attendant earns — 5 million aides paid near minimum wage with no security — plus the free labor of family members who sacrifice income and health to keep relatives out of institutions.

The math doesn't close

The system is built on a retention fiction — that a stable supply of (mostly immigrant) women will do intimate physical labor for $15/hr with no benefits or career path. But turnover is now so severe that agencies can't fill shifts, fewer new aides enter (nursing school looks better), and the unpaid family caregivers are burning out — losing jobs, savings, and their own health. With fewer adult children per parent and tightening immigration, there's no backup plan.

Who ends up ahead

The system and the consolidators, which save money by paying aides little and families nothing.

Who's exposed

The aides (near-unpaid, high-injury) and the 53 million family caregivers subsidizing the whole industry.

The single biggest worry

The math simply doesn't close: raising aide pay to a retention level would erase margins under Medicaid's caps, but leaving it low means the pipeline empties and the family caregivers propping up the system burn out — and no technology or public subsidy is arriving fast enough to fill the gap.

The surprising part

Do the arithmetic on the unpaid caregivers and it's staggering: 53 million people averaging 24+ hours a week add up to more labor than the entire nursing profession — none of it on any balance sheet. Medicaid long-term care saves the federal budget roughly $400B a year by paying aides $15–17/hr instead of the $40–50/hr a facility nurse costs, and by offloading the rest onto unpaid family. The "home-care boom" isn't innovation — it's a shift in who bears the cost, from employers and government to women and immigrants.

In one sentence

The home-care economy runs on the unpaid labor of 53 million family members and the near-unpaid labor of 5 million aides — mostly women and immigrants who do more care work than the entire nursing profession for a fraction of the pay.
