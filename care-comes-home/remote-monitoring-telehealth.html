Monitoring & telehealth

Telehealth spiked and retreated; the quiet winner is remote monitoring — because the insurer profits from it.

The technology layer that makes home-based care possible: video visits (telehealth), and remote patient monitoring — connected blood-pressure cuffs, glucose monitors, scales, and wearables that send data to clinicians who manage chronic disease from afar. Telehealth exploded during COVID then partly retreated; remote monitoring grows steadily because Medicare pays for it and it fits value-based care.

The core bet

Technology could bypass the office visit — video calls plus connected devices sending real-time vitals so clinicians catch problems early and keep people out of the hospital. Telehealth became a pandemic reflex that faded; remote monitoring is the durable winner because the money aligns.

Continuity fractures

Telehealth fractured the doctor–patient relationship: patients now see a different clinician each visit (Teladoc, Ro, Amazon Clinic rotate providers), so no one owns the full picture. Monitoring data piles up in siloed dashboards — a clinic flags a potassium spike but doesn't know the patient switched statins last week. The continuity doctor who managed your chronic disease over years is gone.

Who ends up ahead

Medicare Advantage insurers who use monitoring to catch crises early — and monitoring-device vendors.

Who's exposed

Independent primary care (squeezed and fragmented) and patients whose care loses continuity.

The single biggest worry

Fragmentation is the risk: real-time data beamed from home is worthless if no one owns the context or the coordination workflow to act on it, and alerts pile up unread. Telehealth was meant to democratize access; instead it atomized the person who actually knows you.

The surprising part

The steadier rise of monitoring isn't about sick people embracing gadgets — it's about insurers building moats. Big Medicare Advantage plans buy or subsidize devices for their sickest members, staff the monitoring with their own nurses (not your doctor), and intervene early to avoid expensive admissions — banking the shared savings. Monitoring booms precisely because the incentive aligns for the insurer, not the doctor.

In one sentence

Telehealth broke the office-visit paywall but fractured continuity; remote monitoring thrives because insurers — not doctors — profit from catching costly crises early, so they build and control the networks.
