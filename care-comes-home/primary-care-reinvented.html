Group C · The wider longevity boom

Primary care reinvented

Paid per senior per year instead of per visit, a new primary care spent time to keep patients well — and the retail giants who piled in mostly retreated.

A wave of companies rebuilt primary care around value-based payment, especially for seniors: Oak Street Health, ChenMed, Cano Health, Agilon, and house-call primary care. They spend more time per patient, coordinate care, and get paid to keep seniors healthy and out of the hospital — the opposite of the rushed 15-minute visit. Big retailers (CVS, Walgreens, Amazon) piled in, then several pulled back.

The core bet

Flip payment from per-visit to per-senior-per-year (capitation): the clinic wins when patients stay healthy and avoid hospital stays. That rewires primary care from a transactional treadmill into a population-health business — if the math holds.

The retail giants retreated

The model assumes prevention and coordination save enough that a fixed per-senior payment leaves margin. But risk selection works against you — sicker seniors gravitate to known programs, costs run higher than modeled — and Walgreens, Cano, and VillageMD all hit that wall, underestimating medical costs and overestimating how much better care would cut them.

Who ends up ahead

Disciplined senior-focused specialists (ChenMed, Agilon) that stayed private and priced risk carefully.

Who's exposed

Retail giants (Walmart, Walgreens) and roll-ups that discovered clinics are a low-margin local grind.

The single biggest worry

The value-based bet assumes patients are sick enough that prevention saves real dollars, hospitals share data, and the population stays stable — and when the mix skews older or poorer than modeled, capitation doesn't cover it, and thin margins turn into fast insolvency.

The surprising part

The winners weren't the flashy startups or retail giants — they were focused operators (ChenMed, Agilon) who didn't try to own the whole stack and added insurance risk only where they could price it. The acquirers (CVS, Amazon, Walgreens) learned that owning clinics is a low-margin, local-execution grind, not a software-like lever — which is why they quietly downscaled while the disciplined specialists endured.

In one sentence

Primary care flipped from a volume dash to a value grind where clinics own a fixed population's health — and most acquirers discovered the math works only at 3–5% margins, not the 15–20% they expected, so the retail giants retreated.
