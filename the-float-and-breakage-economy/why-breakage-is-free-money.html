Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why breakage is free money

A dollar you prepay is worth more to the company than a dollar you spend right now.

This is the mechanism. Two things make prepaid and held money so profitable: float — you hold customers' cash before you owe them anything, an interest-free loan you can invest (worth far more when rates are high); and breakage — the portion never redeemed drops almost entirely to profit, because there's no cost of goods, no labor, nothing. Accounting rules even let firms book expected breakage as revenue.

The core bet

Prepaid money is doubly good — you earn on the cash while you hold it (float), and you keep what's never claimed (breakage) at near-100% margin.

The best outcome is a customer who pays and never returns

Put the two together and a strange conclusion falls out: the ideal transaction is someone who pays $100 and never comes back. The company keeps $100 in cash plus the interest it earned holding it, with zero service cost. A dollar prepaid is worth more than a dollar spent immediately, because the seller gets to hold it, earn on it, and maybe keep all of it.

The single biggest worry

Both engines are fragile: every 1% drop in interest rates strips 1% of the prepayment pool from float income, and escheat and consumer-protection rules can force companies to hand unclaimed money back — erasing breakage entirely.

The surprising part

The insight that unlocks the whole collection is that money has a time value the customer gives away. When you prepay, you surrender the use of your cash and, often, your memory of it — and the business monetizes both: the use (float) and the forgetting (breakage). It's the rare product where the seller quietly hopes you don't collect.

In one sentence

Companies profit twice from prepaid money — once by investing it while they hold it, and again by keeping whatever you never spend.
