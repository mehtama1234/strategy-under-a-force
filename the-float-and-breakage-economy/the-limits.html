Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The limits

It thrives in one narrow window — high rates, loose rules, forgetful customers — and shrinks when any of the three changes.

This is the skeptical piece. Three forces threaten the model: interest rates (float income soars when rates are high and collapses toward zero when they fall); regulation (escheat laws claw back breakage, cash-sweep suits force paying customers, gift-card and auto-renewal rules protect consumers); and digital transparency (apps that remind you of balances, one-tap redemption, and 'cash sorting' shrink both breakage and idle balances).

The core bet

The model depends on high rates, loose rules, and forgetful customers — and all three can turn against it.

High rates create the windfall — and the reason to opt out

The cruel twist is that the very thing making float lucrative also destroys it: high rates give customers a reason to care about their idle cash and move it somewhere it earns. At 0% nobody minds leaving $50 in an app; at 5% they resent it — and fintech tools now make sweeping it out effortless. The incentive that boosted float income also handed customers the exit.

The single biggest worry

A business that relies on customers forgetting, or on interest rates staying high, has no real moat — just timing; lose any one pillar and the profit fades fast.

The surprising part

The deeper point is that transparency is quietly euthanizing breakage. Every reminder, every one-tap redemption, every balance alert removes a bit of the forgetting the model was built on. What looked like a permanent hidden profit turns out to be a temporary arbitrage on friction and inattention — and both are shrinking.

In one sentence

The float-and-breakage economy thrives in a narrow window — high rates, loose rules, and forgetful customers — and shrinks the moment any of the three changes.
