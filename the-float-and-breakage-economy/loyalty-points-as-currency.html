Group B · Points as a currency

Points & miles

An airline's frequent-flyer program is often worth more than its planes — and it can devalue your miles at will.

Loyalty points and airline miles are a private currency companies print. They sell it to banks for real cash upfront (float), they control its value (devaluing points whenever they like), and they keep the points that expire or are never redeemed (breakage). It's a float-and-breakage machine dressed up as a rewards program — and the issuer always wins.

The core bet

A company prints its own points currency, sells it to banks for real cash now, controls its value, and profits from the points that expire or are never redeemed.

What the numbers say

$20–32B
Value of major airline loyalty programs (Delta's SkyMiles ~$31.7B) — often more than the airline itself.
~$8B
What Delta earned from its American Express card deal in 2024 — near half its operating profit, from selling miles.
10–20%+
Historical breakage on points — miles that expire or are never redeemed.
20–50%
How much airlines have raised the miles needed for awards in repeated devaluations since 2015.
deferred revenue
How points sit on the balance sheet — a liability recognized only if and when redeemed.

The issuer controls both the price and the liability

An airline sells seats to its own mileage program: the co-brand bank pays cash now, the airline books the revenue, and later it delivers a cheap seat when you redeem. It wins whether you redeem (a low-cost seat) or don't (pure profit) — and because it can devalue the miles at any time, it controls both the currency's price and its own liability. No other business gets to set both sides.

The single biggest worry

Regulators and consumers are starting to push back on sudden devaluations and expiration, and accounting rules force clearer recognition of the liability — so the freedom to quietly shrink what points are worth is under pressure.

The surprising part

The eye-opener is the balance sheet: for several carriers the frequent-flyer program is worth more than the fleet, the routes, and the operating business combined. The airline that appears to sell flights is, underneath, a company that prints a currency, sells it to banks, and profits most when you never cash it in.

In one sentence

Airlines print a currency they can devalue at will, sell it to banks for billions today, and pocket whatever isn't redeemed — a float machine disguised as a loyalty reward.
