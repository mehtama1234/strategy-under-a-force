Group A · The prepaid float

The Starbucks bank

It holds more customer money than many regional banks — with no banking license and no interest paid.

Customers preload money onto Starbucks cards and the app before buying a single coffee. At any moment the company holds around $1.6–1.8 billion of that cash — an interest-free loan from its own customers — plus 'breakage,' the balances that are never spent. Commentators only half-joke that Starbucks is a bank that happens to sell coffee.

The core bet

Get customers to prepay, and you hold a giant pile of their money for free — an interest-free loan from your own customers — plus you keep whatever they never spend.

A deposit machine that sells coffee

Starbucks runs a multi-billion-dollar deposit business with no banking license, no interest paid to customers, and almost no banking regulation. It holds more customer money than plenty of actual banks — and gets to use it, earn on it, and keep whatever's forgotten. The coffee is almost a side effect of the float.

The single biggest worry

The model needs customers to keep money loaded and keep coming back — and some states' unclaimed-property (escheat) laws force companies to hand abandoned balances to the state after a few years, money Starbucks assumed it could keep.

The surprising part

The startling part is regulatory: a coffee chain quietly operates one of the larger consumer-deposit pools in the country while sidestepping everything that makes a bank a bank — reserves, interest, oversight. Prepayment turned its customers into unpaid depositors, and the balance sheet shows it.

In one sentence

Starbucks got customers to prepay billions for coffee they haven't bought yet, handing it free money to invest and keep whatever never gets spent.
