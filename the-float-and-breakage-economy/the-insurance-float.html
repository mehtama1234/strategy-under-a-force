Group C · Float at scale

The insurance float

Buffett's real business isn't insurance — it's investing $177 billion of other people's money, often getting paid to hold it.

Insurers collect premiums now and pay claims years later, holding the money in between — 'float.' Warren Buffett built Berkshire Hathaway on investing this float, calling it the engine of the whole enterprise. It's the largest and most famous float business in the world: a giant pool of other people's money to invest, and if you underwrite well, better than free.

The core bet

Collect premiums upfront and pay claims years later, and in between invest a giant pool of other people's money — if you underwrite well, it's better than free money.

Insurance is an investment business in a protection costume

Buffett's insight was that insurance isn't really about protection — it's an investment business wearing a protection costume. He got to manage $177 billion of capital that wasn't his, earn returns on it, and frequently get paid (via underwriting profit) to hold it. Most insurers barely break even on underwriting; Berkshire turned underwriting into the pump that fills the investment reservoir.

The single biggest worry

A single catastrophe — a hurricane, an earthquake, a pandemic — can flip the math overnight: claims spike, the float shrinks, and the insurer must sell assets in a down market. And if underwriting discipline slips to win market share, the float stops paying and starts costing.

The surprising part

The scale is the surprise. Float let one investor deploy $177 billion of other people's money for decades — the single biggest reason Berkshire compounded the way it did. The premiums people paid for peace of mind became the raw material of one of history's great fortunes, held for free and often at a profit.

In one sentence

Insurers collect premiums now and pay claims years later, and if they underwrite well, they get paid to hold billions of other people's money to invest.
