Group B · Platforms clone their sellers

Amazon Basics

The platform watches what sells, then launches its own cheaper copy.

Amazon Basics is where private label meets the marketplace. Amazon can see exactly which third-party products sell well on its own platform, then launch a cheaper Amazon-branded version and give it prime placement. It's both the marketplace and a competitor on it — which is why regulators call it an unfair conflict of interest.

The core bet

The bet is that Amazon can out-compete the very sellers who depend on it — because it holds two advantages no seller has: their sales data, and control of the search ranking. Spot a winner, clone it, rank your version first.

What the numbers say

2009
Amazon Basics launched (cables, batteries). By 2020 it was an estimated $1B+ business across hundreds of categories.
2023
The US FTC formally sued Amazon, alleging it uses nonpublic seller data to launch competing products and then ranks its own higher.
€746M
EU antitrust fine (2021) tied to Amazon's use of seller data — the bloc found it broke competition rules by using that data unfairly.
leaked docs
In 2021–22, internal documents suggested Amazon used sales data and rankings to decide which products to clone via Basics.

Marketplace and rival at once

It's as if eBay could see every seller's exact profit margin, then launched eBay-branded versions and put them at the top of every search. The platform sets the rules, sees all the data, and competes in the same aisle. Regulators argue that's inherently unfair — not just because Amazon does it, but because the platform structure lets it.

What's newly valuable
Seller data, control of the search page, and a private label that can be ranked first and priced under the products it copied.
What's at risk
The third-party seller who built the winner — now undercut and out-ranked with no fair way to fight back.
The single biggest worry

If Amazon can see what sells, clone it cheaper, and rank its own first, small sellers have no fair shot — they can only accept thinner profits or leave. And the growing antitrust cases could force Amazon to change how it uses that data.

The surprising part

Amazon doesn't hide it — it frames Basics as answering "customer demand." What's striking is the structure: the same company that runs the marketplace, sees every sale, and sets the search order also sells its own competing products. The regulators' claim isn't that Amazon is uniquely greedy — it's that the platform itself makes the conflict unavoidable.

In one sentence

Amazon Basics is the ultimate conflict of interest: a platform that watches its own sellers, clones the winners cheaper, and ranks its version first.
