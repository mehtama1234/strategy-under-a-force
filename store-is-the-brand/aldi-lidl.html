Group A · The store brands that won

Aldi & Lidl

The German discounters that built the whole model on 90% own-brand.

Aldi and Lidl built their entire business on private label — about 90% of what they sell is their own brand. By carrying maybe 1,400–2,000 items instead of a normal supermarket's 30,000+, they cut complexity, negotiate harder, and price everything rock-bottom. That radical simplicity conquered Europe and is now reshaping US grocery as both expand fast.

The core bet

The bet is that shoppers will trade choice for price. Strip the assortment to a tight, mostly own-brand list, run the leanest operation in retail, and undercut everyone. The store is the brand, and the brand is one promise: cheap and decent.

What the numbers say

~90%
Share of sales from own-brand at Aldi (Lidl is similar). National brands are the exception, not the rule.
1,400–2,000
Items Aldi carries — versus 30,000–40,000 at a traditional supermarket. Fewer choices means lower cost, faster restocking, and less labor.
~3,200
US stores Aldi is targeting by 2028, backed by a $9B investment; it opened ~180 in 2026 alone. Lidl entered the US in 2017 and now has 185+.
10–20%
Price cuts and assortment trims that Walmart, Target, and Kroger made in 2025 to keep up — a direct response to the discounters.

Simplicity is the weapon

The magic isn't fancy tech — it's ruthless simplicity. Cutting the item count slashes logistics and labor, speeds checkout, and stacks private-label margin on top. That efficiency lets them price below what traditional grocers can match without wrecking their own margins. US chains tried this model for decades and failed; Aldi proved it works at scale here.

What's newly valuable
Extreme efficiency, own-brand margin, and a simple, trusted promise that pulls in even higher-income price-hunters.
What it destroys
National brands' shelf space and bargaining power — replaced, not supplemented.
The single biggest worry

For food manufacturers, Aldi and Lidl are an existential threat, not a customer. Their private labels replace national brands rather than carry them — and if they reach 5,000+ US stores, they can commoditize whole categories.

The surprising part

The thing that terrifies competitors is how basic it is: one logo, a tiny selection, one promise. By forcing product decisions before you even enter the store, Aldi cuts on-shelf complexity, speeds checkout, and lowers labor — and that operational edge compounds with private-label margin into prices rivals simply can't match.

In one sentence

Aldi and Lidl's 90%-own-brand model is so simple it forces every chain from Kroger to Target to choose: cut assortment, cut margins, or fade.
