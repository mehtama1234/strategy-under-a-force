Group C · Categories falling

Store-brand food

From "cheap knock-off" to the default choice on the shelf.

Private-label food has steadily taken share from the big packaged-food brands — Kraft Heinz, General Mills, Campbell, Conagra. It hit record share of US grocery, especially after the 2021 inflation surge. The quality gap shrank so much that switching became rational, not shameful. Now the giants are stuck: raise prices and lose volume, or hold prices and lose margin.

The core bet

Store brands bet that shoppers would switch once quality was "good enough" and the price gap was real. They were right. Private label crossed from discount fallback to default, and the big food companies face a squeeze with no comfortable way out.

What the numbers say

~25–28%
Private label's share of US grocery dollars (2023–24) — an all-time high after inflation pushed shoppers to switch.
5–15%
The price gap versus national brands today — down from 25–30% a decade ago, as store brands improved recipes and sourcing and national brands hiked prices.
lost volume
Kraft Heinz, General Mills, and Campbell all lost volume (2022–24), hit hardest in staples — pasta, canned goods, cereal, condiments.
25–35%
Retailer gross margin on store brands vs 20–25% on national brands — the whole supply chain is incentivized to push private label.

The trap for big food

The packaged-food giants are caught between margin death and volume death. They can't cut prices enough to match private label without gutting profit per unit — yet every price hold pushes more shoppers to the store version, which is now genuinely credible. The margin compression looks permanent, not cyclical.

What survives
Fresh, organic, and premium tiers, and categories where a specific brand's taste or identity still matters.
What's falling
Shelf-stable staples — canned, frozen, dry goods — where the store version is now indistinguishable in a blind taste test.
The single biggest worry

Big food is stuck between two losses: lower prices and crater profit, or hold prices and keep bleeding volume to credible store brands. Structural margin compression, not a passing dip.

The surprising part

Ten years ago "private label" meant cheap knock-off. Today it's indistinguishable in blind tests for pasta, canned beans, cereal, and peanut butter — retailers invested heavily in quality and hired food scientists away from big brands. The quality floor rose so far that switching became rational rather than shameful.

In one sentence

Private label went from discount option to default choice by closing the quality gap while keeping a 10–20% price edge — leaving national food makers with no pricing room and shrinking volume.
