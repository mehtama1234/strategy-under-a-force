Group D · The forces & the fight

The brand fights back

The brands that survive sell identity and trust, not just function.

National brands aren't dead — they're defending. The winners move fast (innovate before the store can copy), move direct (sell straight to you to escape the retailer's shelf), and move to categories where trust and identity matter more than price — baby formula, medicine, premium pet food, iconic drinks. You'll buy store-brand bleach; you won't buy store-brand Coke or store-brand baby formula.

The core bet

The bet is that speed, innovation, and identity beat cost. Launch products store brands can't copy fast enough; build a direct relationship to dodge the shelf war; and own the categories where people buy on trust or fear, not price. Function is easy to dupe. Identity isn't.

What the numbers say

~3-to-1
Store brands grew ~3.3% in 2025 vs ~1.2% for national brands — private label is outpacing them nearly three to one. The pressure is real.
~78.7%
National brands still hold most of the dollar share overall — but they're bleeding unit volume fastest where function is easy to match.
DTC
Some brands escape the shelf entirely: Nike pulled back from Amazon (2019–2025) to build direct-to-consumer, owning the customer and the data retailers can't touch.
trust
Baby formula (Similac), iconic drinks (Coca-Cola), and premium pet food resist duping — people won't gamble on the cheap version for a baby or a beloved identity.

Where brands still win

The surviving playbook has three moves: innovate faster than the copy cycle; go direct to own the relationship and the data; and anchor in identity — packaging, story, and trust that a store label can't cheaply match. Function is duped in a season. "Who I am" and "what I trust with my kid" are not.

Defensible
Identity and fear categories — baby formula, medicine you trust, iconic taste — plus fast innovation and direct-to-consumer control.
Indefensible
Mid-tier, generic-feeling products whose only claim was a familiar name and a TV ad.
The single biggest worry

This is a structural shift, not a cycle. Younger shoppers see store brands as credible equals, not budget fallbacks — so brands can't just wait out inflation. They're fighting a durably changed perception of quality and value.

The surprising part

The brands that survive are selling identity and fear, not function. You'll buy store-brand bleach but never store-brand Coke or Similac, because some products are wrapped up in who you are or what you'll risk for your child. The brands winning hardest are the ones that escaped the retailer's shelf entirely — owning the customer, the story, and the data.

In one sentence

National brands defend by moving fast, moving direct, and moving to the identity-and-trust categories where a cheaper copy simply won't do.
