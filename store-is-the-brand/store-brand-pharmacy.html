Store-brand pharmacy

The clearest case: chemically identical to the name brand, 30–70% less.

Over-the-counter medicine is where the store brand is literally identical to the name brand. The CVS or Kirkland ibuprofen, the store-brand allergy pill — by law they contain the exact same active ingredient at the same dose. The "Compare to Advil" label says it out loud. The national brand's premium is pure marketing, and shoppers are catching on.

The core bet

The bet is honesty as a selling point. When the FDA requires the store brand to be chemically equivalent, the retailer just has to tell you so — "same active ingredient, less money" — and let the price gap do the rest. There's no quality argument left for the brand to make.

What the numbers say

1984
The Hatch-Waxman Act created the modern generic pathway — the FDA requires the same active ingredient, dose, form, and strength (bioequivalence).
30–70%
Price gap on OTC staples. CVS ibuprofen ~$3–4 for 100 tablets; Advil $8–12 for the same milligrams of the same molecule.
~90%
Share of US prescriptions now filled as generics (2023) — yet OTC awareness lags, so many shoppers still overpay for name-brand pain and allergy pills.
40–50%
Private label's share of OTC pain/cold/allergy volume at US pharmacy chains — driven by the "Compare to" label spelling out the equivalence.

The premium is the marketing

Ibuprofen is ibuprofen; acetaminophen is acetaminophen. Often the name-brand pill and the store-brand pill come off the same line in the same factory. The brand bought decades of TV ads to build trust; the store bought shelf space and quietly undercut it once the molecule went fully generic.

What's newly valuable
The "Compare to" honesty pitch, and the fact that regulation guarantees the store version works exactly as well.
The confusion risk
Shoppers sometimes lump strictly regulated OTC generics together with loosely regulated supplements — and distrust both.
The single biggest worry

The main barrier isn't quality — it's confusion. Store-brand OTC drugs (identical, safe, cheap) get mentally grouped with unregulated supplements and homeopathic remedies (inconsistent, not equivalent), so some shoppers mistrust the whole store-brand shelf unfairly.

The surprising part

Many name-brand OTC pills are made by the same company, in the same facility, as the store brand — you're buying the identical tablet in a different box. The premium bought TV ads and decades of trust; the store brand bought shelf space and silence, then undercut on price the moment the molecule was generic.

In one sentence

Store-brand medicine is legally required to be chemically identical to the name brand but costs 30–70% less — the premium is marketing, and most shoppers still don't know it.
