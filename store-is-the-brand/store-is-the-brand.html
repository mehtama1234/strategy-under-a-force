Group E · The big picture

The store became the brand

The shelf and the data used to be commodities. Now they're the asset.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains all of them. For a century, national brands like Coca-Cola and P&G owned customer loyalty, and the retailer was just a shelf. Now retailers control two things brands don't — the shelf (what gets space, what gets buried) and the shopper data (what you actually buy, at what price) — and they use both to launch their own copy of every top seller and take the sale.

The one idea

The store became the brand. Be fair, though — it overstates the universal case. This doesn't kill all brands: identity and trust products (luxury, baby formula, iconic taste, cult communities) survive by earning their margin. But for ordinary, function-based goods, the trust moved from the brand to the retailer.

The whole force in numbers

~19% → 28%+
Private label's share of US grocery, stable through 2020 then surging after 2021 inflation — with some categories (cereal, pasta, canned) above 40%.
$15–18B
Walmart's Great Value alone — rivaling Pepsi and Coke in household reach. Costco's Kirkland is similar scale.
35–45%
Rate at which Gen Z and Millennials buy private label, vs ~28% for Gen X — citing "same product" and price, not shame.
200–400 bps
Gross-margin compression at Kraft Heinz, Mondelēz, and General Mills (2021–24) as shelf prices flatlined but costs rose — forcing shrinkflation.
zip-code
The structural moat: Kroger and Walmart know what sells next week in each neighborhood, and stock private-label variants before brands can react.
30–70%
The savings on identical store-brand medicine — the clearest proof the brand premium can be pure marketing.

Winners vs losers

Winners
Walmart, Costco, Kroger, Aldi/Lidl, Amazon (own shelf + data); premium and identity brands (luxury, cult, DTC); makers who supply private label at scale.
Losers
Mid-tier packaged goods (Kraft, Mondelēz, General Mills) — too big to be agile, too generic to command a premium; brands whose only moat was a familiar name.
The biggest tension

Private label works because it's often the same product — same factory, same formula, new box. The brand did the R&D and built the trust; the retailer harvests both at half price. Brands can't sue (it's legal), can't advertise away the price gap (math wins), and can't leave (they'd lose the shelf to a rival who plays along). Some retailers even build the private-label volume, then downrank or delist the national brand.

The surprising part

The generation most loyal to private label never had brand loyalty as a concept — so they feel no loss. And trust didn't vanish; it consolidated. Costco and Walmart are now more trusted than individual brands in categories like toilet paper, cereal, and vitamins. The customer doesn't switch brands anymore. They switch stores.

In one sentence

The shelf and the data used to be commodities and the brand was the asset — now that's flipped: the shelf and the data are the asset, and the brand is the commodity.

How this connects to the rest of the series

This is the retail face of a bigger machine. The shopper data that powers private label is the same data behind the sibling retail-media boom — one dataset, two weapons. Controlling the shelf to tax or displace what passes through echoes the toll-booth economy. And the collapse of brand loyalty rhymes with the value shifts across Last Call and the pet economy, where trust and identity — not the old name — decide who wins.
