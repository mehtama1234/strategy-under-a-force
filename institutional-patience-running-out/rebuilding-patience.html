Group D · The mechanism & who keeps patience

Can patience be rebuilt?

We invented long-term exchanges, dual-class shares, and evergreen funds — and mostly moved the appearance of patience.

The attempts to re-lengthen time horizons: the Long-Term Stock Exchange (launched 2020); dual-class shares that let founders ignore quarterly pressure; companies dropping quarterly earnings guidance (Dimon and Buffett's 2018 call); "evergreen" permanent-capital funds with no fixed exit; and government long-horizon programs (DARPA). The tools exist — the question is whether they actually restore patience.

The core bet

Investors, founders, and policymakers are building structures to escape quarterly capitalism, betting that removing the 90-day pressure will let companies make harder, slower, riskier bets. The unstated wager: patience itself is a competitive advantage, and modern finance broke it.

What the numbers say

2020
the Long-Term Stock Exchange launched — designed to reward long-term holders, but adoption stayed in single digits.
2004
Google's IPO normalized dual-class shares; Meta, Snap, and most big tech IPOs copied it to ignore quarterly pressure.
2018
Dimon and Buffett called to end quarterly guidance; JPMorgan, Microsoft, and others quietly dropped it — culture, not law.
evergreen
permanent-capital funds with no forced exit spread — patience became a product marketed to investors.
2014
France's Florange law gave extra votes to shareholders holding 2+ years — "loyalty shares" embedding time into power.

Swimming upstream

The honest verdict is that most of these tools moved the appearance of patience, not the reality. The Long-Term Stock Exchange has almost no volume; companies that dropped guidance mostly just became less transparent; and evergreen funds still chase "10× or bust" by year five, just without saying so aloud. The structures that actually deliver — DARPA, monopoly balance sheets, family offices — had little to do with clever financial design.

Who ends up ahead

The rare structures that truly work — DARPA, mega-firm balance sheets, family offices — and the founders who use them well.

Who gets starved

Everyone who assumed a governance tweak could restore patience against the pull of the whole market.

The single biggest worry

The tools don't work on players who never wanted patience: a founder with dual-class votes can still chase viral growth. The structures that do work serve a tiny sliver of the economy and often fail silently — so the underlying problem, that fast markets make patience economically irrational, persists unchanged.

The surprising part

The surprising twist is that patient structures sometimes make things worse: dual-class founders often used their freedom not to fund long R&D but to launch rival products faster and fight harder — patience as a tool to ignore criticism, not to embrace long bets. The mechanism that truly restored patience wasn't a shareholder structure at all — it was monopoly (firms so profitable they can self-fund moonshots) and the state (accepting losses no private investor would).

In one sentence

We invented many tools to rebuild patience — long-term exchanges, dual-class shares, evergreen funds — but they mostly moved the appearance of it; the few things that actually delivered had nothing to do with financial design.
