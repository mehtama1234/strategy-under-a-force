Group A · Where patience died

Quarterly capitalism

Run a company to hit the 90-day number, and you'll cut the research and buy back the stock instead of building.

Public companies are run to hit quarterly earnings targets — issuing guidance, managing to the number, and pouring cash into stock buybacks and dividends rather than long-term investment. Surveys find most managers would cut R&D or good projects to make a quarterly figure. Buybacks hit record highs. This is the front line of vanishing corporate patience.

The core bet

When hitting the 90-day target becomes the goal — not customers, not durability, not invention — managers cut R&D, defer maintenance, and buy back stock to prop up earnings per share. Short-term optics beat long-term health.

What the numbers say

Buybacks over building

The trap is self-reinforcing: investors expect quarterly beats, so managers deliver them by cutting corners — deferring maintenance, slashing long-term projects, buying back stock to inflate EPS. It works for a few years while the competitive moat quietly erodes; by the time the stock cracks, the CEO has cashed out. Whole industries end up underinvesting in what they'll need a decade out.

Who ends up ahead

Executives (paid in stock) and short-term shareholders who get the buyback-fueled bump.

Who gets starved

The company's own future — R&D, maintenance, and the capabilities needed to compete years later.

The single biggest worry

A company can look perfectly healthy on earnings while being hollowed out. Buybacks and layoffs show up instantly in EPS and make boards look responsive; the lost breakthroughs and eroded market share don't appear for years — so financial engineering steadily crowds out actual engineering.

The surprising part

Many buybacks are actually bad investments: companies tend to buy their own stock near peaks and stop during downturns — the opposite of buy-low. And CEO pay is tied to the very EPS and stock price the buybacks inflate, so executives enrich themselves at the moment they're supposed to be stewards. Even Buffett, who praises cheap buybacks, hates the careless kind Wall Street treats as automatically virtuous.

In one sentence

Quarterly targets tied to CEO bonuses turned the public company into a machine for harvesting short-term stock gains instead of building the long-term advantages that made it worth owning.
