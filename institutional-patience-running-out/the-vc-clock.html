Group C · The impatient replacements

The VC fund clock

A venture fund must return cash in about ten years — so it funds apps that flip fast, not science that takes fifteen.

Venture funds have a fixed life (~10 years) and must return money to their own investors on a schedule, which pushes them toward companies that can grow fast and exit — IPO or acquisition — within that window. That favors asset-light software over slow, capital-heavy hard tech, and pressures startups to "grow at all costs" then sell or die. The clock shapes what gets invented: fast-scaling apps over patient science.

The core bet

The 10-year fund life forces VCs to chase companies that can exit in 5-7 years. Software reaches revenue in a year and exits in five; deep tech needs 5-7 years just to prove the science works. So the fund clock rewards speed over everything — and hard tech withers.

Apps over science

The fund clock creates a cruel mismatch: the companies that most need capital (hard tech, biotech, climate) take 10-15 years to prove value, while the main source of scale capital needs its money back in 5-7. A fund holding a battery startup in year 7 stops investing and pushes for a sale — to anyone, at any price — often to a strategic buyer who milks the IP rather than builds the market. That kills moonshots.

Who ends up ahead

Fast-scaling software startups and the VCs who can exit them inside the fund window.

Who gets starved

Hard-tech, biotech, and climate startups that need a decade — and the breakthroughs that never get funded.

The single biggest worry

When a fund runs out of patience (and capital) during a startup's long R&D slog, the company can't raise from traditional VCs — they need exits within their remaining fund life. Family offices and strategics step in, but they're smaller and fewer, so hard tech that misses the narrow VC window can simply stall out and die.

The surprising part

The twist: successful deep-tech companies often exit faster than SaaS ones — a working fusion reactor or fab attracts a strategic buyer within 2-3 years of proving feasibility. The problem is that VC funds run out of patience during the 7-10 year slog before that moment. The clock doesn't reward the payoff; it punishes the journey.

In one sentence

The 10-year VC fund clock biases innovation toward software and away from anything needing patient R&D — because investors demand cash back by year 8, not by year 12.
