Group C · The impatient replacements

Private equity's short holds

Sold as "patient capital," the standard PE model buys, loads with debt, cuts, and flips in a few years.

Private equity is often sold as "patient capital," but the standard model buys a company, loads it with debt, cuts costs, and flips it in about 3-6 years to hit the fund's return targets — a short leash that can mean deferred maintenance, sold-off assets, and underinvestment. Now, with exits harder (2022-24), PE holds longer than it wants and invents ways to return cash (continuation funds, dividend recaps).

The core bet

PE's fund economics demand quick exits: managers must return cash within a ~10-year fund life, typically flipping companies in 3-7 years. When exits froze, PE's real impatience became visible — aging portfolios, stalled reinvestment, and improvised cash returns squeezing the companies caught in the wait.

"Patient capital" isn't

The backlog of unsold companies is a structural trap: funds can't exit without booking losses that would hurt the next fundraise, so they park companies longer, defer maintenance to protect current cash flow, and pile on more debt via dividend recaps to satisfy restless investors. The companies get hollowed out — infrastructure deferred, talent leaving during the long hold, competitive position eroding.

Who ends up ahead

PE managers (via fees and financial engineering) — and, in good times, their investors.

Who gets starved

The portfolio companies squeezed for cash flow, and their workers, maintenance, and long-term investment.

The single biggest worry

A single recession or rate shock could force a fire-sale of tens of thousands of over-leveraged companies at once, cratering valuations and bankrupting the most indebted. To avoid it, PE mortgages the companies it can't sell (dividend recaps, NAV loans, continuation funds) — all of which return cash without proving the underlying business got better.

The surprising part

The "patient capital" pitch was never really about patience — it was about financial engineering. PE's outsized returns in the 2000s-2010s came mostly from 3-4 year holds at peak valuations, amplified by borrowed money and timing, not operational brilliance. When exits stall and the funds scramble for recaps and continuation vehicles, it reveals that the alpha often came from leverage and timing, not from making companies better.

In one sentence

PE's 3-7 year exit clock was always the real constraint — so when exits froze, sponsors mortgaged the companies they couldn't sell to pay back the ones that wouldn't move, leaving thousands of aging, over-leveraged businesses in limbo.
