Group E · The big picture

Institutional patience running out

The market optimized itself for speed and liquidity — and starved everything valuable that needs to wait.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. The patient, long-horizon capital and institutions that dominated the 20th century have drained away. Quarterly capitalism, activist investors, shrinking public markets, private-equity flips, the VC fund clock, and microsecond trading all shortened the time horizon — so the things that only pay off over decades (basic research, infrastructure, biotech, forests, careful institutions) get starved of the patient money they need.

The core bet

Patience itself became the scarce resource. That hands a rare structural edge to the few who can still wait — sovereign funds, family offices, foundations, a Buffett, and China's state capital — while public companies destroy themselves with buybacks and long-cycle breakthroughs go unfunded.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Sovereign wealth funds and state-backed infrastructure (Norway, the Gulf, China, Singapore); family offices and multigenerational wealth; foundations and endowments; and the private companies (SpaceX, Stripe) that can stay private and invest long.

Losers

Basic research, infrastructure, and slow-cycle industries; public companies stuck on the quarterly treadmill; the scientists who left when the corporate labs closed; and a research culture that once gave us the transistor and has no equivalent today.

The single biggest worry

Markets reward speed and liquidity: an investor can sell a stock or exit a fund fast, while a decade-long infrastructure or science bet offers no quick exit. A CEO who lifts EPS with buybacks gets paid handsomely; one who spends $500M on research and loses billions on basic science gets fired for "destroying shareholder value." Yet almost everything truly valuable — the internet, antibiotics, air travel, the highway system, AI, clean energy — required exactly the patient capital that has now vanished from the private sector. Short-term capital efficiency sets the stock price while long-term value creation starves, because no one can afford to wait. The market is optimizing itself to death.

The surprising part

The winners aren't lean, nimble startups — they're large, patient, "boring" institutions that hoard capital for decades: sovereign funds, family offices, foundations, and states. Buffett's patience is now a moat; China's willingness to run infrastructure deficits is a structural advantage; foundations set up by dead billionaires out-pick the "efficient" private sector because they have no exit deadline. It reverses the startup myth — the tortoise (old money, state capital) is beating the hare (quarterly capitalism, the fund clocks, the traders). And the edge can't be arbitraged away, because patience can't be bought or taught — it's institutional DNA.

In one sentence

Patient capital — once a commodity every large corporation had — has become scarce enough that whoever can still wait wins, while public companies destroy themselves with buybacks and the breakthroughs that need decades go unfunded.
