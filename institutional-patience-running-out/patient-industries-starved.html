Group B · What's being starved

Patient industries starved

A drug takes 10-15 years, fusion needs decades, a forest 30-80 — so impatient money flees to software.

Some things simply take a long time: a new drug takes 10-15 years and $1-2B+; deep tech (fusion, new materials, quantum) needs a decade-plus; a forest takes 30-80 years to grow; farmland and orchards mature slowly. Impatient capital shuns those timelines and gravitates to software, where returns come fast — so hard, slow, physical innovation gets underfunded. "We were promised flying cars; we got 140 characters."

The core bet

Impatient money abandons industries where success takes decades and gorges on software and services. Hard, physical, slow things starve — widening a gap where we're very fast at digital mirrors and glacially slow at the physical world that feeds, heals, and powers us.

Flying cars vs 140 characters

Impatient capital gravitates to whatever returns fastest, so the slow, physical, hard innovations that actually change material life get starved — and the risk isn't hypothetical. The collapse of antibiotics R&D means routine infections are becoming untreatable again; no company can capture enough value to justify a 15-year, $500M cycle, so the market simply fails, and the talent and know-how evaporate.

Who ends up ahead

Software and fast-exit sectors — and governments and foundations that absorb the long-cycle risk.

Who gets starved

Drugs, deep tech, forestry, and soil — anything whose payoff is decades out, plus public health downstream.

The single biggest worry

The erosion of antibiotics and slow-cycle R&D is a ticking public-health catastrophe: infections that antibiotics made survivable are becoming untreatable, in a world of surgery and implants that depend on them. The market fails not because the science is hard but because the return arrives too slowly for anyone impatient to fund it.

The surprising part

Patient money isn't gone — it moved upstream to government and philanthropy. The Gates Foundation, CEPI, and ARPA-style programs are now the only funders reliable enough for long-cycle innovation, absorbing the risk private markets fled. So the physical-world innovation we still do is more bureaucratic and slower to pivot — we traded VC's impatience for government's caution.

In one sentence

Patient capital vanished into software, leaving hard physical innovation to governments and foundations — so we got very fast at the digital and very slow at everything else that matters.
