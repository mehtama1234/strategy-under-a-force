Group D · The mechanism & who keeps patience

Why capital got impatient

Tie everyone's survival — bonuses, tenure, fund flows — to quarterly metrics, and no one can afford to wait.

The deeper causes, in plain terms: the rise of shareholder-value ideology (the Friedman doctrine) that made stock price the goal; executive pay tied to short-term stock; money managers judged on quarterly performance so they can't wait; higher, again-volatile interest rates that discount the distant future heavily; always-on liquid markets that make selling easy; and metrics and algorithms that reward measurable near-term results.

The core bet

The system bet that stock price is the only success metric worth pursuing. Once that's the goal — not customers, not durability, not invention — every actor games it, squeezing shorter and shorter timescales because that's what moves the stock today.

Rational alone, insane together

The architecture became a game where everyone rates everyone else's ratings of value, and nobody actually cares whether the underlying business creates durable worth — they care whether the stock rises tomorrow, because everyone else sells when it drops. Tie compensation to stock price and you've created an incentive to manage the price, not build the company; once a few succeed at it, the pressure cascades onto everyone.

Who ends up ahead

Executives, fund managers, and traders whose pay and survival track near-term metrics.

Who gets starved

The long-term health of companies, and the patient projects nobody is rewarded for defending.

The single biggest worry

The trap is that it's rational for each actor and insane collectively. A CEO who invests for year 10 while missing quarters gets fired; a fund manager who picks durable moats with lumpy results underperforms peers and loses assets. Neither is being greedy in a vacuum — they're responding to the system around them, which made short-termism the only sane move.

The surprising part

The surprising part is how little malice is required. Every actor is responding rationally to incentives — stock options, fund bonuses, job tenure, discount rates — that all point at the next quarter. The short-termism isn't a moral failing so much as an emergent property of a system that made waiting personally ruinous for everyone in it.

In one sentence

Capital got impatient because we tied everyone's personal survival to quarterly metrics instead of years-long results — and once the race started, no one could stop running without being trampled.
