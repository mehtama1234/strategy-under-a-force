Institutional Patience Running Out

How the money that could wait got rare — so anything that only pays off in twenty years struggles to get built.

The 34th force. For most of the 20th century, big institutions — corporate research labs, pension funds, public companies, universities — could think in decades. Now the clock runs fast: CEOs are judged quarterly, funds must return cash on a schedule, and traders hold stocks for microseconds. Patience itself became the scarce resource. The one idea: the money that could wait got rare — so anything that only pays off in twenty years struggles to get funded.

The one question, asked of everyone

What did the draining of institutional patience make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? Fast, measurable, near-term returns became cheap and abundant; long-horizon things — basic science, infrastructure, slow industries — got starved. And the ability to wait became a genuine edge, handed to the few who still have it: sovereign funds, family offices, foundations, a Buffett, and China's state capital.
