Group A · Where patience died

The shrinking public market

The number of US public companies roughly halved — founders now see going public as a burden, not a prize.

The US stock market is shrinking — not in value, but in the number of companies. Public-company counts roughly halved from their 1990s peak; firms stay private far longer (or never IPO), and many get taken private by private equity. Going public means quarterly scrutiny and activists, so founders avoid it — which shrinks where ordinary people can invest and concentrates ownership in private hands.

The core bet

Fewer firms go public, more get taken private, and tech giants stay private for a decade or more before (or instead of) listing. The public market — meant to democratize ownership — is becoming a gated club, and the fastest-growing companies increasingly sit outside it.

A gated club

When the public market shrinks, ordinary investors lose access. The median retirement account holds public stocks; if half the choice set disappears into private hands, that saver can't own the growing companies — they stay owned by PE funds and early employees. Wealth concentrates: gains that once flowed to 401(k) holders now flow to founders and PE partners, and the stock market becomes less a democracy of ownership than a members' room.

Who ends up ahead

Founders and PE partners who capture private-market gains, and firms that avoid quarterly scrutiny.

Who gets starved

Ordinary investors, whose choice set halves, and the broad public whose ownership stake narrows.

The single biggest worry

The public market was the great democratizer of ownership; as it shrinks, the highest-growth part of the economy moves behind a wall only the wealthy and connected can reach. And private PE owners optimize for cash flow and EBITDA multiples, not moonshot R&D — so even innovation can slow.

The surprising part

The surprise is that founders don't want to go public. The moment you list, activists buy in, you face endless earnings calls, your stock gets slammed for a penny miss, and your decade-long R&D bets get second-guessed every 90 days. Private markets now provide liquidity and huge valuations without the SEC weight — so for Stripe or Databricks, staying private isn't fear of scrutiny, it's a rational way to keep investing long-term.

In one sentence

As founders and PE firms exploit looser private-market rules, ordinary investors watch their choices halve while ownership of the fastest-growing companies concentrates among the already-rich.
