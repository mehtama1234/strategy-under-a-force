Group D · The mechanism & who keeps patience

Who still plays long

In a world addicted to speed, the ability to ignore the next quarter for the next forty years became the rarest edge.

In a short-term world, the ability to wait becomes a rare edge. A handful of players still think in decades: sovereign wealth funds (Norway, Singapore, the Gulf) that invest for generations; family offices and family-controlled businesses that think in dynasties; large foundations and university endowments; Warren Buffett's "our favorite holding period is forever"; and China's state capital building for decades. Patience is now a moat because so few have it.

The core bet

A handful of institutions built moats by refusing to chase quarterly returns. Their patience — holding for 30, 50, or 100+ years while others panic — lets them buy when everyone sells, hold illiquid assets others avoid, and reap wealth from truths that take decades to play out.

Patience is now a moat

Because so few can wait, the ability to becomes a genuine competitive advantage — you can buy when others are forced to sell, hold assets others can't stomach the illiquidity of, and let compounding do the work across decades. Foundations set up by long-dead billionaires (Carnegie, Ford, Gates) have beaten the "efficient" private sector at picking research bets precisely because they have no exit deadline.

Who ends up ahead

Sovereign funds, family offices, foundations, and endowments that can compound for decades.

Who gets starved

Everyone forced to move at the market's tempo — the many who can't afford to wait.

The single biggest worry

Even these long-horizon players are shrinking relative to the flood of short-term capital: Norway's ~$2T is only ~1.5% of global equity, and that share falls each year. China's 50-year infrastructure bets are hostage to geopolitics; family offices face succession crises where a founder's patience becomes an heir's impatient liquidation; and endowments face pressure to spend now.

The surprising part

The startling part is that many of the most patient players aren't patient by choice — they're patient because they can't get out. A sovereign fund can't dump its stake without wrecking its own reserves; a state enterprise can't exit a strategic project. That involuntary patience only became an advantage because everyone else got speed-addicted. And family offices quietly win — the Aldi, Hermès, and Mars families compound wealth 2-3× faster than public CEOs, with no earnings calls to blow up their thesis.

In one sentence

In a world obsessed with the next quarter, the ability to ignore it for the next forty years has become the rarest and most valuable edge.
