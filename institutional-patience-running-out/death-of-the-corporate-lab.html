Group B · What's being starved

Death of the corporate lab

Bell Labs gave the world the transistor, the laser, and information theory. Nothing like it gets funded now.

Bell Labs, Xerox PARC, IBM Research, DuPont, and RCA once ran long-horizon basic science that produced transistors, the laser, information theory, and the graphical computer — patient research with no immediate payoff. Short-termism, competition, and antitrust breakups gutted them. Today most corporate R&D is applied and near-term, with a few exceptions like Google/DeepMind.

The core bet

These labs made huge bets on fundamental science with no near-term return — funding thousands of researchers to chase how the world works, trusting a transistor or a laser would eventually remake industries. That patience is exactly what modern finance eliminated.

Who funds pure science now?

The loss of patient capital means discovery now waits for a specific problem to solve. When a Bell Labs researcher stumbled on the cosmic microwave background in 1964, the lab funded him to understand it — and he won a Nobel. Today he'd be told to focus on the product roadmap. Pure curiosity got outsourced to universities, themselves squeezed into short grant cycles.

Who ends up ahead

The few mega-firms (Google/DeepMind) rich enough to fund science, plus universities and government labs.

Who gets starved

Basic research itself, and the mid-career scientists who left when the industrial labs closed.

The single biggest worry

Long incubation periods — the transistor took 15 years, information theory had no commercial use for a decade — are incompatible with quarterly reporting. As corporate labs shrank, the role of patient, curiosity-driven science fell to universities and government, which are themselves under pressure to show impact within a grant cycle.

The surprising part

Xerox's failure with PARC shows that owning the lab wasn't even enough — the real constraint was willingness to cannibalize your own cash cow. Xerox was a copier monopoly; the PC threatened it, so its incentives were to protect copier margins, not disrupt itself. Apple and Microsoft walked off with the market. The darker lesson: companies built labs partly to control what rivals might discover, and once monopolies became unstable, the labs turned defensive, then expendable.

In one sentence

We traded a system where patient corporate labs funded basic science with no near-term return for one where every research dollar must pay off in a few years — so industry-remaking discoveries now come from universities, government, and a handful of mega-firms.
