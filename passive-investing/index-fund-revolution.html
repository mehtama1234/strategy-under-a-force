Group A · The index revolution

The index-fund revolution

A boring, low-fee idea Wall Street mocked became the default way America invests.

Jack Bogle launched the first index fund for regular people at Vanguard in 1976, arguing that most active managers can't beat the market after fees — so just buy the whole market cheaply and hold it. Over 50 years the idea won: passive funds now hold roughly half of all US stock-fund money. It democratized investing and saved savers hundreds of billions in fees.

The core bet

Bogle's radical bet: don't try to beat the market — own it, at rock-bottom cost, and let compounding work. Rather than pay experts to pick winners (and mostly lose after fees), buy everything and pay almost nothing. Boring, and right.

What the numbers say

1976
Vanguard launched the first retail index fund with ~$11M (it hoped for $150M). Wall Street derided it as "guaranteed mediocrity" and "un-American."
2019
Passive funds surpassed active in US stock-fund assets for the first time — roughly a 50/50 split, later tilting past 50–60%.
$100Bs
Estimated fees saved for US investors over 50 years versus active management — the compounding of near-zero costs.
3 giants
Vanguard grew from a niche to one of the three largest asset managers on earth, on the back of the passive-first model.

Slow, then all at once

The revolution wasn't a flash of genius — it was a slow grind of compound economics. For decades, despite academic proof, inertia kept money in expensive active funds. Only after 2008 (when active managers blew up alongside everyone) did money flood into passive. Bogle won by being right about math, not by out-selling anyone.

What's newly valuable
Cheap, diversified market exposure for everyone — the best deal ordinary savers ever got.
The seed of the problem
Everyone buying the same funds concentrated ownership in a few managers.
The single biggest worry

If most capital concentrates in passive funds, those funds own huge stakes in every company at once — raising "common ownership" concerns and structural fragility if passive flows ever reverse. The idea succeeded so well it created new risks.

The surprising part

Wall Street fought indexing for decades as lazy and mediocre — yet a product that does almost nothing (just holds the market) quietly became the dominant way Americans invest. After fees and taxes, most active managers fail to beat the index, and that gap compounds massively over decades. Bogle won on arithmetic.

In one sentence

Bogle proved that boring — buying the whole market cheaply and holding it — beats most experts, and in 50 years that heresy became the default way half of Americans invest.
