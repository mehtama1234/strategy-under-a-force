Group C · The risks

Systemic concentration

Index funds promised diversification — and quietly made everyone own the same seven bets.

Index funds promised low-cost diversification, but the modern market is a concentration trap. A handful of mega-cap tech stocks — the "Magnificent Seven" — dominate the S&P 500 so heavily that millions of passive investors unknowingly hold nearly identical portfolios. When everyone owns the same basket and passive flows reverse, "diversification" can turn into synchronized selling.

The core bet

The paradox: buying "the whole market" now means an outsized bet on a few giant stocks, and everyone making the identical bet. Individual diversification quietly became collective concentration — the correlations are artificial, driven by index inclusion, not fundamentals.

What the numbers say

~30%
Share of the S&P 500 in its top 10 stocks (up from ~20% a decade ago) — the highest concentration since the dot-com era.
~70%
Share of recent S&P 500 gains driven by the "Magnificent Seven" — a handful of names carrying the whole "diversified" index.
$20T+
Passive assets across the Big Three — regulators (SEC, FSB, Bank of England) have studied whether they pose systemic, correlated risk.
lockstep flows
In volatility spikes (March 2020), index funds saw simultaneous redemptions that amplified the downswing — everyone selling together.

Diversification that isn't

Because a few mega-caps dominate the index and everyone owns the identical basket, the "diversified" investor holding a broad index fund may be less diversified than an active manager holding 50 hand-picked stocks. The index's internal correlations are increasingly index-induced rather than fundamental — millions of people effectively making the same handful of bets.

The reassurance
Over long horizons, broad indexing still beats most alternatives for the ordinary saver.
The risk
Everyone owning the same thing means everyone can head for the exit together.
The single biggest worry

If a shock hits, passive investors face a coordination problem: each has an incentive to exit, but the index structure means simultaneous selling amplifies the drop — the opposite of what diversification was supposed to prevent.

The surprising part

Index funds created the opposite of what they promised. By concentrating capital in a few mega-caps and forcing millions into identical portfolios, passive investing may have increased systemic concentration rather than reduced it. The person holding a broad-market fund can be less diversified than they think — everyone chasing the same cheap index means everyone panics together if the market turns.

In one sentence

Passive investing solved individual risk but created collective risk — everyone owning the same cheap index means everyone may exit together when the market turns.
