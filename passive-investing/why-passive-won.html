Group A · The index revolution

Why passive won

It's basically math: as a group, active investors must lose after fees.

The engine behind the shift. Over 10–15 years, roughly 85–90% of active US stock funds underperform their benchmark — largely because fees compound and markets are hard to beat. Passive funds charge ~0.03–0.10% versus ~0.5–1%+ for active. Over decades, that fee gap is enormous. So money rationally flooded from active to passive. It's the "arithmetic of active management."

The core bet

The insight (William Sharpe's "arithmetic"): active investors, as a group, hold the market — so before costs they earn the market return, and after their higher fees they must, on average, underperform it. Paying more to try to win is, on average, paying to lose. The cheap index wins by not trying.

What the numbers say

Not skill — subtraction

The shift wasn't a sudden discovery — Malkiel noted it in 1973, Bogle proved it in 1976. The surprise is how slowly it happened: for decades, inertia kept money in expensive active funds despite the proof. Only after 2008, when active managers blew up too, did the money finally move.

Why it won
Lower fees compound into higher long-run returns — a near-guaranteed edge, not a gamble.
The catch
The triumph of cheapness flipped market structure and concentrated ownership.
The single biggest worry

The very success creates "index hugeness": if most money tracks the same benchmarks, price discovery weakens and orphaned stocks (small-cap, emerging-market) get left behind. Winning on cheapness reshapes the whole market.

The surprising part

It's math, not talent. As a group, active investors must underperform the market after their costs — so paying more to try to beat it is, on average, paying to lose. The cheap index quietly wins by doing nothing, and it took millions of investors decades (and a crisis) to accept the arithmetic.

In one sentence

Passive won because, after fees, the math guarantees active managers as a group must underperform — so rational investors chose not to pay for a loss.
