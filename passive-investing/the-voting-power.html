Group B · Common ownership & control

The voting power

Index funds can't sell — so their only weapon is the vote.

An index fund must hold every stock in the index — it can't "sell" a company it dislikes. So its only lever is voting its shares: on directors, mergers, executive pay, and shareholder proposals. That made the Big Three enormously powerful in corporate governance — the swing vote across America — which turned them into a lightning rod for political fury from both the left and the right.

The core bet

Because index funds can never exit, their influence runs entirely through the proxy vote. Hold ~20% of every big company and your vote decides board seats, mergers, and pay across the market — a form of power no one voted to give three money managers.

What the numbers say

2018
BlackRock's Larry Fink "purpose" letters pushed companies on climate and governance — kicking off a multi-year ESG proxy push.
~800–1,000
Proxy votes BlackRock alone casts each annual season; the Big Three together sway a large share of all proxy votes.
2022–23
Red-state backlash ("woke capitalism") pulled billions from funds and pressured the firms; they retreated from ESG-heavy voting.
"voting choice"
In response, BlackRock (and others) launched programs to let fund holders direct their own proxy votes — fragmenting the bloc.

Attacked from both sides

The strange arc: the Big Three went from being criticized for not using their vote enough (2015–18) to being accused of pushing a political agenda (2022–23). The backlash wasn't only from activists — much of it came from conservative states and donors framing ESG voting as unelected overreach. Their escape hatch, "voting choice," paradoxically reduces their own influence.

What's newly valuable
Proxy voting power — the one lever a permanent owner has, worth enormous influence over governance.
What it costs them
Becoming a political target that no one elected — hostage to their own inescapable power.
The single biggest worry

Because index funds can never sell, they're hostage to their voting power — three managers as corporate America's de facto board, exposed to political fury from every direction, with "voting choice" their only way to duck it.

The surprising part

The backlash flipped the whole debate. First the Big Three were told they used their votes too little; then that they used them for a political agenda. Their answer — hand votes back to fund holders — actually lowers their influence over the exact companies where index funds are most powerful. Immense power that no one wants to be seen holding.

In one sentence

Index funds' inability to sell makes their voting power immense and inescapable — turning three managers into the swing vote on corporate America, and a lightning rod for both sides.
