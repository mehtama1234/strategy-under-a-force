Group B · Common ownership & control

Index inclusion

Because passive funds must buy the index, a tiny committee moves trillions.

Because passive funds must buy whatever's in the index, the private committees that decide index membership — at S&P Dow Jones, MSCI, FTSE Russell — became enormous kingmakers. Add a stock and trillions in passive money are forced to buy it; drop one and it can crater. These committees are unregulated, discretionary, and opaque. The more money goes passive, the more power the index-makers hold. (This is the flip side of the sibling gatekeeper tax.)

The core bet

Passive investing was supposed to remove gatekeepers. Instead it created new, unregulated ones: instead of thousands of active managers making independent bets, trillions now flow on a single committee's binary "in or out" decision — a bigger market mover than most regulators.

What the numbers say

$50–90B
Tesla's Dec 2020 S&P 500 inclusion forced that much passive buying; the stock jumped ~70% between announcement and entry — on index mechanics.
~$12T
Global passive assets (up from ~$1T in 2000) that must buy what the committees add — a tiny staff (~50–100 people) directing it.
discretion
Passing every quantitative test doesn't guarantee entry — committees have near-total discretion and no duty to explain a rejection.
no oversight
Index providers aren't SEC-regulated as advisers, face no appeal process, and earn $500M–$1B+ a year in licensing fees.

Kingmakers by default

Inclusion is the closest thing to an official endorsement in modern markets, and the body granting it answers to almost no one. Traders front-run rebalances because passive funds are forced buyers and sellers on known dates — so there's no price discovery at the moment of inclusion, just mechanical demand. If you're excluded from the S&P, MSCI World, and FTSE all at once, there's no one to appeal to.

What's newly valuable
The power to direct passive flows, and the licensing fees — a kingmaker role bigger than most regulators.
What's fragile
Opacity, discretion, and conflicts — a closed-room call can rocket or crater a company.
The single biggest worry

Index committees became kingmakers with zero accountability — they can crater a company by exclusion or inflate a bubble by inclusion, and on those days there's no price discovery because passive funds are forced buyers, with no appeal and no consequences for bad calls.

The surprising part

Passive investing was meant to reduce gatekeepers — instead it created new, unregulated ones with more power than before. Because trillions now flow on one committee's decision, the three index families function like a cartel: excluded from one and you're likely hurt across all of them, with no way to challenge it. Efficient money, opaque power.

In one sentence

Index committees at S&P, MSCI, and FTSE wield more unregulated buying power over global stocks than most regulators — because passive funds must buy whatever they add.
