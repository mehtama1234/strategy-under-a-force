Group C · The risks

Price discovery

If everyone's passive, who's left to figure out what a stock is worth?

Index funds don't analyze companies — they just buy everything in proportion. If passive keeps growing, fewer investors are doing the research that sets a "right" price, so critics worry markets get less efficient: money flows into a stock simply because it's in the index, inflating big names regardless of merit, and mispricings persist. Passive is a free-rider on the price discovery that active investors pay for.

The core bet

The quiet dependency: index funds rely on the shrinking pool of active managers to figure out what things are worth. If that active margin gets too small, the very mechanism that makes prices accurate — constant repricing on new information — may erode, even though passive is brilliant for the individual investor.

What the numbers say

Passive share of US equity fund assets (crossed 50% ~2019). On some measures passive drives 50–60% of trading volume.

"Active share" of many active funds by ~2015 — they hug the index anyway, so even "active" money often isn't setting independent prices.
closet indexing
Capital increasingly flows by market-cap weighting, pushing money into the biggest, most-popular names — merit optional.

The free-rider problem

Index funds profit from the price discovery that active investors do the work of. As passive grows, that's not just slightly less efficient — it could eventually starve the research infrastructure that justifies index prices. Defenders note that active traders still set prices "at the margin," so the market isn't broken. But the margin is shrinking.

The reassurance
Only a small slice of active trading is needed to set prices; the market still functions.
The worry
If active shrinks too far, mispricings could persist for years with no one paid to correct them.
The single biggest worry

If passive keeps growing and active gets too small to move prices, out-of-favor stocks may find no buyer willing to research and value them — mispricings could persist for years because there's no longer enough smart capital hunting them.

The surprising part

Index funds are a textbook free-rider problem: brilliant for the individual (cheap, diversified) but quietly dependent on active investors doing — and paying for — the price discovery. As passive vacuums up half of all new capital, fewer analysts have any incentive to work for a slim edge, slowly starving the very machinery that makes index prices trustworthy.

In one sentence

Passive investing free-rides on active price discovery — and if it keeps growing, there may soon be too few people doing the research that makes stock prices accurate.
