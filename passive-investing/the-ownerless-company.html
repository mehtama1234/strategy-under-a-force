Group C · The risks

The ownerless company

When owners can't sell and can't scrutinize, who actually holds the CEO accountable?

Who holds companies accountable now? Index funds are permanent, indifferent owners — they must hold the stock no matter what, they don't analyze the business, and they own thousands of firms, so they can't seriously oversee any one management team. That weakens the traditional shareholder discipline that kept CEOs honest, and quietly shifts real governance power to two proxy-advisory firms most people have never heard of.

The core bet

As index funds became the dominant shareholders — buy-and-hold, unable to sell — the active, watchful owner faded. Power drifted to proxy advisers (ISS and Glass Lewis) who make voting recommendations that passive funds largely rubber-stamp, leaving CEOs answerable to a consultant's algorithm rather than a real owner.

What the numbers say

~30%+
Share of the S&P 500 held by big index managers (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street) — buy-and-hold owners at the core, unable to walk away.
~40%
Share of US proxy ballots influenced by ISS and Glass Lewis — most passive managers outsource voting to them rather than studying companies.
70–90%
Compliance rates with proxy-adviser recommendations — near-binding, from for-profit, unelected firms with their own conflicts.
drift since ~2000
CEO pay decoupled from performance, cash hoarding and buybacks grew, and activist discipline waned as passive ownership took hold.

Discipline replaced by indifference

The old villain — the activist hedge fund — at least forced management to explain itself and compete for capital. The new world sounds safer (diversified, stable ownership) but is arguably more dangerous: managers face no existential threat because passive investors can't sell and proxy advisers have no skin in the game. Active oversight got traded for passive indifference, and two consulting firms quietly became the real board.

The accountability gap
Permanent owners who won't engage; proxy advisers who optimize for process; managers no one is really watching.
The counterweight
Activist funds still show up — but they're a smaller, unelected force too.
The single biggest worry

Corporations are steered by a self-serving loop: index funds pretend to own but refuse to engage; they vote via proxy advisers optimized for process, not outcomes; and management knows no real owner is scrutinizing returns — so boards become rubber stamps.

The surprising part

Index funds killed the disciplinarian shareholder, and two firms most people have never heard of — ISS and Glass Lewis — inherited the throne, without the incentive or the skin in the game to use it. You traded active oversight for passive indifference, and nobody noticed that a pair of consultants became the actual board of corporate America.

In one sentence

Index funds killed the watchful shareholder, and proxy advisers inherited the power — but they can't and won't really use it, leaving companies quietly ownerless.
