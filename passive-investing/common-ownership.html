Group B · Common ownership & control

Common ownership

Your index fund may own United and Delta and American — all at once.

When the same few funds are the biggest shareholders of all the major airlines, or all the big banks, those "rival" firms share owners. And that may soften competition — why fight hard to steal a customer from a company your own biggest shareholder also owns? A famous 2014 study linked common ownership among airlines to higher fares, and economists and regulators have debated ever since whether it quietly raises prices.

The core bet

The concern: when a small number of index funds own large stakes in every firm in an industry, those firms' interests subtly align. Rather than compete fiercely to take share from each other, they have a financial incentive to keep prices high and avoid destructive rivalry — no explicit collusion needed.

What the numbers say

2014
Azar, Schmalz & Tecu published "Anticompetitive Effects of Common Ownership," linking common ownership to airline fares ~3–4% higher.
2017–18
Follow-up work extended the concern to banking, beer, and more; it entered FTC/DOJ policy discussions (though the effect is hotly debated).
identical top-10
The largest shareholders of United, Delta, and American are often the same funds — and the same across big banks. Rivals share owners.
~20%+
Share of the S&P 500 the Big Three hold collectively — enough to be the dominant owner of competitors simultaneously.

A soft cartel, fully legal

The mechanism is simple: a large stake in both United and Delta means you gain nothing if United steals Delta's customers — you'd rather the duopoly stay calm and profitable. Standard capitalism rewards fierce competition; common ownership quietly dampens the incentive for it. And it happens automatically, with no contract and no meeting.

The worry
Legal index ownership may replace old-fashioned cartels — softer competition, higher prices — with no collusion to prosecute.
The honest caveat
The size of the effect is genuinely contested; some economists find it small or absent.
The single biggest worry

If common ownership truly suppresses competition, it's a massive blind spot — a legal soft cartel with no explicit collusion — and regulators have few tools to stop index funds from owning everyone.

The surprising part

Most retail investors see their S&P 500 fund as passive and harmless. But that fund — and millions of identical ones — holds the same stakes in every major competitor, making the investors (not the firms) the true owners of a potential soft cartel. It's fully legal, happens automatically, and nobody signed anything.

In one sentence

Your passive index fund may own both United and Delta — so its biggest owner has no real incentive for them to compete hard.
