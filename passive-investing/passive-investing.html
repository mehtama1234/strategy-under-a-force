Group E · The big picture

Passive investing

Three firms quietly own everyone's competitors.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains all of them. The move from active stock-picking to cheap index funds — Bogle's idea, now roughly half of US stock-fund money — was genuinely great for savers, who saved a fortune in fees. But it concentrated ownership in three giants who are now the biggest shareholder of nearly every large company at once. That common ownership reshapes competition, voting power, accountability, and where capital flows.

The one idea

Three firms quietly own everyone's competitors. Be fair, though — the alarm can overstate it. Indexing genuinely helped ordinary investors, and most doom scenarios are about the extreme, not today's 50–60% mix. The real issues are concentration, competition, accountability, and price discovery — not that index funds are bad.

Winners vs losers

Winners
Ordinary savers (cheap, diversified, better after-fee returns), the low-fee giants, and companies enjoying softer scrutiny from passive holders.
Losers
Active stock-pickers, smaller managers, price discovery, and — arguably — competition and corporate accountability.
The biggest tension

Passive gave ordinary people the best deal ever — diversification at near-zero cost. But it handed three firms, which didn't seek this power and mostly vote conservatively, leverage over wages, climate capex, and merger approval that only Congress or the Fed had before. The solution (cheap indexing) created the problem (concentration), and you can't undo it without breaking retirement.

The surprising part

The Big Three often vote more conservatively than the median active manager on contested issues — because they own everything, so making one company "greener" doesn't move their portfolio. That passive conservatism is the real lock-in: passive holders can shape outcomes by doing nothing. The most powerful owners in the economy exert their power mostly through inertia.

In one sentence

Three firms now quietly own most of everyone's competitors — helping savers retire cheaper while handing unprecedented, mostly-passive leverage to managers who mostly keep the engine running as-is.

How this connects to the rest of the series

Passive investing braids into several forces. Its index-committee power is the market-moving arm of the gatekeeper tax. Its flight to private markets is the fuel behind the roll-up and private-credit boom, and rhymes with real estate as the moat. And the whole shift is downstream of the end of cheap money era that shaped where capital hunts for return.
