Group D · Forces & limits

The private-markets shift

As the index made public markets cheap, the real action fled to private ones.

The flip side of indexing. As public-market investing became cheap and commoditized, the money chasing higher returns — and control, and secrecy — fled into private markets: private equity, private credit, venture, infrastructure, where fees and control are higher and there's no index. The number of US public companies roughly halved since the 1990s, and companies stay private far longer. The "market" everyone indexes is a shrinking slice of the real economy.

The core bet

As indexing made public stocks a cheap, transparent commodity, sophisticated capital went looking for the opposite — high fees, control, and opacity — in private markets. So the index everyone owns increasingly tracks the aging, mega-cap remainder, while the fastest-growing companies stay hidden. This links to the roll-up and real-estate collections.

Indexing the remainder

The cheaper and more transparent the index became, the more attractive it was to run the real economy — venture, buyouts, infrastructure — away from public view. So indexers own a bigger slice of less: the public market is increasingly a concentration play on mature mega-caps, while the interesting, fast-growing parts happen where ordinary investors can't go.

Who benefits
Private-market managers (high fees, control) and the insiders who can access the fastest-growing companies pre-IPO.
Who's left out
Ordinary savers, whose index owns a shrinking, aging slice of the economy.
The single biggest worry

If the best companies never go public and the smallest public firms can't refinance competitively, public markets become a museum for mature, slow-growth businesses — and ordinary indexers own a shrinking slice of an economy increasingly run from private deal rooms.

The surprising part

Passive indexing was supposed to democratize wealth. Instead it priced public markets so efficiently that active capital abandoned them for private ones. The cheaper and more transparent the index got, the more the real economy moved out of public view — indexers own a bigger share of a shrinking pie.

In one sentence

Passive investing broke the fee structure that sustained public markets, so capital and the fastest-growing companies fled to private markets — leaving the index tracking an aging, hollowed-out slice of the economy.
