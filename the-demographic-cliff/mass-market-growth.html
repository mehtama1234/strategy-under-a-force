Group C · Built on the young crowd

Mass-market brands — when growth by "more people" runs out

For a century, consumer giants grew by simply having more customers each year. That engine is dying.

Think of the big consumer companies — packaged food, drinks, fast food, retail. For a century they grew the easy way: more people every year, more mouths, more households. Population growth was the silent engine under the whole business. When population flattens and ages, that engine dies — and growth suddenly has to be taken from rivals instead of handed over by demographics.

Why aging makes it worse

  • Fewer customers. Over 60 countries are now below replacement fertility; developed markets have been flat or shrinking for decades.
  • They also buy less. Older consumers eat smaller meals, drive less, and have already bought their durable goods. They can drive ~20–30% less consumption than people aged 25–45 — even while making up a growing share of the population.

The substitutes for volume

With "more customers" gone, firms reach for three levers: premiumization (charge more for less — premium beer, "better-for-you" variants), share theft (fight rivals for a fixed pie), and chasing young markets abroad. Most are doing all three at once.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the fixed-pie fight

When volume growth dies and you can't change the demographics, companies claw over a shrinking pie. That means price wars that destroy margins, or price hikes that lose you customers to cheaper rivals. Whole categories — mass beer, soda, packaged snacks — get squeezed between rising costs and a population that just isn't there to buy more.

The surprising part

These giants have been quietly betting against their own home countries for over a decade — and saying so, if you read closely. A CEO won't announce "America is shrinking," but the numbers in their own filings show flat home-market volumes propped up by galloping growth in young nations. The demographic cliff isn't hidden in these businesses; it's spelled out in the fine print. It's just not priced in, because Wall Street keeps rewarding the price hikes and buybacks that paper over it.

In one sentence

Mass-market brands in a nutshell

When the population stops growing, consumer giants lose the free volume growth that carried them for a century and must fight over a fixed, aging pie — raising prices, stealing share, and quietly betting on young markets abroad.
