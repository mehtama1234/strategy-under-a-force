Pensions & Social Security — the math that quietly broke

Today's workers pay for today's retirees. That only works when there are lots of workers per retiree.

Most public pensions, including US Social Security, work on a simple deal: today's workers pay for today's retirees, and when it's their turn, the next generation of workers pays for them. That deal only holds if each retiree is supported by many workers. Longer lives and fewer babies broke that ratio.

The ratio collapsed

5 → ~2.7
US workers per retiree, 1950s to today — and still falling toward ~2.3. Each retiree now leans on far fewer paychecks than the system was designed around.
The cliff has a date

Social Security's trust fund is projected to run dry in the mid-2030s. After that, without changes, it can only pay about 75–80% of promised benefits from incoming payroll taxes. That's not "bankrupt," but it is an automatic ~20–25% benefit cut for tens of millions of people.

The main worry

There's no fix that wins an election. The three options are all political poison:

  • Raise the retirement age → France tried (to 64) in 2023 and got weeks of riots.
  • Raise taxes → hits workers already stretched thin.
  • Cut benefits → breaks a promise to 70+ million retirees and near-retirees who vote.

So Congress has done almost nothing for 40 years while the math got steadily worse.

The surprising part

The danger isn't a sudden collapse — it's the slow one. Because the system can limp along paying partial benefits for decades, there's never a single crisis that forces action. That's the trap: a slow-motion shortfall is politically harder to fix than a sudden cliff, because you can always kick it one more year. And every year of delay makes the eventual fix — higher taxes on the young, or deeper cuts for the old — more brutal.

In one sentence

Pensions & Social Security in a nutshell

Fewer workers supporting more retirees broke the pay-as-you-go math, and because the shortfall is slow rather than sudden, no politician will touch it — leaving higher taxes, later retirement, or smaller checks as the only exits.
