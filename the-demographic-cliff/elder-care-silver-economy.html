Group A · The silver economy

Elder care — the biggest boom market is starving for workers

A giant, growing, well-funded customer base. And nowhere near enough hands to serve it.

This is the clearest winner of an aging world. As the huge post-war generations retire and people live longer, the number of old people explodes — a vast, growing market with money to spend. Senior housing, home care, hearing aids, and adult incontinence products all boom. But there's a brutal catch built into the same force.

The boom is real

~830M → 1.7B
People over 65 worldwide, today to ~2054. The over-80s grow fastest of all. The "silver economy" is already a multi-trillion-dollar market and roughly doubling by the mid-2030s.
  • Adult incontinence products are a ~$16B market growing ~7% a year — the 80+ group needs them most, and that group is exploding.
  • Hearing aids are a ~$10B market; one in three people aged 65–74 has hearing loss.
  • Senior housing is effectively at capacity.

The catch: the money is there, the workers aren't

The single biggest risk — you can't hire caregivers

The very same aging that creates the customers removes the workers. Around 87% of US nursing homes report staffing shortages, and most are limiting new admissions because they can't find staff. In some states, seniors approved for care get half the hours they're entitled to — not for lack of money, but for lack of people. The model breaks on labor, not demand.

The surprising part

The constraint here isn't what you'd expect. It's not demand or funding — families are spending trillions, governments are paying, seniors have assets. The bottleneck is hands. That flips who actually wins: not the nursing-home chains (all fighting over the same tiny pool of workers and drowning in labor costs), but whoever can reduce the need for a human caregiver — the robotics, remote monitoring, and prevention drugs. In an aging world, the winning move in elder care is to need fewer people to deliver it.

In one sentence

Elder care in a nutshell

Aging created the world's biggest boom market and, with the very same stroke, the worker shortage that's strangling it — so the real winners are whoever can care for the old with fewer human hands.
