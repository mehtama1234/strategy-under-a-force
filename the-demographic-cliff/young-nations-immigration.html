Group D · The national cases

Young nations & migration — where the young people still are

The flip side of aging: in a world short of young people, the places that still have them hold the asset.

While the rich world and China age and shrink, some places are still young and growing — above all India (now the most populous country) and Sub-Saharan Africa (where most of this century's population growth will happen). In a world running short of young workers, that youth becomes a globally scarce, valuable resource — and immigration becomes the main bridge between the two halves.

Where the young are

age ~28 / ~18
Median age in India (~28, and now the most populous country as of 2023) and across Sub-Saharan Africa (~18–19). Nigeria alone could reach ~800 million by 2050.

Sub-Saharan Africa is set to drive around 70% of global population growth this century — the one region still in "youth-bulge" mode for decades.

Immigration flips from choice to necessity

The inversion

For aging countries, immigration stops being a cultural debate and becomes arithmetic. In most projections, net immigration is now the only source of workforce growth in the US and much of Europe — not a surplus, but a deficit being filled. That flips the power: young nations like India and Nigeria, not the US or EU, hold the demographic advantage.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the mismatch traps everyone

A young population is only a "dividend" if there are jobs. Africa's youth unemployment runs ~20–25%. If young nations can't create jobs fast enough and aging countries put up migration barriers, you get a trapped generation — youth that promised growth delivering instability and migration pressure instead. The bridge is exactly where the politics is most explosive.

The surprising part

Two inversions at once. First, immigration becomes economically essential precisely as it turns politically toxic — aging voters resist the very inflows their pensions and hospitals depend on. Second, a young population is not automatically a blessing: without jobs, the same youth bulge becomes a liability, not a dividend. That's why some of the fastest-growing regions are also the most fragile — and why the century's biggest economic question may be whether the world's labor-starved half and job-starved half can meet in the middle.

In one sentence

Young nations & migration in a nutshell

The rich world's cliff makes the young people of India and Africa the scarcest resource of the century — economically essential yet politically toxic — creating a global mismatch between labor-starved aging countries and job-starved young ones.
