Group B · The squeezed systems

Healthcare systems — exploding demand meets a shrinking supply of carers

Old people use far more healthcare. And the nurses and doctors are themselves retiring.

Old age is where healthcare spending lives — the last years of life are the most expensive. So as the population ages, healthcare demand balloons and lands on the public purse (Medicare in the US, national health systems elsewhere). At the same time, the nurses and doctors who deliver care are aging and retiring, with too few young ones to replace them. It's a double squeeze.

Who wins, who strains

Win

Drugmakers and device makers — aging guarantees rising demand for treatments (Alzheimer's, cancer, heart failure). Dementia cases alone are set to roughly double by 2050.

Strain

The public payers who foot the bill. Medicare's share of the US budget keeps climbing, crowding out everything else.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — you can't print nurses

The binding limit turns out not to be money but people. You can raise taxes, but you cannot quickly train hundreds of thousands of nurses. When there aren't enough carers or beds, systems ration by waiting — long queues — which quietly splits care into two tiers: those who can pay to skip the line, and everyone else who waits.

The surprising part

Most people frame this as "costs rising faster than revenue." That's true but misses the real shock: the ceiling is workforce capacity, not budget. And the cheapest fix of all — keeping 40-year-olds healthy so they cost less at 70 — is politically invisible, because the savings show up two decades later, long after any election.

In one sentence

Healthcare systems in a nutshell

Aging makes healthcare demand explode while the supply of nurses and doctors shrinks, so the real limit becomes carers rather than cash — and systems ration by waiting while drugmakers profit.
