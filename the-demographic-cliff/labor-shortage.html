Group B · The squeezed systems

The labor shortage — workers went from cheap to scarce

For 70 years, labor was abundant and employers held the power. That balance is flipping.

For most of modern history, there were always more young workers arriving — so labor was cheap and plentiful, and employers set the terms. As birth rates fell, the working-age population (roughly 15–64) is now peaking and shrinking across the rich world and China. That flips the balance: workers become scarce and more valuable, and employers scramble.

The turn

peaked ~2015
China's working-age population peaked around 2015 and now shrinks. Japan's peaked in the mid-1990s (down ~15 million since). The rich world is following.

You could see the power shift after 2020: unemployment hit 50-year lows and wages jumped fastest in the hardest-to-fill jobs — care, trades, hospitality, construction — after decades of flat pay.

The three responses

  • Automate — replace missing workers with machines (see robots).
  • Immigrate — import workers to fill the gap (see young nations & migration).
  • Raise participation — keep older people working longer, pull in anyone previously outside the workforce.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The same shrinking workforce that lifts wages also starves the pension and healthcare systems that need a big tax base (see pensions). Fewer workers per retiree means either punishing taxes on the young, collapsing benefits for the old, or immigration at a scale that's politically explosive. Good news for the individual worker; hard news for the public books.

The surprising part

Two things defy the forecasts. First, automation is not riding to the rescue as fast as economists predicted — robots are expensive and clumsy, so immigration is quietly doing most of the gap-filling, which is politically harder to sustain. Second, the one group whose numbers at work are rising is the oldest: workers over 65 are staying employed longer, reversing a century of early retirement. The "shortage" is putting the elderly back to work.

In one sentence

The labor shortage in a nutshell

A 70-year buyer's market for labor is flipping to a seller's market in a single generation — lifting workers' wages while straining the pension and health systems that depended on a big, young workforce.
