Group C · The re-localization

Reshoring the plate

Vertical farms mostly grow lettuce — because staples don't pencil out and the economics keep breaking.

In response to fragile global food chains, there's a push to localize: regional food systems, 'grow it closer,' controlled-environment agriculture (vertical farms, greenhouses), and rich desert nations buying farmland abroad. Vertical farming had a loud boom and a brutal bust — and the hard economics keep getting in the way.

The core bet

If global food chains are fragile, growing food closer to where it's eaten — indoors, regionally, controllably — becomes a resilience play, even if it costs more.

Indoor farming can't compete on calories

A head of vertical-farm lettuce costs 3–5x a field-grown one, and staples — wheat, corn, rice — are hopeless indoors because they run on thin margins and vast scale. So controlled-environment farming works only for premium niches (herbs, tomatoes, microgreens); it can't feed billions at $2 a meal. The moment energy prices rise or investors lose patience, the argument crumbles.

The single biggest worry

Indoor and local farming is expensive and energy-hungry and can't replace commodity staples, so the economics collapse whenever power gets dearer or funding dries up.

The surprising part

The telling detail is the lettuce. Vertical farms grow it because it's the rare crop where quick turnover and a local premium overlap — yet lettuce is ~3% of global produce calories. And rich desert nations 'reshored' food not by growing it at home but by buying other countries' farmland, which arguably makes the fragility problem worse, not better.

In one sentence

Local and indoor farming promise resilience but can't beat field farming on the cost of calories, so rich markets brand premium lettuce while rich nations hoard farmland abroad.
