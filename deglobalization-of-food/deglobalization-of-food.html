Deglobalization of food

We thought distance insulated us; instead it made us fragile — a war or a virus far away now moves your grocery bill.

Pull the whole collection together and one line runs through it. Cheap global food for seventy years rested on three bets — oil staying cheap, trade routes staying open, and weather staying predictable — and all three are breaking. So food is fragmenting into regional blocs, and power is pooling at the choke points: the fertilizer, the seeds, the silos, the land — because you can't ship calories as easily when energy costs more and wars close ports, and you can't replace the inputs that make food grow.

The core bet

Cheap global food rested on cheap energy, a stable climate, and open trade, all now eroding — so food fragments into blocs and power pools at the input and trade choke points.

Winners and losers

Fragmentation sorts cleanly into who controls the scarce thing and who depends on the market.

Who ends up ahead

The input controllers (fertilizer makers, the seed and chemical oligopoly), the grain traders who profit from volatility, farmland owners, and net food-exporting nations.

Who's exposed

Import-dependent poor nations, consumers facing higher and more volatile grocery bills, and anyone who assumed food would always be cheap and available.

The single biggest worry

Full deglobalization is impossible — geography dictates what grows where — and localism is costly, so the likely future isn't autarky but a more fragmented, more expensive, more volatile, still partly-global system: efficiency traded for resilience.

The surprising part

The note to end on is the fragility we built without noticing. We optimized the whole food system for one thing — moving calories as cheaply as possible — which meant thin margins, no redundancy, and chains that snap if a single node breaks. A war in Ukraine moves bread prices in Egypt; a virus in a California chicken coop ripples to omelets in Tokyo; a heatwave in Ghana shows up in your chocolate. We thought distance insulated us; it just made us fragile. Now the withdrawal of cheap energy and open trade forces every country to ask a question it hadn't in generations: can I actually feed myself? For most, the honest answer is no.

In one sentence

The global food system is reshuffling from an efficiency-first network into competing regional blocs, making fertilizer, seeds, and land the new scarce assets while pushing food prices up and volatility higher.
