Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why cheap food was a subsidy

We optimized the food system for efficiency and deleted its resilience — and the props are wobbling.

This is the mechanism. For seventy years food got cheaper because of a stack of hidden supports: cheap fossil-fuel fertilizer and transport, a stable climate, open global trade, thin just-in-time inventories, and specialization (each region growing what it does best). All of those are now under stress at once — so the era of cheap, reliable global food was really borrowing against conditions that are ending.

The core bet

Cheap global food rested on a stack of hidden supports — cheap energy, a stable climate, open trade, thin inventories, and specialization — and all are eroding at once, so the cheapness is ending.

Cheap food was a bet on conditions that held for decades

Modern cheap food was really a wager that oil stays cheap, trade never breaks, and weather stays mild — all at once. To squeeze out cost, the system deleted redundancy: thin reserves, single-source dependencies, razor-thin margins. It worked beautifully under the exact conditions of the last several decades — and had no room to move the moment any of them broke.

The single biggest worry

Withdrawing several hidden subsidies at once — cheap energy, a stable climate, open trade — could make food structurally more expensive and more volatile, not just briefly pricier.

The surprising part

The core insight is a trade-off we made without noticing: we optimized every farm, chain, and reserve for efficiency and, in doing so, deleted resilience. The system became fragile precisely because it was so efficient — able to run smoothly only under the conditions we'd always had, and exposed the instant they changed.

In one sentence

Cheap global food for 70 years wasn't earned — it was a side effect of cheap oil, open borders, and mild weather, and now that those props are shaky, food is structurally pricier with almost no buffer.
