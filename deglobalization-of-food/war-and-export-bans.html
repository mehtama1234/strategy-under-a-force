Group B · The shocks fragmenting it

War & export bans

When food gets scarce, countries hoard it — and each ban makes the global market smaller and more dangerous.

When food gets scarce, countries treat it like a military resource and stop exporting to protect their own people. Every ban shrinks the global market and makes it less reliable. Russia's 2022 invasion choked Black Sea grain; India banned rice exports in 2023; Indonesia restricted palm oil in 2022; more than twenty countries imposed food-export curbs. This is deglobalization happening in real time.

The core bet

In a crisis, food becomes a national-security asset, so countries stop exporting to protect themselves — and every ban makes the global market smaller and more dangerous to rely on.

What the numbers say

25–30%
Share of world wheat exports from Russia and Ukraine before the 2022 Black Sea blockade; wheat jumped ~50% in weeks.
~40%
India's share of the global rice trade; its July 2023 export ban spiked prices ~50% in days.
2022
Indonesia's palm-oil export restriction — tightening a supply used in food, oil, and soap worldwide.
20+
Countries that imposed food-export bans or heavy restrictions in 2022 alone.
2007–08 / 2010–11
Earlier export-ban food crises that doubled rice and wheat prices — the pattern repeating.

Bans are contagious — a shortage becomes a panic

Export bans spread: when one country stops selling, others panic and follow, turning a regional shortage into a global price shock. The nations hit hardest are the poorest importers — Egypt, Yemen, Tunisia — that can't grow their own food and can't absorb the price spike, so a policy meant to protect one country's people starves another's.

The single biggest worry

Bans cascade — one triggers the next — converting a manageable shortage into a full-blown panic, and the import-dependent poor pay first and worst.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable revelation is that free trade in food only holds during abundance. The moment there's a shortage, the assumption 'you can always buy it on the market' evaporates, because every government values feeding its own voters over honoring open-trade rules — and each has the incentive to ban first.

In one sentence

When countries hoard food in a crisis instead of selling it, everyone's ability to buy stable food erodes — and the poorest, farm-less nations pay the price.
