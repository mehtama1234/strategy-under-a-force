Deglobalization of Food

Cheap global food was a hidden subsidy — and the bill is coming due.

The 48th force. For seventy years the food system globalized — cheap grain, thin just-in-time supply chains, and a few trading houses moving calories around the planet. Now it's fragmenting into regional and "friendly" blocs. War (Ukraine grain and fertilizer), export bans (India rice, Indonesia palm oil), fertilizer and energy shocks, climate-driven crop failures, and animal-disease border closures are all breaking the old assumption that you can always buy food from wherever it's cheapest. The one idea: as cheap energy, a stable climate, and open trade all erode at once, the scarce, valuable things become the inputs nobody can substitute — fertilizer, seeds, and the ground itself — and the middlemen who move the calories.

A fresh force (Tier-7) Not from the Inc. 5000 taxonomy — surfaced by a Haiku discovery-scout pass for genuinely new macro forces. The physical-inputs cousin of the-decoupling and reshoring, applied to the plate.
The one question, asked of everyone

What did the deglobalization of food make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? The un-substitutable inputs — fertilizer, seed genetics, and farmland — became strategic and valuable; the "you can always buy it" assumption became risky; and food security turned into statecraft. The danger: the same scarcity that enriches the input owners and the traders who profit from volatility means higher, more volatile grocery bills — and hunger for the poorest, import-dependent nations.
