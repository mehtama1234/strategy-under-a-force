Group A · The choke points

The grain traders

Four firms you've never heard of move most of the world's food — and make the most when it's scarce.

Four giant, mostly private trading houses — ADM, Bunge, Cargill, and Louis Dreyfus, the 'ABCD' — dominate the global trade in grains and oilseeds. They own the elevators, ships, and ports, and they know what farmers are selling and what nations need to buy. They profit from moving calories and, above all, from the volatility — so a fragmenting, crisis-prone food world is where they thrive.

The core bet

Own the infrastructure that moves food and the information about supply and demand, and you profit from every ton that crosses the world — and even more when prices swing.

They make the most exactly when food is hardest to find

The traders are structurally positioned to profit from food crises: they earn when prices rise, when they fall, and most of all when they swing violently. That draws political scrutiny in importing nations and, in turn, food nationalism can disrupt the very flows they depend on — a tension baked into the model.

The single biggest worry

They profit from volatility and scarcity — which invites political backlash in a hungry world — and rising food nationalism can choke the international flows their business runs on.

The surprising part

The quiet astonishment is the scale of the anonymity: four companies most people can't name control more of the world's traded food than every visible supermarket, restaurant chain, and food brand combined. The machinery that feeds the planet runs silently, in the hands of traders you've never heard of.

In one sentence

Four traders you've never heard of own the ships and silos that move most of the world's grain, and they earn the most exactly when food is scarcest and dearest.
