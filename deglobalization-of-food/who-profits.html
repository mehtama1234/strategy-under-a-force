Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

Billionaires are buying farmland at the exact moment food is getting scarcer — that's the strategy.

This is the follow-the-money piece. In a fragmenting, volatile food world, profit and power pool at the choke points: the input controllers (fertilizer makers, the seed and chemical oligopoly), the grain traders who thrive on volatility, farmland owners treating land as an inflation-proof asset, ag-commodity investors, and the nations that are net food exporters. The losers are import-dependent poor countries and ordinary shoppers.

The core bet

Scarcity and volatility reward whoever owns the scarce inputs (fertilizer, seeds, land) and whoever moves the calories (traders) — food fragmentation concentrates power at the choke points.

Volatility and crisis are profitable — if you're on the supply side

The same scarcity that fills the wallets of fertilizer makers, seed companies, and traders means grocery bills the poorest can't afford — food riots in Egypt, Sri Lanka, and Yemen in 2022–23 tracked grain-price spikes directly. A trader earns on every wild swing; a seed company earns when a farmer gets desperate; the crisis is someone's record quarter.

The single biggest worry

The scarcity that enriches input owners and traders means higher, more volatile prices and real hunger for the poorest — a moral and political flashpoint that can topple governments.

The surprising part

The clarifying move is what the richest are doing: buying farmland hand over fist exactly as fragmentation makes food scarcer. That's not a contradiction — it's the play. In a world of scarcity, they're purchasing the one asset that matters, because the cheapest solution for the rich is to own the supply, while the only 'solution' for the poor is to go hungry.

In one sentence

Deglobalization turns food scarcity into an asset class for the few who control seeds, land, and trade routes, while the poor pay higher prices with no choice.
