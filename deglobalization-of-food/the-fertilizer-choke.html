Group A · The choke points

The fertilizer choke

Half the world's food rests on synthetic fertilizer — controlled by a handful of countries.

Modern agriculture depends on synthetic fertilizer — nitrogen made from natural gas, plus potash and phosphate — and the supply is dangerously concentrated. Russia and Belarus dominate potash; nitrogen tracks the price of natural gas; a few countries control phosphate. The 2022 Ukraine war spiked fertilizer prices and exposed just how fragile the world's food really is.

The core bet

Fertilizer is the invisible input that half the world's food depends on, and it's controlled by a few countries — so whoever controls fertilizer controls food.

There's no quick substitute for a fertilizer shock

A fertilizer shock — from war, an export ban, or an energy collapse — cascades straight into higher food prices and hunger, especially in poor countries. The nitrogen in a field of wheat can't be swapped out quickly for compost or manure at scale, so a squeeze at the top of the chain shows up in grocery bills within weeks.

The single biggest worry

A single disruption to fertilizer — a country hoarding potash, a gas-price spike shutting nitrogen plants — cascades into a global food-price and hunger crisis, and there's no fast replacement.

The surprising part

The startling fact is personal: roughly 40% of the nitrogen in your body came from a fossil-fuel-powered factory. About two billion people are, in part, made of energy-intensive artificial nitrogen — so a fertilizer embargo isn't a distant trade quarrel, it's about whether people get fed.

In one sentence

Three countries control the nitrogen and potash that feed billions, and a war or export ban can spike food prices worldwide within weeks.
