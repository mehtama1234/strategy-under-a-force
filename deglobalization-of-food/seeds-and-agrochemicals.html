Group A · The choke points

Seeds & agrochemicals

Four companies own most of the world's seeds and the chemicals farmers must buy every year.

A handful of giants — Bayer (with Monsanto), Corteva, Syngenta (owned by ChemChina), and BASF — control most of the world's commercial seeds and crop chemicals. Patented seeds farmers can't legally replant, a treadmill of ever-stronger chemicals, and heavy consolidation give them enormous power over the starting point of the entire food chain. In a fragmenting world, seed genetics become a strategic asset.

The core bet

Control the seed and the chemicals farmers must buy every season, and you control the starting point of the whole food chain — a recurring, patented, near-monopoly input.

The dependency is the business model, not a loophole

Farmers legally can't replant the seeds they harvest — that's not a cheat, it's the intended design, a locked-in annual purchase of something as essential as food. And the 'chemical treadmill' compounds it: resistant weeds evolve, farmers spray more, the company sells stronger herbicides, and the cycle repeats, raising costs each season.

The single biggest worry

Concentration raises farmer costs and food-security risk, and it's exposed to litigation (Roundup) and regulation (glyphosate bans in Europe and Brazil) that can jolt supply.

The surprising part

The strategic twist is who holds the seeds. A nation that controls seed genetics — as China now does through Syngenta — holds a quiet weapon in a food-fragmented world: it can restrict exports, raise prices, or extract concessions. The choke point sits before the crop has even sprouted.

In one sentence

Four giant chemical companies own most of the world's seeds and the chemicals farmers must rebuy yearly, making food security hostage to their patents and prices.
