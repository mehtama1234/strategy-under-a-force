Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The limits

You can't grow oranges in Norway — full food independence would make you poorer and hungrier, not safer.

This is the skeptical piece. Food can't fully deglobalize: geography and climate dictate what grows where, self-sufficiency and indoor farming are expensive and often uneconomic, alt-protein stalled, and full food autarky would make food more expensive and less varied. The efficient global system is hard to replace — so the realistic future is partial fragmentation (friendlier trade blocs, bigger reserves), not full deglobalization.

The core bet

Food can't fully deglobalize because geography dictates what grows where, and going local is costly — so the realistic future is a more fragmented, more expensive, but still partly-global system.

Overcorrecting toward food nationalism backfires

Food nationalism sounds like safety but can create the opposite: if countries hoard reserves and wall off trade, global shocks hit faster and spread further before aid arrives, and expensive local food hits poor families hardest. The strongest economies would lose more from full autarky than they'd gain — a self-sufficient America would eat less, pay more, and forfeit trade's leverage.

The single biggest worry

Overcorrecting toward food nationalism could make food more expensive and less secure, not more — a fragmented system with no efficient global flow lets shortages spread locally before relief arrives.

The surprising part

The counter-intuitive resolution is that the resilient answer isn't isolation, it's the opposite done carefully: keep trade open, build months of reserves, and 'friend-shore' with trusted allies rather than wall yourself off. You can't grow everything everywhere, so the goal is a buffered, diversified global system — not an impossible, impoverishing self-sufficiency.

In one sentence

You can't grow everything everywhere, local farming is costly and unreliable, and trying to be food-independent would make you poorer and hungrier, not safer.
