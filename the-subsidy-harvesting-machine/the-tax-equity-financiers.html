The tax-equity financiers

Over 90% of America's affordable housing gets built because banks need to park a tax deduction.

Beyond clean energy, 'tax equity' is a broad, quiet business: banks and corporations invest in subsidized projects primarily to capture tax credits and deductions — Low-Income Housing Tax Credits, New Markets credits, historic-rehab credits, and renewables. A few big banks dominate because they have the tax appetite. The investment is really a tax-reduction product with a social side effect.

The core bet

A company with a big tax bill can invest in a subsidized project mainly to capture its tax credits, turning 'doing good' into a way to lower taxes — the credit is the return.

The social good is a side effect of tax arbitrage

More than 90% of affordable housing in America gets built because banks need to park tax deductions, not because the units pencil out as a business. The housing is real and needed — but the entire production machine runs on tax arbitrage, which is why it's hostage to keeping the credit permanently in the code.

The single biggest worry

The pool of tax-appetite investors is small and concentrated, and the whole structure depends on the credits existing — a policy change or a shift in bank profits shrinks the pool and can crater production overnight.

The surprising part

The reveal is that 'impact investing' here is a tax-reduction product wearing a social costume. A bank puts $100M into affordable housing for the $25–40M of tax it won't have to pay — flip the incentive and the whole thing stops, which is why the nation's affordable housing depends on a line in the tax code.

In one sentence

Big banks finance affordable housing and renewables by buying tax credits to shrink their own tax bills — making the social impact a side effect of tax reduction.
