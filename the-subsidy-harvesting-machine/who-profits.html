Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

For every dollar aimed at a solar company or a student, a chunk sticks to the hands in the middle.

This is the follow-the-money piece. Public subsidy money pools with the companies designed to capture it (EV and clean-energy makers, for-profit schools, affordable-housing developers), the intermediaries who monetize it (tax-equity banks, credit brokers, ERC and R&D consultants), the lobbyists and law firms who shape it, and the corporations that buy transferable credits to cut their taxes. The public pays; a cut is skimmed at every step.

The core bet

The government money flows to whoever is positioned to capture and monetize the subsidy — the harvesters and the middlemen — and a cut is skimmed at every step between the taxpayer and the goal.

Big corporations buy the leftover credits at a discount

Apple, Google, and banks routinely buy transferable credits at 80–95 cents on the dollar to cut their tax bills — arbitraging the gap between a credit's legal 'value' and what a developer desperate for cash will take. The intermediaries undercut book value to cover their fees, so the original recipient gets quick cash and the middle keeps the spread.

The single biggest worry

A big share of public subsidy is captured by intermediaries and well-positioned companies rather than delivering the public benefit intended — leaks estimated at 20–40 cents on the dollar.

The surprising part

The system's irony is that the harvesters now lobby to expand the subsidies — not because the public gains more, but because a bigger subsidy pool means bigger intermediary fees. A parallel economy of banks, mills, and brokers exists to intercept public money and resell it, and it has every incentive to grow the very programs it skims.

In one sentence

The government sends money to clean energy and schools; banks and consultants intercept it, take a cut, and resell what's left to big corporations looking to cut their taxes.
