Group A · Where the subsidy IS the business

EV & clean-energy credits

For years, Tesla's profit wasn't the cars — it was the regulatory credits it sold to rivals.

Electric vehicles and clean-energy makers lean heavily on government money: the $7,500 US consumer EV credit, IRA manufacturing credits, and — famously — Tesla's early profits from selling 'regulatory credits' to other automakers who couldn't meet emissions rules. For much of a decade the subsidy, not the product, was the margin.

The core bet

The government pays people to buy EVs and pays companies to make them, so the credit is often worth more than the profit on the product — design the business to capture it.

Tesla's early business was government arbitrage

For years Tesla's profit came not from cars but from selling regulatory credits to rivals who needed them to pass pollution laws — it helped other companies meet quotas, pocketed the check, and funded car production with it. When you buy an EV, a big slice of the affordability is a decision by Congress, not an achievement of engineering.

The single biggest worry

The whole model rests on subsidies a change in government can end overnight — the 2025 EV-credit repeal — leaving businesses whose margin was the credit suddenly exposed.

The surprising part

The startling reveal is where the profit actually lived: a company celebrated for its cars was, for years, a government-arbitrage play in disguise. The 'business' wasn't building EVs profitably — it was capturing and reselling the incentives around them.

In one sentence

The EV industry is a government-subsidy arbitrage where the credit can be worth more than the profit on the car, so a change in law can kill the business overnight.
