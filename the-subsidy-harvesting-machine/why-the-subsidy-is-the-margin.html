Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why the subsidy is the margin

When the check is bigger than the profit, you optimize for the government — not the customer.

This is the mechanism. In many industries the government subsidy is not a bonus but the actual profit margin: a product that loses money on its own becomes profitable once the credit is added. That distorts behavior — companies design products to qualify (chasing the rule, not the customer), invest where the subsidies are (not where demand is), and build businesses that only work with the incentive. The subsidy becomes the real customer.

The core bet

When a subsidy is bigger than the profit on the product, the rational move is to optimize for the subsidy — design, price, and build for the check, not the customer.

The buyer becomes incidental

The government is the real customer — the entity writing the check — while the end-user is just the vehicle for capturing the credit. That flips incentives backwards: a company can ignore the buyer's actual problem because it's paid to satisfy the government's definition of the problem, which may not be the same thing. A credit meant to spur solar adoption instead pays companies to game the rules and oversell.

The single biggest worry

Optimizing for the subsidy instead of the customer builds fragile businesses with no product margin to fall back on, and misallocates capital toward the credit rather than real demand.

The surprising part

The core distortion is a reversal of who's being served. When the subsidy exceeds the product's profit, the smart company stops solving the customer's problem and starts solving the rule-writer's — engineering to the threshold, siting to the map of incentives, and treating the end-user as a formality on the way to the check.

In one sentence

When the subsidy is bigger than the profit, the business optimizes for the credit, not the customer — and fragile companies and wasted capital are the real output.
