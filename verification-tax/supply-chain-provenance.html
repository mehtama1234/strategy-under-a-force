Group B · Proving a thing is real

Supply-chain provenance

Proving where it came from — and how it was made — became a tax of its own.

Proving a product's backstory — that the fish was caught legally, the cocoa wasn't child labor, the diamond wasn't a blood diamond — is now a business and a compliance burden. Food-safety traceability, "conflict-free" minerals, diamond certification, organic and fair-trade labels, and new EU deforestation rules all demand documented provenance. Certifiers and traceability software sell the proof — even though the paperwork can be gamed.

The core bet

The bet: proving where something came from, convincingly, is now a material business cost — driven by regulation, conscience, and liability. The infrastructure that delivers it (certifiers, audits, traceability platforms) captures part of that cost as a growing industry.

The paperwork gets gamed

Provenance systems are undermined constantly — forged certifications, false shipping records, bribed auditors. A farm can buy a "conflict-free" certificate it doesn't deserve; a diamond can be laundered through a third country and re-certified. So verification becomes an arms race that raises costs without fully closing the gaps.

What's newly valuable
Certifiers, auditors, and traceability software — a protected industry selling documented proof of origin.
What's hollow
Certificates that can be forged or bribed — "compliance theater" as much as real due diligence.
The single biggest worry

The paperwork is gamed constantly — forged certs, false records, bribed auditors — so provenance becomes a fraud-vs-auditor arms race that pushes costs higher without truly closing the gaps.

The surprising part

The verification infrastructure itself became a protected interest that resists automation. Blockchain was supposed to slash certification costs 70–80%; incumbents fought back with complexity, and the resulting platforms are slower and pricier than the paper systems they meant to replace. Verification cost has partly decoupled from actual fraud prevention.

In one sentence

Proving a product's origin and ethics became a regulatory tax where the cost of proof often exceeds what you'd save by simply trusting the supply chain.
