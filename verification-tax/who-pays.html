Group D · The mechanism & the paradox

Who pays

A hidden, quietly regressive tax — heaviest on those least able to pass the check.

Verification isn't free. It's a tax paid in money (compliance, fraud tools, authentication fees baked into prices), in time and friction (endless logins, 2FA, CAPTCHAs, ID uploads), and in exclusion (real people locked out when a check fails). And it's regressive — it lands hardest on the poor, the elderly, immigrants, and anyone whose documents or biometrics don't match the system's narrow idea of "legitimate."

The core bet

The uncomfortable truth: verification doesn't reduce cost — it redistributes it. A company that cuts fraud doesn't lower prices; it raises them to fund the prevention. And the person with an unusual name, no smartphone, or older paperwork gets locked out, while the well-documented breeze through.

Cost redistributed, not removed

100+ passwords per person, 2FA loops, ID uploads — that's 15–20 minutes a day for the average user. A single Apple-toggle-style regulation (GDPR cost the EU $3–5B) gets passed to users in higher prices and longer signups. The "unusual" person pays most: no clean paperwork means no paycheck, no loan, no benefits, while a glitch offers no explanation and no appeal.

Who pays most
The poor, elderly, immigrants, and anyone whose face, name, or documents don't fit the model — locked out, not just slowed.
Who barely notices
The well-documented and high-income — a friction point, not a barrier.
The single biggest worry

Verification systems get more aggressive exactly when they should get more lenient — and they fail hardest on the people who can least afford to be locked out. A glitch means no paycheck, loan, or benefits for months, against a system that won't explain why it said no.

The surprising part

Verification doesn't reduce cost — it redistributes it. A firm that cuts fraud raises prices to pay for the prevention, and the person with an unusual name or no smartphone gets locked out entirely. The well-documented breeze through; the verification tax adds cost and exclusion for exactly the people already struggling — and calls it security.

In one sentence

The verification tax is a regressive hidden levy — paid by everyone in money and friction, but with exclusion falling hardest on those whose documents or biometrics don't match the system's narrow definition of "legitimate."
