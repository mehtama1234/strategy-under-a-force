Group A · Proving who you are

KYC / AML

$200B a year to prove customers aren't criminals — and it catches maybe 1%.

Every bank and fintech must, by law, verify each customer's identity and screen for dirty money — "know your customer" and "anti-money-laundering." That legal duty spawned a giant compliance industry. The strange part: it costs enormous sums and catches only a sliver of actual laundering, but firms pay anyway because the fine for not having the system is far worse.

The core bet

The bet is really about legal cover, not effectiveness. Build the expensive screening and you can't be blamed for the launderer who slips through; skip it and face crushing fines. So the whole industry runs on effort, not results — the 1% catch rate almost doesn't matter, the 100% effort requirement does.

What the numbers say

$200B+
Global financial-crime compliance spend a year — large banks put 10–15% of operating budgets into compliance, KYC/AML a big chunk.
~1%
Share of laundered money the system actually catches (UN/FATF consensus). Trillions flow; a fraction is seized.
$1.9B
HSBC's 2012 fine for moving cartel cash — the precedent that made nine-figure penalties routine, and effort mandatory.
3,000–5,000+
Dedicated AML analysts at a single large bank; some spend $500M–$1B a year on AML operations alone.

Effort, not outcome

The rules trace to the Bank Secrecy Act (1970), tightened by the PATRIOT Act (2001) and globalized via the FATF standards in 200+ countries. What they demand is process: identify everyone, screen everyone, file the reports. Whether it works is almost beside the point — having the machinery is what shields the bank from liability.

What's newly valuable
Compliance software and armies of analysts — a durable industry selling legal cover.
What it costs
Enormous sums for thin results, plus friction and exclusion pushed onto ordinary customers.
The single biggest worry

The system is so fragile that even sophisticated banks occasionally miss a criminal flow — and the fine for that miss is often larger than the money they actually caught. It's a tax you must pay in full to avoid a bigger one.

The surprising part

The compliance industry is backwards: firms spend $200B+ to catch perhaps 1% of laundering, because the absence of the machinery is infinitely more expensive than its poor results. It's less a security measure than a legal-liability tax on the entire financial system — pay for the effort, and you're covered.

In one sentence

Banks spend $200B+ a year to verify customers aren't criminals, catch ~1% of laundered money, and do it anyway — because it's less a security measure than a legal-liability tax.
