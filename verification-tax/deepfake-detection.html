Group C · Proving content is real

Deepfake detection

A single fake video already stole $25 million — and the detectors are losing.

AI can now fake voices, faces, and video convincingly, so a new industry sells detection — and it's losing the arms race. A deepfake of a company's CFO on a video call stole $25M in one go. Voice clones drain relatives and executives. Detectors that work in the lab collapse on new, real-world fakes. "Is this real?" is becoming a question you literally can't answer by looking or listening.

The core bet

The bet was that you can spot a fake after it's made. But generators improve monthly, and detectors trained on yesterday's fakes fail on today's. The industry is quietly pivoting from catching the fake to proving the real, because detection has become too unreliable to trust.

What the numbers say

$25.6M
Stolen from engineering firm Arup (Hong Kong, Feb 2024) in a day — staff duped by a deepfake video call of the "CFO" and colleagues.
50–65%
Real-world accuracy of commercial detectors — versus 95–98% on lab benchmarks. A 20–60 point drop on each new generator.
3 seconds
Audio needed to clone a voice convincingly; voice-scam ("vishing") activity surged sharply, with six-figure CEO-voice thefts.
$2.77B
US losses to AI-assisted business-email/deepfake fraud in 2024 — average executive wire-transfer scam over $250k.

Detection can't generalize

The core failure: a detector trained on one deepfake pipeline fails on the next. Cross-dataset accuracy is ~50% or worse — a coin flip. So today's detector is already obsolete, and worse than useless when it gives false confidence that a new fake is real. That's why the field is turning to provenance — proving real content at the source instead of chasing fakes (see the next page).

What's newly valuable
Voice/video verification for banks and enterprises, and — increasingly — provenance signing over detection.
What's failing
Detection itself: it lags generators by 6–18 months and collapses on anything new.
The single biggest worry

A single convincing deepfake already stole $25M, unrecovered, and detectors now create false confidence while failing on the newest fakes. If accuracy keeps dropping as quality rises, "is this real?" becomes unanswerable — breaking trust in video evidence for fraud, courts, and journalism.

The surprising part

The fakers are simply winning. Detection tools that work today fail on next-generation deepfakes, and both making and detecting fakes run on the same AI, so the two escalate together. The likely fix isn't a better detector — it's abandoning detection for provenance, proving the real rather than chasing the fake.

In one sentence

Deepfake generators now beat detectors so reliably that the industry is pivoting from spotting fakes to proving originals — because "is this real?" can no longer be answered by looking.
