Group D · The lock itself

Right to repair — the fight to fix what you bought

Companies engineered products to be un-fixable. This is the rare movement actually cracking the lock.

As companies made repair the profit center, they also made it hard for anyone but themselves to do it — proprietary parts, glue instead of screws, software locks, and "parts pairing" (a replacement part won't work unless the maker's software authorizes it). The right-to-repair movement demands that makers provide parts, manuals, and tools — and it's starting to win.

The wins

  • Parts pairing is the frontier: Apple used serial-number locks so a third-party screen or battery triggers warnings or loses features.
  • State laws: New York (2022), then Minnesota, California, Colorado, Oregon (2023–24) passed right-to-repair acts; Oregon banned parts-pairing.
  • The EU passed a binding right-to-repair directive (2024).
  • Apple made a partial reversal (self-service repair, then supporting some laws).

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Repair monopolies mean higher prices, longer waits, and premature replacement — over half of Western households hold broken electronics that aren't worth fixing. E-waste is the fastest-growing waste stream on earth.

The surprising part

Companies deliberately engineer products to be un-repairable — glue, proprietary screws, parts-pairing software — so you must go to them or buy new. Even Apple's "self-service repair" is a half-measure that still requires serial-number authentication and omits key parts. But this is one of the rare forces actually cracking the lock-in economy: laws like Oregon's parts-pairing ban target exactly the loophole. The pushback is winning where it's written into law.

In one sentence

Right to repair in a nutshell

Right to repair is the rare regulatory push that breaks corporate lock-in — forcing makers to compete in the aftermarket instead of monopolizing it — but only where laws force them to.
