Group B · The machine, then the meter

Elevators — a razor-and-blades in every skyscraper

The elevator maker barely profits on installing it. The 25-year service contract is where the money hides.

Elevator makers (Otis, KONE, Schindler, TK Elevator) compete fiercely — even at a loss — to install an elevator, because the real profit is the maintenance contract that follows. Elevators must legally be inspected and serviced, and proprietary parts and software mean only the maker can do it. Once it's installed, you're locked in for the building's whole life.

The hidden goldmine

91% of profit
Share of Otis's segment operating profit that comes from service (2025) — despite being only ~60% of revenue. New-equipment margins are ~6–8%; service runs ~24%. Renewal rates top 90%.

Contracts run 25–30 years — the elevator's whole life. Independent service companies can't get the proprietary software, diagnostic tools, or spare parts.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Regulators are starting to target the lock: Israel's competition authority declared one elevator maker a monopolist in its own aftermarket (2025), and right-to-repair pressure could force makers to open their parts and software — collapsing the fat service margins.

The surprising part

Building owners never see the trick. They negotiate hard on the upfront equipment price (which barely covers cost), then accept whatever the service contract charges — not realizing the vendor already won by getting its foot in the door. The installation is really a 25-year option on nearly all of a building's elevator profit — and every firmware update deepens the moat.

In one sentence

Elevators in a nutshell

Elevator makers install at a loss to lock buildings into proprietary service contracts for life — turning a commodity machine into a 25-year captive goldmine hidden inside every skyscraper.
