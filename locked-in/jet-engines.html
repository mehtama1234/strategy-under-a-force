Group B · The machine, then the meter

Jet engines — buy the engine, rent 25 years of service

The engine is the razor. The decades of mandatory servicing are the blade.

Jet-engine makers (Rolls-Royce, GE Aerospace, Pratt & Whitney) sell engines to airlines cheap — sometimes below cost — and make the real money on decades of mandatory service, spare parts, and maintenance. Rolls-Royce pioneered "Power by the Hour": the airline pays a fee for every hour the engine flies, and Rolls handles all the upkeep.

The aftermarket is the business

~60–70%
Share of a jet-engine maker's lifetime profit that comes from the aftermarket (parts + service), not the engine sale. Engines run 20,000–30,000+ flight hours over 25–30 years — a quarter-century of locked-in service.

Only the maker (and its approved network) can legally service the engine — proprietary software, tooling, and liability lock out everyone else. A CFM56 costs ~$25–35M to build; the airline pays less upfront and the maker recoups it over 25 years of fees.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The maker bears the maintenance risk. If an engine proves unreliable (unexpected overhauls), airlines defect — so the manufacturer eats the cost of surprise repairs to protect the decades of guaranteed revenue.

The surprising part

The engine sale looks like the business; the real business is invisible. Rolls-Royce's stock is driven more by its service-contract backlog than its engine order book. When an airline signs a "TotalCare" deal, it's a $500M+ commitment over 25 years — locked in before the first engine ships. The buyer thinks it's buying a machine; it's really buying a quarter-century of non-negotiable servicing from one company.

In one sentence

Jet engines in a nutshell

Jet engines are the razor-and-blades at industrial scale: makers sell the engine cheap to win decades of captive, high-margin service revenue that dwarfs the sale.
