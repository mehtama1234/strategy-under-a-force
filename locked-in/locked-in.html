Group E · The big picture

Locked in — the whole force in one page

One idea ties every page together: the sale is the bait; the refill, the service, and the repair are the business.

Step back from the twelve stories and one idea explains them all. The sale is just the beginning. Companies sell the device cheap (or at a loss), then make the real money on the thing you can't do without afterward — the refill (ink, pods, blades, test strips), the service (jet engines, elevators), the spare part — and lock you so you can't buy it anywhere else.

The one idea: give away the razor, sell the blades forever

The whole force in a sentence

The one-time sale is the bait; the recurring, captive follow-on is the profit — and the whole game is making sure (with proprietary parts, chips, firmware, and "parts pairing") that you can't get it from anyone else. Value flows to whoever controls the refill and the repair; it drains from the customer, who owns the device but not the freedom to feed or fix it cheaply.

Where the frame holds — and where it bends

It's a fair description, not a blanket condemnation. Sometimes the model genuinely lowers the upfront cost and spreads payment over time; service contracts can add real value and reliability (a jet engine is engineered to run for decades). The abuse isn't the model — it's the forced lock: making it illegal or technically impossible to buy the refill elsewhere or to fix what you own.

The scale

50–80%
Share of lifetime value that comes from the aftermarket (parts + service) across many industries — for premium gear (surgical robots, jet engines), even more. The upfront sale is the hook; the recurring aftermarket is the business.

Who wins, who loses

Win

The makers who control the refill/repair (HP, Intuitive, Deere, Oracle, elevator & engine makers) — plus, increasingly, the independent repairers and regulators winning right-to-repair fights.

Lose

Customers locked into expensive consumables; patients on insulin; farmers who can't fix a tractor; anyone who "owns" a device but not the freedom to use it.

The biggest tension

The open question

Lock-in and repair monopolies vs. the right-to-repair movement and antitrust. Makers claim the lock is for quality or safety; the real reason is profit capture. The question: can enforcement (EU mandates, FTC settlements, state laws) break the lock without breaking the model? The inflection is happening now (2024–26), and the makers are still fighting.

The surprising part

You own the device but not the freedom to feed it, fix it, or fully use it — ownership got hollowed out into a perpetual license. It's the offline, physical cousin of the toll-booth economy: control the chokepoint that comes after the sale. Before software and firmware, owning a thing meant owning its repairs too. Now the property right (you own this) has been legally split from the use right (you can do what you want with it) — a recent, unusual, and quietly enormous change.

In one sentence

Locked in in a nutshell

The lock-in economy turns the device into the bait and the refill into the prison — hollowing out ownership itself — and right-to-repair is finally starting to break the lock, but only where laws force it.
