Printers & ink — priced like champagne, enforced by firmware

The printer is the bait. The ink — one of the most expensive liquids on earth — is the business.

HP and others sell printers cheap — sometimes at a loss — because the money is in the ink, famously one of the priciest liquids on the planet. And they lock you in: chips in cartridges, firmware "updates" that disable refilled or off-brand ink, and subscriptions that brick your cartridges if you stop paying.

The trap

~$1,500–3,000/gal
The effective price of printer ink — more than champagne or perfume — for something that costs pennies to make. Cartridges run 40–70% margin; the printer is sold near or below cost.
  • 2018: HP's "dynamic security" firmware update bricked third-party and refilled cartridges — printers suddenly rejected working ink, triggering lawsuits.
  • HP Instant Ink is a subscription that disables your ink if you cancel.
  • Chips in cartridges validate every print job and reject anything not on the maker's list.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

As the locks tighten toward cloud authentication, used and refurbished printers can be permanently bricked after warranty — millions of working machines pushed to landfill to force a repurchase.

The surprising part

Ink is priced like a luxury good not because it costs that much to make, but because the business model requires you to keep buying it. The cartridge chips, the timed firmware updates, the subscription — those aren't bugs, they're features, engineered to prevent escape. You don't really own the printer; you rent the right to feed it.

In one sentence

Printers & ink in a nutshell

Printers are sold cheap to trap you in expensive proprietary ink, enforced by chips and firmware that disable your freedom to refill — the original razor-and-blades, now defended with software.
