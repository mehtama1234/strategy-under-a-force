Group C · Health & tech lock-in

Medical-device consumables — a $2M robot with instruments that expire

The surgical robot is the razor. The instruments — programmed to die after ~10 uses — are the blades.

Medical-device makers place the big machine and make the real, recurring money on the proprietary consumables and service around it. The clearest case: Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci surgical robot. The hospital buys the ~$2M robot, but the profit engine is the instruments — which are chipped to expire after ~10 uses and must be rebought.

The razor-and-blades of the hospital

~60–65%
Share of Intuitive Surgical's revenue from recurring instruments and service, not robot sales. Instruments expire via an RFID chip after ~10 uses — by design, not wear — costing a busy OR $300k–500k/year.
  • CPAP machines (ResMed): cheap device, then $50–200/month in replacement masks, tubing, filters.
  • Dialysis (Fresenius, DaVita): new dialyzers + tubing every session, ~$7.5–13.5k/year per patient.
  • Surgeon retraining (50–200 hours) and integration make switching enormously costly.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Regulators (FTC, states, EU) are challenging "expiration by design" as anticompetitive lock-in, not medical necessity — the RFID instrument expiry is a business choice, not a safety rule. If right-to-repair reaches medicine, the consumable moat cracks.

The surprising part

The $2M robot isn't the money-maker — it's a beachhead. The profit is instruments engineered to fail after ~10 uses, forcing hospitals to rebuy the same $40–200 part dozens of times a year at ~$1,500 per instrument. A single OR can spend half a million a year on instruments alone. The device is the hook; the instrument is the extraction — a subscription disguised as a consumable.

In one sentence

Medical-device consumables in a nutshell

A $2M surgical robot is a razor-and-blades machine where the instruments expire by chip after ~10 uses — so the device is the bait and the endlessly-rebought consumable is the business.
