Group D · The lock itself

Software-locked hardware — the feature's installed; pay to switch it on

You bought a car with heated seats already wired in. Now pay $18/month to use them.

Increasingly, the hardware you buy has features physically present but disabled in software, which the maker will "unlock" for a fee or subscription. The seat is heated, the battery holds the range, the motor can go faster — but the maker keeps the switch. The marginal cost to them is zero, so it's pure recurring profit.

Pay to use what you own

  • BMW tried charging ~$18/month for heated seats already installed (2022) — big backlash, reversed in 2023.
  • Tesla sells software unlocks: extra battery range ($1,000–1,600), an "Acceleration Boost" ($2,000), Full Self-Driving — all capability the car already has.
  • Mercedes charges up to ~$1,200/year to unlock more acceleration.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Once makers prove people will pay to unlock built-in hardware, it becomes a template — every car, appliance, and device follows. Ownership quietly shifts from "you control the product" to "you license access to its capability," with no ability to resell or transfer the feature.

The surprising part

The economics are perverse: there's no R&D cost to unlock a feature already built in — it's pure recurring profit at infinite margin. And backlash only works when the feature is physical (heated seats you can see and feel). With software (acceleration, range), the lock feels abstract and people accept it — even though it's the same trick. BMW's reversal was a brand cost, not a strategy change: it still plans more software subscriptions.

In one sentence

Software-locked hardware in a nutshell

You can pay $50,000 for a car that already contains a heated seat and still owe $18/month to use it — because the maker holds the software key and it costs them nothing to let you in. (See also the subscription economy.)
