Coffee pods — the pod, not the coffee, is the product

Keurig sold the machine cheap and made money on the pods. Then it tried to DRM your coffee — and got burned.

Keurig and Nespresso sell the brewing machine cheap and make the money on the pods, which cost far more per pound of coffee than regular grounds. The machines accept only compatible capsules, so you're locked to one vendor. And when Keurig tried to enforce that lock, it backfired spectacularly.

The economics

~$40–50/lb
The effective cost of coffee in a K-Cup — 5–10× bulk grounds. A ~$0.30 pod sells for $0.50–0.75; the coffee inside is worth pennies. The pod, not the coffee, is the product.
  • 2014: Keurig's "2.0" machines used technology to block off-brand pods — DRM for coffee.
  • 2015: customers revolted, sales fell, and Keurig had to back down — removing the lock by 2018.
  • Nespresso used patents to protect its pods; when they expired, cheaper compatible pods flooded in.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Had the DRM stuck, Keurig would have won permanent lock-in — machines forcing you to buy only its expensive pods forever. The backlash killed it: customers punished the company hard enough to fold.

The surprising part

Keurig's DRM failure is the opposite of Apple's walled garden. Lock-in only works when customers believe the ecosystem justifies the premium (Apple) or don't notice the trap. Keurig customers saw the DRM as naked rent-extraction, not value, and voted with their wallets. The lesson: the moment a lock looks like "overpriced plastic with cheap beans inside," it dies.

In one sentence

Coffee pods in a nutshell

Keurig's razor-and-blades made the pod the product, not the coffee — and its 2014 attempt to DRM-lock the machine backfired so badly it became a case study in customer revolt against naked lock-in.
