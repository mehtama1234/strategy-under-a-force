The patterns

Line up everyone the lock touched, and one theme dominates: the sale is the bait, the refill is the business, and the lock is what makes it pay.

Each page asks: what did lock-in make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions repeat.

The one thing to take away

Sell the device cheap; make the money on the refill, service, or part you can't do without — and use proprietary parts, chips, firmware, and "parts pairing" to make sure you can't buy it anywhere else. You own the device but not the freedom to feed it or fix it. Ownership got hollowed out.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Give away the razor, sell the blades forever

The device is the bait; the recurring, captive refill is the business — often 50–80% of lifetime value. Ink, pods, blades, test strips, instruments, service hours.

inkjet enginesmedical devices
Pattern 02

The lock is what makes it pay

The margin only holds if you can't buy the refill elsewhere. Chips, firmware, patents, proprietary tools, and parts-pairing exist to prevent escape — the lock, not the product, is the moat.

firmwareparts pairingthe monopoly
Pattern 03

The aftermarket is the invisible business

Sell the engine, elevator, or robot at a loss to win decades of high-margin service. The order book looks like the business; the service backlog is the business.

jet engineselevatorssoftware
Pattern 04

You own it — but not the freedom to use or fix it

The property right (you own this) got split from the use right (you can do what you want). Own a tractor you can't repair, a car with features you must rent, a printer you can't feed with cheap ink.

tractorslocked hardwareright to repair
Pattern 05

The cruelest lock is where you can't say no

When the refill is survival (insulin) or the switch is impossible (core software), pricing power is near-total — makers raise prices almost without limit until public outrage or a body count forces a cap.

insulinenterprise software
Pattern 06

Zero marginal cost, pure recurring profit

Unlocking a built-in feature, or authorizing a repair, costs the maker nothing — so the fee is almost all profit. That's why software-locked features and expiry-by-chip are irresistible to sellers.

locked hardwareexpiry by chip
Pattern 07

Overreach triggers backlash

Keurig's coffee DRM and BMW's heated-seat subscription failed because the lock was too visible and felt like naked extraction. Lock-in works best when it's invisible or feels like value.

KeurigBMW seats
Pattern 08

Only law breaks the lock

The law recognized aftermarket monopoly in 1992 (Kodak), but the lock only cracks where right-to-repair laws and antitrust force it open — Oregon's parts-pairing ban, the EU directive, the Deere settlement.

Fifteen forces, one method

The point of the series

This is the fifteenth "strategy under a force" collection. Its signature: "the sale is the bait; the refill is the business." It's the physical cousin of the toll-booth economy (control the chokepoint that comes after the sale) and rhymes with the bank and loyalty economies (own the recurring relationship). Same method every time: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group by the answer, read the patterns.
