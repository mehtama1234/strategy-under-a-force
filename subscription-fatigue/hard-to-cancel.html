Group C · The company's counter-moves

Hard to cancel

Sign up in one click; cancel only after phone calls, hidden menus, and a maze of "are you sure?" — until the regulators noticed.

When customers try to leave, many services make it deliberately painful: one-click to sign up, but a gauntlet of phone calls, hidden buttons, retention nags, and multi-step mazes to cancel — the "roach motel" pattern. The bet was that enough people would give up or forget. Now regulators are fighting back: the US FTC's "Click to Cancel" rule and lawsuits against Amazon Prime and Adobe.

The core bet

The cancellation dark pattern is a deliberate asymmetry: frictionless to join, deliberately tedious to leave. It adds no value — it just converts one-time sign-ups into involuntary loyalty by betting customers give up. That bet is now legally and reputationally toxic.

Friction stopped being clever

The real threat isn't one lawsuit — it's the cascade. Once Amazon's cancellation maze went public, customers recognized the same pattern everywhere and regulators saw a playbook being copied. Enforcement against one big player makes the liability obvious to all, so companies that invested in "friction engineering" now face both legal costs and a customer backlash as the practice turns culturally toxic.

Who ends up ahead

Services that make leaving easy and advertise it — they build trust and lose fewer customers to sheer frustration.

Who's exposed

Early movers who engineered cancellation mazes — now facing lawsuits, fines, and brand damage.

The single biggest worry

The friction playbook turned retention into a hidden tax on trapped customers, and regulators across the US, UK, EU, and Australia are now criminalizing exactly that inertia. Companies that built retention on making cancellation painful must reverse it publicly — an admission that they were doing it on purpose.

The surprising part

The twist is that easy cancellation actually reduces churn. Customers who stay because they chose to don't rage-quit or trash you online, and those who leave don't badmouth you. Adobe's subscription backlash pushed creatives to rivals like Affinity; meanwhile services that made leaving frictionless found the regulatory win and the business win converged — trust keeps more people than a maze does.

In one sentence

Regulators decided "easy in, impossible out" wasn't clever retention but a hidden tax on trapped customers — and made cancellation symmetry the law, forcing services to compete on value instead of friction.
