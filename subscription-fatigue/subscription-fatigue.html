Group E · The big picture

Subscription fatigue

The most valuable business model of the 2010s ran on people not noticing — and then they noticed.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. Turning a one-time sale into a recurring monthly bill became the most prized business model of the 2010s — Wall Street pays a premium for predictable revenue — so software, streaming, physical goods, apps, and even car features all became subscriptions. It worked beautifully at five subscriptions. By the mid-2020s the average household juggled a dozen or more, hit a wall, and started cancelling, sharing, and downgrading.

The core bet

The scarce, valuable thing changed. In the 2010s it was a new subscriber; in the 2020s it's a subscriber who doesn't cancel. Recurring revenue is still gold, but growth ran out — so retention replaced growth, and the whole game became defending churn.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Bundle-first platforms (Apple, Amazon, telecoms) that cut friction by burying services together; high-switching-cost SaaS (Adobe, enterprise tools); and genuinely sticky, high-engagement services that retain through value, not traps.

Losers

Pure-play streamers that leaned on sharing-friction and cancellation mazes; low-value standalone subscriptions with near-zero switching costs; and high-burn startups that bet on endless subscriber growth.

The single biggest worry

The model's profit lever is default inertia — a subscriber who stops thinking and just renews is gold. But that inertia is exactly what fatigue and regulators attack. Every dark pattern (buried cancel buttons, silent auto-renewal, "you're about to lose your account" nags) works beautifully until fatigue hits a tipping point, at which the customer cancels everything out of spite and never comes back — and regulators start criminalizing the inertia itself. So companies face a cruel paradox: the profit lever (default renewal) is precisely the thing that now carries legal and reputational risk.

The surprising part

The deep irony is that subscriptions were supposed to help consumers — access instead of ownership, rent the stream instead of buying DVDs, rent the software instead of a pricey license. Instead the model's own logic drove a race to the bottom in cancellation friction and creeping prices, pushing people back toward owning: buy-once apps as a selling point, library apps, even a little piracy. The model's perfection created the conditions for its own backlash.

In one sentence

Subscription revenue is still the most valuable model in digital business — but the scarce thing shifted from the new subscriber to the subscriber who doesn't cancel, so profit now goes to those who defend churn with real value instead of friction.
