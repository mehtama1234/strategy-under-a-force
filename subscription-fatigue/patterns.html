The patterns

Line up every corner of the subscription boom and its fatigue, and the same handful of mechanics repeat — nearly all of them turning on one thing: default inertia.

Each page asks: what did the subscription boom — and its fatigue — make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions repeat.

The one thing to take away

Turning a sale into a monthly bill is the most valuable trick in modern business, so everyone did it — and the household drowned in quiet charges. The whole model runs on default inertia: the subscriber who stops noticing and just renews. Fatigue, and now regulators, attack exactly that. So the scarce, valuable thing flipped: it's no longer the new subscriber — it's the subscriber who doesn't cancel, and keeping them by real value (not friction) is the hard new game.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Wall Street pays a premium for a monthly bill

A dollar that recurs is worth many times a dollar that arrives once, so every company raced to turn its sale into a subscription — from software to heated seats.

Pattern 02

The model runs on forgetting

Small, automatic, scattered charges made the total invisible. The profit lived in the subscriptions people stopped noticing — until tracking tools revealed the bill.

Pattern 03

Grab cheap, then extract

Hook everyone with low introductory pricing, then raise prices, thin the tiers, and add ads — the classic land-grab-then-squeeze.

Pattern 04

Growth ends, extraction begins

Once new subscribers ran out, the moves turned inward: charge the password sharers, add ad tiers, hike prices. Every one is a signal the growth era is over.

crackdownssaturation
Pattern 05

Un-bundle, then re-bundle

Streaming killed the cable bundle for choice, then rebuilt it — five apps, ads, and a cable-sized bill — because bundles cut churn and restore pricing power.

full circlesuper-bundles
Pattern 06

Retention replaced growth

With new customers scarce and pricey, the whole game became not losing the ones you have — bundling, annual locks, win-back offers, "pause not cancel."

retention
Pattern 07

Friction retention turns toxic

Easy in, a maze on the way out worked — until fatigue and regulators (FTC "Click to Cancel") made deliberate friction a legal and reputational liability.

Pattern 08

The customer becomes the product again

Ad tiers let a service charge the subscriber and the advertiser for the same viewer — reversing the ad-free promise because attention pays more than the subscription.
