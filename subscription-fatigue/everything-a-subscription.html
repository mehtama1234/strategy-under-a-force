Group A · How subscriptions ate everything

Everything-as-a-subscription

Razors, meal kits, socks, apps — and then a monthly fee to switch on the heated seats already in your car.

Once software and streaming proved the model, everyone copied it. Razors (Dollar Shave Club), meal kits (HelloFresh, Blue Apron), beauty and clothing boxes (Birchbox, Stitch Fix), pet food (Chewy autoship), and every phone app got a monthly plan. Then it reached its logical extreme: BMW tried to charge a monthly fee to switch on heated seats already built into the car, and Mercedes charged for extra acceleration.

The core bet

The insight was that a monthly relationship keeps customers and locks in lifetime value in a way a one-time sale never does. So if software could charge $10/month instead of $50 once, why not toothbrushes, socks — and eventually features you already paid for? Profit moved from the product to the ongoing relationship.

Renting what you already own

The model inverts who owns the problem. When you bought a razor once, the maker had to make it good enough to justify the price. Now it owns the retention problem: if the blade is mediocre, you cancel. For subscription boxes the novelty wore off; for meal kits the churn was brutal; and for car features that were already installed, charging monthly felt like theft — which is exactly why BMW's experiment flopped.

Who ends up ahead

Sticky, genuinely useful autoship models (Chewy) — and the billing platforms (Stripe, Recurly) that made it all trivial.

Who's exposed

Novelty subscriptions with weak value (boxes, meal kits), and any brand that turned goodwill into a monthly nag.

The single biggest worry

The model turns every product into a game of extraction: how long can we keep them before they notice the value isn't there? For low-value or resented subscriptions — a forgotten box, a paywalled feature you already own — that noticing comes fast, and the backlash (and cancellation) is brutal.

The surprising part

The real winner of the subscription wave wasn't the sellers — it was the plumbing. Firms like Stripe and Recurly made one-click recurring billing a commodity, so every founder could slap a monthly fee on anything. That accidentally flooded the market: if the hard part is billing, not quality, everyone adds a subscription — and the glut is precisely what bred the fatigue.

In one sentence

Once billing became frictionless, everything from razors to heated seats became a monthly fee — turning product quality into a churn risk and flooding the market until fatigue set in.
