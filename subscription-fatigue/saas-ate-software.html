Group A · How subscriptions ate everything

Software became a service

Software used to be a thing you bought once and owned. Now it's rent you pay forever.

This is the model that started it all. Software used to be a one-time purchase — buy Office on a disc, own it forever. Then Adobe, Microsoft, and Salesforce turned it into a monthly or yearly subscription you rent for good. It made software companies vastly more valuable, because revenue became steady and predictable — and it taught every other industry to want the same magic.

The core bet

Vendors realized they could earn far more, far more predictably, by renting software month to month than by selling it once. A lumpy one-time sale became a steady recurring stream — and Wall Street rewards predictability even at the same annual revenue.

You rent forever; you own nothing

The model quietly flipped power to the vendor. You no longer own the software — your files, your license, your access all live at the vendor's pleasure. Prices can rise every year, and the old owned version eventually stops working on new machines, so there's no going back. Switching costs (exporting data, retraining) are high enough that vendors can turn the price up slowly without losing you.

Who ends up ahead

Software vendors and their investors, who traded a one-time sale for a permanent, predictable monthly stream.

Who's exposed

Customers, who lost ownership and now pay indefinitely for tools they can't fully leave.

The single biggest worry

The subscription can be cancelled next month, so the whole business hinges on retention — which means the vendor must keep earning the payment forever. But it also caged the customer: the moment you adopt a SaaS tool, you've signed up for an open-ended rent, and the vendor can slowly raise it because leaving is painful.

The surprising part

SaaS didn't just change software — it rewrote how every industry thinks about ownership. Once boards saw that recurring revenue was worth five-to-ten times more than a one-time sale, everyone wanted it: car features, fitness gear, appliances, even tractors went subscription. SaaS became the playbook for turning any product into a permanent tenant paying rent.

In one sentence

Renting software instead of selling it made vendors far richer and more valuable — because Wall Street pays many times more for a predictable monthly bill than for a one-time sale.
