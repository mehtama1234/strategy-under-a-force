Group C · The company's counter-moves

Password crackdowns

For a decade Netflix winked at password sharing to grow. Then it ran out of new customers and cracked down.

For years Netflix tolerated — even joked about — password sharing, because it fueled growth. Then, out of new subscribers to add in its big markets, it cracked down hard in 2023: shared users had to pay an extra fee or get their own account. It worked spectacularly, adding millions of subscribers, and every rival copied it. The crackdown was the moment the growth era openly ended and extraction began.

The core bet

Netflix had nearly exhausted new-subscriber growth in the West, so the crackdown wasn't about fairness or technology — it was the shift from buying growth with permissiveness to extracting profit from every household. Kill the free riders; convert or churn them; watch the stock climb.

The end of growth-era permissiveness

The crackdown revealed a brutal truth: Netflix had been living on borrowed growth, and now had to look inward. For a decade it winked at sharing because a shared account still beat cable or piracy. But once the big markets were saturated, the only lever left was to charge the people already watching — a clear signal that the days of growth-at-all-costs were over.

Who ends up ahead

Netflix and the rivals who copied it — unlocking millions of paying users hiding inside existing accounts.

Who's exposed

Password borrowers forced to pay or leave — and, longer term, the goodwill that made the brand feel generous.

The single biggest worry

The crackdown masked the real problem it exposed: there are only so many people on earth who will pay, and the big services have already found most of them. Converting freeloaders bought a one-time bump, but it doesn't create new demand — so once that latent revenue is captured, growth has nowhere left to go but price hikes and ads.

The surprising part

The surprise is that it worked without wrecking the business — the doom prediction that "people will just cancel and pirate" mostly didn't happen. That revealed password sharing was driven by convenience-friction, not poverty: charging $8 made people shrug and pay rather than set up their own account. It also proved the content lock-in was real enough that customers wouldn't walk.

In one sentence

The password crackdown was the exact moment streaming stopped chasing growth and started extracting — turning a tolerated freebie into revenue, and proving the growth era was over.
