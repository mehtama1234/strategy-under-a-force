Group D · The mechanism & the moves

What comes next

The model isn't dying — it's splitting into cheap-with-ads, pay-for-what-you-use, and all-in-one bundles.

As the pure monthly subscription hits its limit, the model is evolving rather than dying. Expect super-bundles (many services in one bill — basically cable again), usage-based pricing (pay for what you actually use, now spreading from cloud to AI), a small comeback for one-time purchases (sold as "no subscription required"), and ad-supported free tiers as the new default. The wheel keeps turning.

The core bet

Subscription fatigue isn't killing the model — it's fragmenting it into three tiers: a cheap ad-supported entry, pay-per-use for heavy users, and bundled all-in-one for convenience seekers. Each targets a different willingness to pay.

The wheel turns back toward the bundle

The likely next chapter is a return to the bundle — but with three competing oligopolies (telecoms, streaming super-packages, and usage-based cloud/AI) instead of one cable company, each exploiting a different willingness to pay. Usage-based pricing genuinely helps light users; bundles genuinely reduce friction; buy-once returns as a premium virtue. The model adapts to the fatigue by offering escape routes that keep you paying somewhere.

Who ends up ahead

Bundle aggregators (telecoms, big platforms) and usage-based providers who match price to real use.

Who's exposed

The "good deal" in the middle — squeezed between intrusive ad tiers, creeping usage rates, and lock-in bundles.

The single biggest worry

The death of the good deal. In a three-tier world the middle gets squeezed: ad tiers feel cheap and intrusive, usage-based punishes heavy users if rates creep, and bundles lock you into services you don't want for the one or two you do. Expect dynamic pricing that quietly tracks what you'll tolerate — the shift from "pay for what you use" to "pay what we think you'll bear."

The surprising part

Bundles may be worse for competition than the fragmentation people complained about. When every app was standalone, a newcomer could undercut on price or features; once the bundle is "the package," an app that isn't in it becomes invisible. So the fix for too-many-subscriptions — consolidation — risks creating a few mega-bundle oligopolies, which is exactly what could pull regulators back in to force unbundling.

In one sentence

Subscription fatigue didn't kill subscriptions — it returned us to the bundle, but split across ad-supported, pay-per-use, and super-bundle oligopolies, each mining a different willingness to pay.
