Group C · The company's counter-moves

The ad-tier pivot

After swearing for years they'd never have ads, the streamers quietly made ads their fastest-growing plan.

For years, ad-free was the whole pitch — the clean escape from cable's ad bombardment. Then Netflix and Disney+ launched cheaper ad-supported tiers (2022–23), and it turned out ads make more money per user than subscriptions in many cases. The ad tier became the fastest-growing plan, and Amazon simply made ads the default on Prime Video. The original promise is being quietly reversed.

The core bet

Streaming bet its identity on being ad-free. Then it realized the ad tier could charge the subscriber a low price and sell that same viewer to advertisers — earning more per user than ad-free. So the escape-from-ads promise became obsolete the moment growth slowed.

Monetize the same viewer twice

The leverage is that the service owns the audience, the content, and the ad inventory — so it can raise prices on both sides at once: charge the subscriber more for ad-free, and charge advertisers more for reach. Advertisers love it because they get an engaged viewer in a premium, brand-safe setting rather than algorithmic chaos, so they pay TV-like rates.

Who ends up ahead

The streamers, who now earn from the subscriber and the advertiser at once, and the ad-buyers who reach engaged viewers.

Who's exposed

Subscribers, who lose the ad-free promise; and the pricey ad-free tier, positioned mainly to push you to ads.

The single biggest worry

Subscribers hate ads — escaping them was the point — so the ad tier risks becoming a cheap "junk tier" that attracts low-value, churn-prone users. There's a hard ceiling on how many ads people tolerate (roughly 4–5 minutes an hour) before engagement drops and they cancel entirely, which could trap the business in a lower-revenue spiral.

The surprising part

Counterintuitively, ad-tier viewers often stay longer and watch more, because they treat the near-free service as low-stakes and binge. Advertisers pay premium rates to reach them, so streaming quietly discovered its real product was never the subscription — it was the audience, monetized twice.

In one sentence

Streaming realized its real product was the audience, not the subscription — so ads came back, letting services charge the subscriber and the advertiser for the very same viewer.
