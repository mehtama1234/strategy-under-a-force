Group D · The mechanism & the moves

Retention is everything

In the growth era you won by adding customers. In the fatigue era, the whole game is not losing the ones you have.

New customers used to be plentiful and cheap; now they're scarce and expensive. Companies learned that keeping an existing customer costs a fraction of finding a new one, so the entire playbook flipped from "sign people up" to "make them stay." That means bundling services together, annual plans, win-back offers, loyalty perks, making the product habitual, and "pause instead of cancel."

The core bet

With new customers scarce and pricey, the battle became keeping the ones you have — cutting churn. Since keeping a customer costs a fraction of acquiring one, a point of churn saved is worth more than a marketing campaign, and strategy reorganized around retention.

Keeping replaced getting

The tactics all aim at the same thing: make leaving harder or staying easier. Bundles raise switching costs; annual plans use commitment; "pause instead of cancel" catches people who'd otherwise walk; win-back offers chase the ones who left. In a saturated market, a point of retention is worth more than a wave of new sign-ups.

Who ends up ahead

Bundlers and high-value, sticky products (enterprise SaaS, Costco-style memberships) that retain through real worth.

Who's exposed

Low-value standalone subscriptions with near-zero switching costs — the first to be cancelled.

The single biggest worry

The retention playbook can work too well and become a negative-sum trap: bundling and cancellation mazes hold customers without making the product better, which trains people to expect bad faith and bolt the moment a rival offers an easier exit or genuinely better value. And win-back discounts plus deep bundles can quietly cost more than the customers are worth.

The surprising part

The obsession with retention can become a profitability trap: you spend more keeping customers (discounts, perks, support, bundles) than you earn from them, reshuffling revenue from new customers to old rather than creating value. The escape hatch is the Costco model — charge enough that the customers worth keeping actually pay for themselves — because otherwise retention is just organized decline.

In one sentence

With new customers scarce, keeping them became the whole game — but weaponizing retention through bundles and friction, instead of real value, leaves everyone worse off.
