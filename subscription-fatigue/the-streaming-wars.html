Group A · How subscriptions ate everything

The streaming wars

Netflix proved people would pay monthly for on-demand video — so every media giant piled in, and most lost billions.

Netflix showed that people would happily pay a monthly fee for on-demand video and cut the cable cord. So every media giant launched its own service — Disney+, Max, Peacock, Paramount+, Apple TV+ — and spent enormous sums on shows to grab subscribers, chasing growth over profit. Now the market is saturated, growth has stalled, and most of these services lose money or barely break even.

The core bet

Each media company bet that owning its content and its own service would let it capture streaming growth and defend against cord-cutting. The flawed assumption: that more services chasing subscribers would simply grow the pie, rather than fragment the audience and destroy everyone's pricing power.

A race to zero profit dressed as a land grab

The fatal miscalculation was thinking anyone could copy Netflix by throwing money at content while keeping prices low. But Netflix had a 15-year head start and 250M+ subscribers to spread costs across. When everyone launched at once at similar prices, they fragmented the audience so badly that no one but Netflix could gather enough subscribers to cover a $15–20B annual content bill — collectively destroying pricing power while building unsustainable losses.

Who ends up ahead

Netflix (the scaled survivor) and, eventually, the disciplined players who cut spending and raised prices.

Who's exposed

The legacy studios that launched late and cheap, and the creators whose projects got axed to cut costs.

The single biggest worry

The whole land-grab assumed the streaming market would keep expanding. Instead it saturated, and the money-losing services now face a brutal choice: keep spending to compete and bleed cash, or cut back and lose subscribers. Either way the golden growth era is over, and profitability-per-subscriber — not subscriber count — is suddenly the only metric that matters.

The surprising part

The wars made watching cheaper for a while, but made content scarcer for everyone who makes it: studios axed mid-tier and prestige projects to fund a few blockbuster franchises, creators faced cancelled contracts, and viewers who wanted to see everything now needed five subscriptions. The scarce resource turned out not to be content but profit — and attention.

In one sentence

Every media giant copied Netflix's subscription and spent itself toward bankruptcy chasing subscribers — until profit-per-subscriber, not growth, became the only thing that mattered.
