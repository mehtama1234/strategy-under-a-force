Group E · The big picture

The staffing economy

When labor became the bottleneck, the middleman between worker and work became the growth business.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. When an aging workforce and skills gaps turned labor into a chronic bottleneck, "getting the work done" became a $500B+ global business — not in wages to workers, but in fees to the middleman. Companies can't find the workers they need, so they do one of three things: rent workers (agencies, travel nurses, gig apps), outsource the whole function (PEOs, BPO, recruiting), or automate the work away with AI.

The core bet

In every path an intermediary sits between the worker and the work and keeps a durable cut — 20–40% of wages for temp staffing, a few percent of payroll for PEOs, 30–50% of revenue for BPO. Labor scarcity flipped the power structure: the worker gained leverage and wages rose, but the middleman rose faster, capturing most of the gain while shifting the risk onto the worker.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Big staffing firms and travel-nurse agencies; PEO and BPO leaders; gig platforms; automation and AI-agent vendors; and recruiters who moved up into strategy.

Losers

Direct-hire workers whose wages lag their scarcity; temp, gig, and contract workers with no benefits or security; in-house HR; small businesses locked into vendors; and workers in the roles AI is now automating away.

The single biggest worry

Workers won a Pyrrhic victory: the shortage gave them real wage gains and leverage (2021–23), but the intermediary — staffing firm, PEO, gig platform, BPO — captured 30–50% of it, leaving a smaller real uplift than their scarcity deserved. Worse, the rise of temp and contract work shifted all the risk onto the worker — no healthcare, no pension, no security — while the company outsourced the headache. And now AI erodes the premise itself: the very shortage that made staffing a growth business is being answered by automation that eliminates the low-wage, hard-to-fill roles staffing profited on most.

The surprising part

The staffing economy isn't a workaround — it's a fundamental restructuring of how companies relate to labor. The 20th-century firm built talent and kept it for decades; the staffing economy inverted that, so companies now expect labor to be rented, temporary, and externally sourced, freeing them to exit the "talent business" entirely. Like offshoring manufacturing, it reveals a dirty secret: the intermediary doesn't add skill — it adds flexibility, and extracts rent. It's a risk transfer — companies pay for the freedom not to care about people, and workers pay for the freedom to work.

In one sentence

When labor became scarce, the middleman — staffing firm, PEO, gig platform, BPO — became the business, capturing 20–50% of the value the shortage created while shifting all the precarity onto the worker.
