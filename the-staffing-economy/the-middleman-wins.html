Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The middleman wins

Whenever labor is scarce, the intermediary that connects worker to work keeps a durable cut of both sides.

This is the core mechanism of the whole collection: whenever labor is scarce, the intermediary that connects the worker to the work — the staffing agency, the PEO, the gig platform, the recruiter — captures a durable cut. They own the relationship and the matching, so both sides depend on them. The company pays a markup; the worker takes a haircut; the middleman keeps the spread and scales it across millions of placements.

The core bet

When labor is scarce, the bottleneck is matching — finding the right person fast. The intermediary owns that match and the ongoing relationship with both sides, captures the spread between what the company pays and what the worker gets, and keeps nearly all of it because it touches both sides of a fragmented market.

What the numbers say

40–70%
a staffing agency's markup on the bill rate — a $25/hr temp often takes home $14–17; the agency keeps $8–11.
20–30%
a gig platform's take of every fare or task; a recruiter's fee is 20–30% of first-year salary.
$50–150/mo
a PEO's per-employee admin fee — recurring, regardless of the client's profit.
owns the data
the intermediary holds the match data, reliability scores, and wage signals — which compound and lock both sides in.
low capital
no inventory or plants — one recruiter or algorithm manages hundreds of placements; the model scales cheaply.

Rent on both sides

The intermediary's edge is data and switching cost: it knows which workers fit which clients, so a worker can't easily move to another agency without losing their placement history, and a client can't quickly build internal recruiting. The relationship locks in, and the spread — historically high — scales across volume with almost no capital, which is why intermediation is the winning position in a labor shortage.

Who ends up ahead

The intermediary — capturing a 20–70% spread that scales with volume and locks in both worker and employer.

Who's exposed

Both sides pay: the employer the markup, the worker the haircut — and neither can easily route around the match.

The single biggest worry

The whole position depends on labor staying scarce and fragmented. If labor gets abundant, workers find work directly and employers recruit in-house; if transparent wage databases and worker portals cut the matching friction, the moat collapses and the business becomes a low-margin logistics play. The rent lives entirely on continued scarcity and lock-in.

The surprising part

The worker rarely sees the markup as a loss — they see the job the agency or app brought them as a win, because it solved their search problem. Both sides feel they got a deal even though the spread is historically high; the rent extracts silently as margin rather than as a visible cost. That invisibility is the stickiness.

In one sentence

The intermediary wins by owning the match between scarce labor and scattered employers — capturing a durable 20–70% spread that scales with volume and locks in both sides through switching cost and convenience.
