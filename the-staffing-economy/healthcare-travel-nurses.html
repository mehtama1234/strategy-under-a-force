Group A · Renting the workers

Travel nurses

Hospitals rent nurses on 13-week contracts at 2–3× staff pay — and can't get off the treadmill.

Hospitals face chronic nurse shortages, so they rent nurses through travel-nursing agencies (AMN Healthcare, Aya, Cross Country) that place them on 13-week contracts far from home at premium pay. During COVID (2021–22), travel-nurse pay exploded and agencies boomed; it has cooled since but stayed structurally elevated. Hospitals became dependent on the escape hatch.

The core bet

Hospitals outsourced their chronic staffing shortage to agencies, shifting from long-term employment commitments to short-term rentals. The agency captures the wage gap between what a staff nurse earns and what a hospital pays for temporary coverage — while the hospital avoids fixed benefit and training costs.

What the numbers say

$5–10k/wk
travel-nurse pay at the 2021–22 peak (vs ~$2–2.5k/wk for staff nurses); now ~$2.5–4k, still elevated.
~$20–25B
the US healthcare staffing market by 2022–23; travel nurses are ~5–8% of acute-care nursing.
~$5B
AMN Healthcare's revenue peak (2022), which fell as utilization normalized — a very cyclical business.
25–40%
the agency's markup — the spread between what the hospital pays and what the nurse receives.
200k–450k
the projected US nurse shortfall by 2025, as boomers age and bedside nurses leave for management.

The crutch that deepens the wound

Faced with COVID, hospitals could invest in permanent nurses (bonuses, retention, training) or rent expensive temps. Most rented. Now the permanent shortage is deeper than it would have been — no new cohorts trained, and core staff demoralized watching travelers earn 2–3× for the same work — so hospitals are structurally locked into the agencies even as pay normalizes.

Who ends up ahead

Travel-staffing agencies, which own the supply exactly when hospitals are most desperate.

Who's exposed

Hospitals (fragile, hostage to surge pricing), staff nurses (demoralized), and continuity of patient care.

The single biggest worry

Care quality and continuity suffer — travelers rotate every 13 weeks and build no institutional knowledge — and hospitals become hostage to agencies that can hold them up on price during crises, then exit regions when demand cools, leaving units suddenly understaffed and forced to pay even more to refill.

The surprising part

The travel-nurse boom didn't solve the shortage — it masked it. By renting temps instead of building a pipeline, hospitals trained no new cohorts and demoralized their base, so the agencies became not a safety valve but a permanent crutch that makes the underlying problem worse — and they own the escape hatch.

In one sentence

Hospitals mortgaged permanent staffing for temporary coverage, and now can't exit a model that deepens the shortage while enriching the agencies that own the way out.
