Group E · The big picture

The cohort economy

When a brand becomes part of who you are, you stop being a customer and become a member who won't churn on price.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one idea explains them all. Identity, belonging, and community became the deepest moat in business. When a brand, creator, or product becomes part of who someone is — a Harley rider, a Swiftie, a CrossFitter, a Soho House member, a Bored Ape holder — they stop being a customer and become a member who won't churn on price and who markets to others for free. Belonging beats points, attention, and subscriptions, because you don't cancel your identity.

The core bet

The scarce, valuable thing modern business chases is no longer attention or even a subscription — it's belonging. A brand that captures identity gets free marketing, price insulation, and stickiness that outlasts product quality. But the same power is a trap: tie your business to an identity and the tribe owns you as much as you own it.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Identity-first brands (Harley, Apple, Patagonia); self-organizing fandoms (Swifties, ARMY, K-pop) that market for free; capped clubs and creators who own the direct relationship; and community-led products where members recruit members.

Losers

Brands that betray the tribe's values (Bud Light); scarcity plays once access normalizes (Clubhouse, most NFTs); over-scaled clubs that dilute the aura (Soho House); and points programs any rival can out-bid.

The single biggest worry

The identity moat is strongest at small scale and dies at large scale. Harley and Taylor Swift keep loyalty high by protecting scarcity; Soho House scaled globally and the exclusivity evaporated. The paradox: you build the moat by saying "this community is for us," then the business model demands "let's sell it to everyone," and the moat collapses. Bud Light showed the inverse danger — betray the tribe's core values even once, and the switching cost flips from infinity (I won't leave my identity) to zero (this was never my tribe).

The surprising part

Tribes police and organize themselves more ruthlessly than any brand could. ARMY built global streaming strategies without being asked; Swifties spent $5B without permission. That's why the parasocial bond is the engine, not a bug — fans feel in a relationship, so they self-organize to protect and grow the tribe. But it inverts power: a single misaligned decision by leadership can trigger coordinated retaliation at the same scale, because the machinery that markets for you for free runs equally well in reverse.

In one sentence

Belonging is the only durable competitive moat — until the tribe decides you don't belong to them, at which point the free marketers become the mob you united against.
