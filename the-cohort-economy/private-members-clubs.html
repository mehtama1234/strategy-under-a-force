Group B · Membership & the club

Private members' clubs

You're not paying for the cocktails — you're paying to be vetted and surrounded by other vetted people.

Soho House, and a wave of newer clubs (Zero Bond, San Vicente, Casa Cipriani, NeueHouse), sell membership in a curated community of "interesting people." You pay thousands a year not for the drinks but for who else is in the room and the feeling of being chosen. It's the business of manufactured exclusivity and belonging — and it runs into a brutal paradox.

The core bet

The space is secondary; the vetted people and the feeling of being selected are the product. Membership dues, not food and drink, are the business — and the whole thing depends on saying no to enough people that being in feels rare.

Growth is incompatible with exclusivity

The core business is a paradox: to justify a big valuation and its rent, the club must keep adding rooms and members — but every new member makes belonging feel less special. A $4,000 membership means nothing if everyone in your industry is already inside. The waitlist that was a moat becomes proof the company is desperate to fill occupancy. Soho's hardest problem wasn't recruiting; it was that existing members churned because the place stopped feeling exclusive.

Who ends up ahead

Small, capped clubs that keep saying no — and, briefly, the operators who scale before the aura fades.

Who's exposed

Members once the aura dilutes; and any club torn between exclusivity and the occupancy its landlords and investors demand.

The single biggest worry

The exclusivity that is the product is mathematically incompatible with the growth the business model demands. Scale to satisfy investors and debt, and you dilute the aura; stay truly small, and you can't grow revenue. Going public forced Soho into scale and transparency — which exposed the tension at the heart of the model.

The surprising part

These clubs barely make money on food and drink; the real product is dues plus the feeling of being chosen. But the cruel bind is that they must also keep occupancy high to cover luxury-real-estate rent — so they're forced to admit the very people whose admission destroys the scarcity. You can't un-commodify belonging once you've sold too much of it.

In one sentence

Soho House commoditized belonging just enough to become a company — but the growth that made it a company is exactly what made the belonging worthless.
