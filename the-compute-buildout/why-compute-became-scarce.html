Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why compute got scarce

For fifty years computing got cheaper every year. AI reversed it and made it the constraint again.

This is the mechanism. For decades chips got cheaper and faster on a predictable schedule (Moore's law), so software scaled almost for free. AI broke that overnight: models need exponentially more compute, the gains from more compute kept paying off ('scaling laws'), and chips, power, and buildings all have long lead times. For the first time in a generation, raw compute became a hard physical bottleneck — and scarcity is what hands power to whoever owns it.

The core bet

AI turned compute from an abundant, ever-cheaper thing into a scarce physical bottleneck — and scarcity, not abundance, is what gives its owners power.

Atoms gate bits again

Before AI, adding a million users cost almost nothing — the same code on more servers. AI flipped it: the model itself is the constraint, and the model needs silicon, power, and buildings that take years to make. Investors who'd forgotten that atoms matter got a hard reminder that physical bottlenecks win in the end.

The single biggest worry

If scaling stops paying off — if 10x more compute yields only 10% better models — the scarcity premium evaporates and the entire case for hoarding chips collapses. That quiet fear of a 'scaling wall' hangs over the whole boom.

The surprising part

The historical irony is sharp: for half a century, cheap and abundant compute was the engine of the tech economy, so much so that everyone assumed it away. AI made computing the constraint again for the first time since the 1980s — and rediscovered that the digital world still runs on very physical things.

In one sentence

AI's hunger for compute is growing far faster than the world can build chips and power to feed it, so for the first time since the 1980s, raw compute is scarce and valuable.
