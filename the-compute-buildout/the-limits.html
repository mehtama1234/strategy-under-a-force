Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The limits

The bubble question, the power ceiling, and the trap of borrowing against chips that decay.

This is the skeptical piece. The build-out faces serious risks: the spend is far ahead of AI revenue (with circular financing that echoes the dot-com telecom bust); power and permitting cap how fast it can grow; GPUs depreciate fast, so the assets lose value quickly; and if a cheaper method appears — as the DeepSeek efficiency shock hinted in early 2025 — the need for so much hardware could drop suddenly.

The core bet

The build-out assumes demand and scaling keep rising and hardware stays scarce. Each of those assumptions could break — and betting on the boom is betting none of them do.

A breakthrough that makes AI cheaper could hurt the boom

Here's the paradox at the heart of the risk: a method that makes AI training 10x cheaper would destroy the infrastructure thesis — cratering demand for the exact hardware everyone bet on, turning a scarce-resource play into an overcapacity one. The assets everyone's borrowing against assume scarcity; abundance kills the model overnight.

The single biggest worry

A demand or efficiency shock could leave hundreds of billions of hardware running at low utilization with debt still owed and collateral worth a fraction of its price — the exact way the telecom fiber boom ended.

The surprising part

The deep irony is that progress is the danger. In most industries a big efficiency win is pure good news; here it could puncture the whole build-out, because the boom is priced for scarcity. Everyone is betting that AI stays expensive to run — and quietly terrified that it won't.

In one sentence

The compute build-out is a bet that you must keep spending hundreds of billions on scarce hardware — but an efficiency win or demand slowdown could leave it worth a fraction of its price, with the debt still due.
