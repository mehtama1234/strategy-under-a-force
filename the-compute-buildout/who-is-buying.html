Group C · The money machine

Who's actually buying

A few money-losing labs, some cautious enterprises — and now whole nations treating compute like oil.

The build-out only pays off if buyers keep buying, and the demand comes from three buckets: the frontier model labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Meta) burning cash on training; enterprises adopting AI cautiously; and — increasingly — nations building 'sovereign AI' so they don't depend on U.S. clouds. Understanding how durable each of those is is the key strategic question.

The core bet

The build-out is only worth it if the buyers keep buying — and demand is broadening from a few labs to enterprises and whole nations that want their own AI.

Nations now treat compute like a strategic reserve

Sovereign demand behaves differently from the rest: Gulf states and India will keep buying even as prices rise, because the alternative is being locked into foreign platforms forever. That makes national buyers less price-sensitive and more durable than fickle enterprise demand — and turns the real contest into 'who controls the fabs and supply chains.'

The single biggest worry

Most of today's demand comes from a handful of labs that don't yet make money, sustained by one-time mega-investments from the same giants selling them compute. If those labs slow, hit a scaling wall, or get absorbed, the buyer base thins fast.

The surprising part

The strategic shift is that countries started treating AI compute like oil — a reserve that confers economic and military leverage. That's more durable than enterprise buying, but it also means a nation will outbid any private buyer when national security is at stake, reshaping who ultimately gets the chips.

In one sentence

Compute demand is real but fragile — a few labs on borrowed money, enterprises still early, and nations in an arms race — so the build-out pays off only if the labs keep spending and no one country locks the rest out.
