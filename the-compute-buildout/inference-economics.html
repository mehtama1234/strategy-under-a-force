Group C · The money machine

Does it pay for itself?

The whole build-out rests on one question: can running AI for real users earn more than it costs?

There are two kinds of AI compute: training (building a model — a huge one-time cost) and inference (running it for users — an ongoing cost that grows with usage). As AI products roll out, inference becomes the bigger, recurring bill. The strategic question underneath the entire boom: can the revenue from AI products cover the enormous compute cost? Reasoning models made each query far more expensive.

The core bet

The build-out only pays off if inference — running AI for real users — earns more than it costs. So the whole boom rests on AI products actually covering their compute bill.

Falling costs are both the threat and the whole thesis

The same force that crushes today's margins is what keeps the long bet alive: a given AI capability gets ~10x cheaper every year, so today's expensive model becomes tomorrow's cheap commodity. The real play isn't 'profit now' — it's 'spend huge now to own the infrastructure when everyone needs AI and it's dirt cheap.' But that only works if you survive the losses your rivals don't.

The single biggest worry

If inference costs stay high or prices collapse in a race to the bottom, the gap between spend and revenue never closes — and the industry will have poured hundreds of billions into capacity for a market that doesn't pay for itself.

The surprising part

The cruel wrinkle is that success can mean bigger losses: if each query loses money, more usage digs a deeper hole. So the boom is a race between two exponentials — falling cost per token versus rising usage and reasoning overhead — and nobody yet knows which one wins.

In one sentence

The entire AI boom is a bet that inference gets cheap and popular enough to pay for the build-out — but today, running AI for users still costs more than users pay.
