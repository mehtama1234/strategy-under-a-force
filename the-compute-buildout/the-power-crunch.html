The power crunch

The real bottleneck to AI isn't chips — it's electricity, and the grid can't scale fast enough.

Data centers need staggering amounts of power, and the grid can't supply it fast enough. So tech companies are signing giant electricity deals, restarting nuclear plants, ordering small reactors, and building their own generation. Electricity — not silicon — is becoming the real constraint on where AI actually gets built, and whoever secures gigawatts controls the map.

The core bet

The scarce thing shifted from chips to power. Whoever can lock down gigawatts of reliable electricity decides where AI gets built — the AI race is really an electricity race.

Tech companies became energy companies

Microsoft, Google, and Amazon stopped being customers of the grid and started owning or contracting power directly — nuclear restarts, reactor orders, their own wind and solar — signing long deals that quietly lock everyone else out. The energy sector didn't disrupt tech; tech reached down and started eating the energy sector.

The single biggest worry

Power simply can't scale as fast as buildings go up. Transmission, substations, and generation take years; demand rises in months. The result is half-built data centers waiting on power, higher electricity bills for ordinary customers, and regions where the grid becomes the hard ceiling on growth.

The surprising part

The twist is who the AI giants turned into. To feed their models they became utilities — reviving shuttered reactors, buying turbines, contracting gigawatts — because the one thing money couldn't instantly buy was electrons. The bottleneck on the digital future turned out to be the century-old power grid.

In one sentence

Electricity, not chips, now gates where AI gets built, and whoever can secure gigawatts of reliable power wins.
