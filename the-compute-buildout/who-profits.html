Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

In a gold rush, the sure money is in shovels — and most miners never strike gold.

This is the follow-the-money piece. In the AI gold rush, the surest profits so far go to the picks-and-shovels: Nvidia (chips), TSMC (making them), the memory and networking suppliers, the power and cooling companies, and the data-center landlords — not the AI application companies, many of which lose money on every query. It's the oldest pattern in every boom.

The core bet

The reliable money goes to whoever sells the scarce inputs — chips, power, memory, buildings — while the AI app-makers spending that money may or may not ever turn a profit.

The safest AI bet was to own no AI product

Over the past three years, the surest way to profit from AI was to have zero AI product and just own the pipeline — the chip, the fab, the power, the building — the thing everyone had to buy regardless of whether their own AI worked. That reverses the usual tech rule where the app layer captures the value; this time the infrastructure layer captured it first.

The single biggest worry

If AI applications never generate revenue that justifies the compute spend, the whole chain that profited from selling to them eventually contracts. Nvidia, TSMC, and the power companies are rich only because someone is desperately buying — the moment that stops, the edifice shrinks.

The surprising part

The pattern is as old as 1849: in a gold rush the shovel-sellers get rich and most miners break even or lose. Nvidia and the chipmakers, power firms, and data-center owners are today's saloon and pickaxe sellers — profiting handsomely from miners who may never find the gold.

In one sentence

In the AI gold rush, the chipmakers, power companies, and data-center owners are getting rich the way pickaxe sellers did in 1849 — by selling to miners who might never strike gold.
