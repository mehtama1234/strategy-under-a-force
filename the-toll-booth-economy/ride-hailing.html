Group C · The gig & travel tax

Ride-hailing — a rising, hidden cut of the fare

The driver owns the car, the gas, and the risk. Uber owns the software — and a growing, invisible slice.

Uber and Lyft sit between riders and drivers and take a "take rate." It started around 20–25%, but drivers report it has crept much higher — often 40–50%+ on individual rides — while the driver, who provides the car, labor, gas, and risk, keeps a shrinking share. And the split is now hidden.

The creep, and the trick

20% → 40–50%
Uber's effective take rate over time (2012 to now) once stacked service and booking fees are counted. Median driver pay is often ~$12–14/hour before expenses.
"Upfront pricing" decoupled the two sides

Around 2020–22, Uber and Lyft stopped showing drivers the rider's fare. Now they charge the rider one price and pay the driver from a separate, hidden formula — pocketing the spread. Riders and drivers see different numbers for the same trip.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

As the companies chase Wall Street margins, they can squeeze driver pay without losing supply — millions chase gig income, and there's no real "escape hatch" (Lyft uses the same mechanics). Regulators are always a step behind.

The surprising part

"Upfront pricing" is the cleverest part of the toll: by showing the rider and driver different numbers, the platform can raise its cut without raising the rider's fare — it just quietly pays the driver less. Passengers often think the driver keeps 75%+; the driver may keep ~50–55%, and the gap vanished into the platform's pocket, invisible to everyone.

In one sentence

Ride-hailing in a nutshell

Uber and Lyft quietly grew from ~20% to 40–50% effective cuts by hiding the split between what the rider pays and what the driver gets — turning gig workers into perpetually squeezed suppliers.
