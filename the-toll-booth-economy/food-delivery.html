Food delivery — the app keeps more per meal than the kitchen

The restaurant cooks the food, pays the rent, and takes the risk. The app takes the biggest slice.

DoorDash, Uber Eats, and Grubhub charge restaurants 15–30% per order (effectively 30–40% with marketing and processing fees). Most restaurants run 5–10% margins — so a single delivery order can turn a profit into a loss. The restaurant does the cooking, labor, and rent; the app takes the toll.

The squeeze

$1–2 vs $2–6
On a $20 order, the restaurant nets maybe $1–2 while the app takes $2–6 in commission — the platform often keeps more per meal than the kitchen that made it. The driver, meanwhile, earns a fraction.
  • DoorDash holds ~60–67% US market share; opting out means losing the customers who order via app.
  • Cities capped commissions at ~15% during COVID; SF and NYC made caps permanent.
  • Restaurants in heavy-delivery markets are ~23% more likely to close within two years.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Restaurants can't opt out without losing sales, because most diners now order through apps. It's a market failure: monopoly-ish power forces acceptance of an unprofitable deal.

The surprising part

Both productive ends of the meal — the kitchen and the driver — are squeezed below their real cost, while the middle (the app), which cooks nothing and drives nothing, captures the most value. The restaurant does the food, the driver does the labor, and the platform, sitting in between, takes the toll on both.

In one sentence

Food delivery in a nutshell

Delivery apps charge restaurants ~30%+ that most can't refuse, capture more profit per meal than the kitchen, and underpay the drivers who do the work — the middle eats both ends.
