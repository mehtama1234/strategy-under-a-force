Group A · The storefront tax

Steam & game stores — 30% that holds even against 12% rivals

A competitor offered less than half the cut. Steam still grew. Here's why.

Valve's Steam takes ~30% of every PC game sale — the same cut PlayStation, Xbox, and Nintendo take on their stores. In 2018 the Epic Games Store launched at 12% to undercut it. Steam didn't shrink; it grew. The reason is the deepest lesson in the whole collection: lock-in beats price.

The toll and the moat

~30% / ~$3M
Steam's cut (25%/20% for big sellers), and Valve's revenue per employee — among the highest of any tech company, from a toll on a market it dominates (~75% of PC digital sales).

Epic spent ~$300M on exclusives to break in and peaked at ~5–8% share. Steam's grip held: your game library, your friends, your achievements all live there.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Dominance makes Valve a regulatory target (UK, EU). Most of its costs are sunk, so a forced fee cut to 20% or lower would slash its extraordinary margins — its whole model rests on the 30% holding.

The surprising part

Epic offered developers a cut less than half Steam's — and gamers still didn't move, because gamers don't pay the 30%; developers do. The price to the player is the same on both stores. So the switching cost (library, friends, achievements) overwhelms the developer's saving. The toll survives not because it's fair or cheap, but because no one can realistically leave the platform.

In one sentence

Steam & game stores in a nutshell

Steam's 30% persists despite a 12% rival because leaving the biggest library and friend network in PC gaming is harder than any price cut — proof that lock-in, not fairness, sustains the toll.
