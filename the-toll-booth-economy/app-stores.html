Group A · The storefront tax

App stores — 30% of every sale, for a gateway they control

Apple and Google take a third of what developers earn, for a door only they own.

Apple and Google take roughly 30% of every app sale and in-app purchase (15% for smaller developers). The developer builds the app and takes the risk; the platform runs the store and skims a third — at near-zero cost. It's one of Apple's most profitable lines, and developers can't reach iPhone users any other way.

The toll

~30% / ~$20B
The standard cut (since the 2008 App Store launch), and Apple's rough annual App Store take — at 80%+ margins. A pure software tax the size of a Fortune 500 division.
  • Epic v Apple (2020): Fortnite pulled after trying to bypass Apple's payment; years of lawsuits.
  • EU Digital Markets Act (2024): forced Apple to allow other app stores and payment in Europe — met with grudging, complicated compliance.
  • South Korea, Japan, the UK, and the US DOJ all pushed back.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

You can't build a sustainable business if a third of revenue goes to a toll-taker who bears no product risk and faces no competition — iOS has no alternative store (until the EU cracked it open). It prices out indie developers and concentrates the market toward big players.

The surprising part

Apple fights to keep the 30% ferociously — spending hundreds of millions on legal defense and building convoluted EU "compliance" schemes with new fees rather than yield. It treats this as an existential fight, not a minor revenue line — which quietly reveals how much more valuable the toll is than the "security and discovery" it claims to pay for.

In one sentence

App stores in a nutshell

Apple's 30% cut is a monopoly toll on mobile software that developers can't escape — defended more fiercely than any other Apple business, which tells you how good the toll is.
