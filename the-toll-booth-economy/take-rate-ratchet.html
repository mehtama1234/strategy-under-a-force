Group D · The ticket, the creator & the ratchet

The take-rate ratchet — get big, then squeeze

The pattern behind every page: start cheap to win the market, then raise the toll once nobody can leave.

Here's the mechanism under the whole collection. Platforms start cheap and generous to attract sellers, creators, hosts, and drivers — to win the market. Once that supply is locked in and dependent, the platform steadily raises its cut and adds new fees, because the captured supply can't leave. This is the "ratchet" — and it almost never turns back.

The ratchet in action

  • Amazon seller fees rose toward ~50% over 20 years. No rollback.
  • Etsy's 2022 hike triggered a 100,000-signature strike — Etsy proceeded anyway.
  • Uber's take crept from ~20% (2012) to 40–60% total markup. No reversal.
  • App Store's 30% has held for 15 years; even the EU forced structure, not a fee cut.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Once a platform controls the network, raising fees is nearly costless — sellers stay because leaving means losing customers. The only real constraint is regulation, which moves slowly and rarely forces an actual cut.

The surprising part

The ratchet almost never reverses, even when the fees are visible and hated. Etsy's failed strike proved coordinated protest rarely works — sellers mostly stay because the switching cost is real. More striking: platforms often raise fees during downturns (Uber in 2020, Ticketmaster's surge pricing), correctly betting that demand for the network beats fee sensitivity. Lock-in is powerful enough to override public backlash.

In one sentence

The take-rate ratchet in a nutshell

Platforms exploit supply lock-in to raise their cut 50–100% over a decade, and the toll reverses only under regulation or a genuine rival the supply can actually flee to — both rare.
