Group B · The picks & shovels

Creator tools

Selling shovels to creators is a far steadier business than being one.

A whole software industry sells tools to creators: Patreon and Substack (paid subscriptions and newsletters), Kajabi and Teachable (courses), Linktree (link-in-bio), Canva and CapCut (design and editing). These are the reliable businesses of the creator economy — they get paid whether or not the individual creator succeeds, because the next creator always arrives needing the same shovel.

The core bet

Selling tools and infrastructure to millions of creators is a steadier business than being a creator — you get paid whether the creator succeeds or fails.

What the numbers say

$8B+
Cumulative payouts Patreon has routed to creators; it takes 8–12% and has ~3M paying members.
$1.1B
Substack's June 2025 valuation; it takes 10% and has ~5M paying subscriptions.
$1.7B
Linktree's 2022 valuation — a 'link in bio' aggregator that grew to ~70M users.
$42B
Canva's valuation (~$4B 2025 revenue, ~260M monthly users) — the biggest creator-tool success.
8–12% / $50–399
Take-rate range: a cut of earnings (Patreon, Substack) or a flat monthly SaaS fee (Kajabi, Teachable).

The failed creator gets replaced; the tool gets paid either way

If a creator bombs, they vanish — but the next one arrives tomorrow needing the same editing app, newsletter host, and storefront. That converts wildly unpredictable creator income into reliable, recurring SaaS revenue, which is why the tool-makers are among the most valuable companies in the whole economy.

The single biggest worry

The platforms can copy the tool — YouTube added memberships, TikTok added tips and subscriptions — squeezing the middlemen. And the tools still ultimately depend on creator incomes staying healthy enough to keep paying.

The surprising part

The tell is Linktree: an entire billion-dollar company built on a single feature — a list of links. It worked because every creator needed it and a free tier with paid upgrades prints money at scale. The safest way to profit from the creator gold rush turned out to be selling the pickaxes, not swinging one.

In one sentence

The creators who failed got replaced by new ones, but Patreon, Substack, Canva, and Shopify got paid either way — turning unpredictable creator income into reliable SaaS revenue.
