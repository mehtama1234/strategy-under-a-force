Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

The safest way to profit from the creator gold rush is to never create at all.

This is the follow-the-money piece. The durable profit pools not with the average creator but with the infrastructure: the platforms (who take 30–55% and own the audience), the tool-makers (Patreon, Canva, Linktree — selling shovels), the ad-tech and brands, the agencies, and the small number of top creators who graduated to owning products. The typical creator earns almost nothing.

The core bet

The reliable money goes to the infrastructure around creators — platforms, tools, agencies, ad-tech — and to the few creators who own products, not to the millions chasing views.

A gold rush that enriches the shovel-sellers and the landlords

Selling the shovel (Patreon, Canva, Linktree), building the platform (YouTube, TikTok), or managing the miners (agencies) is far steadier than trying to be a creator. The platform owners and tool-makers win regardless of which creators succeed — and the millions of hopeful creators are the ones funding their growth.

The single biggest worry

The model depends on an endless oversupply of creators working for almost nothing — a race to the bottom. If creators stop accepting near-poverty pay, or the next generation looks elsewhere, the system has no obvious plan B.

The surprising part

The clean restatement of the 1849 pattern: fifty million miners chasing gold built a quarter-trillion-dollar industry, and almost none of the money ended up in the miners' pockets. It went to whoever sold the shovels and owned the land — which, in the creator economy, means the tools and the platforms.

In one sentence

Fifty million creators chasing income built a $250B industry — and almost none of it ends up in the average creator's pocket.
