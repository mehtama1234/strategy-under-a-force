Group B · The picks & shovels

The platform take-rate

Creators are running a business on someone else's land — and the landlord sets the rent.

The big platforms host the audience and take a cut of everything: YouTube keeps ~45% of ad revenue, app stores take 30% of tips, Twitch fought over sub splits, and 'creator funds' pay little while keeping creators locked in. The platform owns the audience relationship and the algorithm, so it sets the terms — and can change them without warning.

The core bet

The platform owns the audience and the algorithm, so it can take a large cut and dictate terms; creators are contractors on rented land.

'Creator funds' are a lock-in tool, not generosity

Platforms pay just enough to stop creators from leaving for a rival, but not enough that most can quit chasing growth. The 'creator fund' isn't charity — it's a retention device that keeps creators uploading to the platform's turf while the platform keeps the audience relationship and most of the money.

The single biggest worry

Creators have almost no leverage: the platform can slash payouts or bury a creator's reach in the algorithm overnight, with no contract, no warning, and no appeal — and an income built over years can evaporate in weeks.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable design is that the platform's interest and the creator's don't align. Paying creators just enough to stay but not enough to be independent keeps the supply of content flowing and the power on the platform's side. Creators build the audience; the landlord owns the land.

In one sentence

Platforms own the audience relationship, so they can take 30–55% of every dollar a creator makes while keeping them dependent enough to fear losing it all.
