Group C · Who tames it

Complexity as a service

The messier the rulebook, the better the business — so the incentive is for rules to get worse.

Beyond healthcare, complexity itself is sold as software across the economy: payroll and tax compliance, sales-tax automation, multi-state and global employment rules, licensing, and more. The pattern is always the same — wherever rules are complicated and vary by place, a subscription business arises to handle the paperwork the customer can't avoid, and it grows richer every time the rulebook thickens.

The core bet

Wherever rules are complicated and differ by state, country, or industry, a software company can turn that complexity into a recurring subscription — selling relief from paperwork the customer can't escape.

An industry that quietly benefits from more red tape

The worse the rulebook, the better the business — these firms earn more when governments add rules, not fewer. In a twisted way that gives them an incentive to favor more regulation, because their customers pay them to comply with whatever gets written. And switching costs (years of loaded data) make the revenue extraordinarily sticky.

The single biggest worry

The value depends entirely on rules staying complicated — simplify the tax code or harmonize state employment law and half the revenue vanishes — which makes 'complexity is permanent' a scary bet to stake billions on.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable logic is that these companies profit from the exact thing everyone complains about. Every tangle in the law is a feature to sell, so the messiness that burdens customers is the moat that protects the vendor — and nobody in the loop is rooting for a simpler rulebook.

In one sentence

Governments make rules too complex to follow alone, so software companies charge a subscription to decode them — and profit more when the rules get worse.
