Claims & denials

Every denied claim is work and money for someone — so the tug-of-war itself is a business.

Health insurers deny a meaningful share of claims; providers and patients then appeal. That friction created a whole economy: insurers with denial systems, providers with denial-management vendors, patient-advocacy startups that fight bills, and AI on both sides. The gap between 'submit' and 'get paid' is monetized by everyone in the middle — and the denial 'works' as a strategy even when it's wrong.

The core bet

Every denied claim creates work and revenue for someone — the denial-management vendor, the appeals firm, the patient advocate — so the tug-of-war itself is a business.

Denial works precisely because most people give up

The math is brutal: most denials are never appealed, even though appeals often win — so denying by default is profitable regardless of whether the patient fights back. The system gains from information asymmetry, and a whole services industry then grew up to monetize the predictable tug-of-war on the other side.

The single biggest worry

AI lets insurers deny at industrial scale — thousands of claims at once — while the burden of appeal falls on patients who are sick, broke, or busy and mostly never learn they can push back.

The surprising part

The revealing twist is that fighting a denial became a business model, not a bug report. Because denials are predictable and most go unchallenged, denial-management vendors, appeals firms, and bill-fighting apps all built revenue on the friction — so both the denying and the appealing are now paid work, and nobody profits from the claim just being paid the first time.

In one sentence

Insurers deny claims at scale knowing most patients won't fight, and an entire economy of vendors and apps grew up to monetize the appeals on the other side.
