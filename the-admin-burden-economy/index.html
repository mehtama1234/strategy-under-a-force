The Admin-Burden Economy

The paperwork itself became a huge business — for the people who wade through it and the people who promise to make it disappear.

The 42nd force. American life and business run on staggering amounts of paperwork and friction — prior authorizations, medical billing and coding, claims and denials, credentialing, compliance, onboarding. A whole economy grew up on both sides of that burden: firms that process it (revenue-cycle management, business-process outsourcing) and firms that promise to tame it (automation software, AI). Healthcare is the flagship, where roughly a quarter of every dollar goes to admin, not care. The one idea: the friction is somebody's revenue — so nobody in the machine is fully on the side of making the paperwork actually go away.

Seeded by the Inc. 5000 data This one runs across two big themes — Regulated-Industry Software (634 companies, ~$33.7B) and Health & Longevity (578 companies, ~$47.6B) — with "revenue-cycle-management consolidation" recurring as a named growth driver. This collection follows that money.
The one question, asked of everyone

What did the admin-burden economy make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? The ability to get paid, get approved, or prove compliance became a service worth billions; the paperwork stayed abundant on purpose; and "waste" for the system became "revenue" for the middle. The danger: the friction is monetized by everyone in the middle, AI could either gut the processing jobs or just relocate the work, and the vertically integrated giants can profit from creating the friction and from clearing it.
