Group A · The paperwork mountain

Healthcare's admin overhead

A quarter of every U.S. health dollar goes to shuffling paper, not healing — and that's a business.

U.S. healthcare spends an enormous share of its dollars not on care but on administration — billing, coding, insurance forms, claims. Estimates run from ~15% to ~25% of spending, far above other rich countries. That paperwork is the flagship of a whole 'admin-burden economy': friction that costs money, employs armies, and makes more money for the people who manage the complexity than for anyone trying to end it.

The core bet

U.S. healthcare's paperwork is so vast and expensive that processing it — or promising to reduce it — is itself a giant industry, and almost no one inside it is paid to make it smaller.

What the numbers say

15–25%
Share of U.S. health spending that goes to administration — roughly $300–500B a year in a ~$4.5T system.
2%
Medicare's administrative overhead — one payer, one rulebook — versus the multi-payer system's 15–25%.
5% / 7%
Admin's share of health spending in Germany / Canada — a fraction of the U.S. figure.
~1 : 2
Roughly one billing-and-coding specialist for every two doctors in the U.S.
300+
Private insurers, each with its own forms and rules, that a hospital must bill against.
~$250B+
Admin spend researchers estimate is 'potentially avoidable' — pure friction, no clinical value.

The complexity is a choice nobody chose

The gap between Medicare's 2% and the system's 15–25% is entirely self-inflicted: it comes from having hundreds of payers with different rules, not from higher quality. But no single actor is on the side of simplifying — hospitals profit from billing expertise, insurers keep control through complex rules, and software vendors sell tools that adapt to the chaos rather than end it.

The single biggest worry

The complexity is self-reinforcing, because everyone who touches it makes money from it staying complex — so there is no constituency for making it simpler, and reform has stalled for decades.

The surprising part

The striking comparison is Medicare's own numbers: it runs on 2% admin because it has one payer, one set of rules, one claims database. The rest of the system spends 7–12x that — a difference measured in hundreds of billions a year that pays not for better care but for the sheer effort of navigating deliberate complexity.

In one sentence

The U.S. built a $300–500 billion-a-year industry to manage billing complexity that other countries solved for a fraction of the cost — and now profits by keeping it broken.
