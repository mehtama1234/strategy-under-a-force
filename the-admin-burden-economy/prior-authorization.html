Group A · The paperwork mountain

Prior authorization

Friction by design: the insurer says 'get approval first,' and delay becomes a strategy.

Prior authorization is when an insurer requires a doctor to get approval before a treatment, test, or drug. It's meant to control costs, but it ballooned into a huge source of delay, denial, and paperwork — spawning software to submit and manage it, and firms on both sides: insurers automating denials, providers fighting them. It's the purest example of friction that is somebody's revenue.

The core bet

Prior authorization is friction by design — insurers use it to gate spending, doctors spend hours fighting it, and a growing industry monetizes the paperwork in between. Both sides profit from the bottleneck existing.

An AI-versus-AI arms race that heaps on cost

The same technology that lets insurers auto-deny at scale is now used by providers to auto-generate appeals — an arms race that adds cost on both sides and makes the paperwork heavier, not lighter. Reform (CMS transparency rules) threatens to strip away the friction that vendors, consultants, and insurers all profit from maintaining.

The single biggest worry

AI-powered auto-denial lets insurers reject claims at speed and scale, pushing the burden onto sick patients and busy doctors — while reform that forces transparency could remove the exact friction a whole industry monetizes.

The surprising part

The system is working exactly as built: the delay and the denial aren't failures, they're features. Every request that gets slowed, questioned, or bounced is a cost saved for the insurer and a fee earned for the vendors in the middle — so the friction is defended, not fixed.

In one sentence

Prior authorization began as a cost control and became a rent-extraction machine where delay and denial are the point, not the problem.
