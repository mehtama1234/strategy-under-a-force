Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

One company can own the insurer that denies, the provider that appeals, and the software that processes it.

This is the follow-the-money piece. The admin burden pays two sets of players: those who process the friction (RCM firms, BPO, billing and coding, claims vendors) and those who promise to tame it (automation software, AI, compliance SaaS). Both profit from the paperwork existing — and the vertically integrated giants, above all Optum, sit on both sides at once.

The core bet

The friction pays everyone in the middle — the processor who handles it and the vendor who promises to remove it — so the whole ecosystem quietly depends on the paperwork staying.

What the numbers say

~$250B
Optum's revenue — it owns the insurer, a provider network, the pharmacy-benefit manager, and an RCM business.
~$60B
The RCM market; firms charge 2–10% of collected revenue, so they scale with the complexity they process.
~$300B
The global healthcare-BPO market, staffing millions of billing, coding, and credentialing roles.
60M+
U.S. prior-auth requests a year; 40–80% of denials are reversed on appeal — much of it administrative, not medical.
$500B–$1T
Estimated annual U.S. health inefficiency — which is 'waste' to the system and 'revenue' to the middle.

The same giant profits when friction rises and when it falls

A single company can own the insurer that denies a claim (creating friction), the RCM firm that processes the appeal (earning a fee to resolve it), and the software that automates the next round. That vertical integration means the same firm profits whether friction goes up or down — so there's no alignment toward actually simplifying anything.

The single biggest worry

The deepest conflict is scale: one firm can profit from creating the friction as an insurer and from clearing it as an RCM and compliance vendor — a conflict of interest at Fortune-10 size, with no one structurally on the side of simplification.

The surprising part

The reframe that lands hardest: the 'waste' everyone decries isn't waste to the people inside it — it's their business model. A vertically integrated giant can own the denial, the appeal, and the tool that speeds them both, collecting at every step, so the $500B–$1T of 'inefficiency' is revenue to protect, not a bug to fix.

In one sentence

Healthcare's administrative burden is a Fortune-10-sized business for the firms that process it and the vendors that automate it — so nobody in the system is fully incentivized to make it disappear.
