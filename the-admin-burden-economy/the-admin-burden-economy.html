The admin-burden economy

Waste for the system, revenue for the middle — so the friction is defended, not fixed.

Pull the whole collection together and one line runs through it. America's paperwork and administrative friction became a huge economy on both sides — the firms that process it (revenue-cycle management, outsourcing, billing, claims) and the firms that promise to tame it (automation, AI, compliance software). Healthcare is the flagship, where roughly a quarter of spending is admin. The through-line: the friction is somebody's revenue, so nobody in the machine is fully on the side of making the paperwork disappear.

The core bet

The paperwork itself became a huge business for the people who wade through it and the people who promise to make it vanish — and the friction is someone's revenue.

Winners and losers

The economy sorts cleanly into who gets paid by the friction and who is buried by it.

Who ends up ahead

The processors (RCM, BPO, billing and coding, claims vendors), the tamers (automation and compliance SaaS), and above all the vertically integrated giants (Optum) who own the insurer that denies, the provider that appeals, and the software that processes it.

Who's exposed

Patients and doctors buried in paperwork (physicians lose 30–40% of their time to it), the ~90% who don't appeal denials, and — soon — the admin workers and coders whose jobs AI may automate away.

The single biggest worry

Nobody in the machine is fully incentivized to simplify, because the friction is the revenue — so the rules pile up, the codes proliferate, and reform stays politically stuck because too many jobs and too much money depend on keeping it complicated.

The surprising part

The note to end on is the perverse alignment at its core. The 'waste' everyone decries — the $300–500 billion a year in admin overhead — is not waste to the people inside it; it's a multi-hundred-billion-dollar industry with millions of jobs. And the 'solution' to admin burden keeps becoming another layer of paid complexity: one company can own the denial, the appeal, and the AI that promises to speed them both, profiting at every loop. The friction isn't a bug in the system — for the middle, it's the whole business.

In one sentence

America's addiction to paperwork created an economy where the firms that process the red tape and the firms that sell tools to cut it both profit from the friction staying high — so it keeps growing instead of shrinking.
