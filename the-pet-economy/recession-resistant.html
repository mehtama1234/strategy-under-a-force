Group D · The forces & the limits

Recession-resistant?

Owners cut their own spending before their pet's — but the label is cracking.

Investors love the pet category for one reason: it's supposed to be recession-proof. Owners treat pet spending like rent — a non-negotiable emotional duty they protect before cutting their own extras. Total US pet spending kept climbing through downturns. But look closer at 2023–25 and cracks appear: people trading down, delaying vet visits, and fewer adoptions.

The core bet

The bet is that pet spending is non-discretionary — a household treats the dog like family and defends its budget last. That makes the category a defensive investment. The question this page tests: is it really recession-proof, or just recession-resistant until inflation bites hard enough?

What the numbers say

$158B
US pet spending in 2025, up 3.7%, projected to hit ~$165B in 2026 — steady growth right through the economic squeeze. The macro story looks resilient.
68%
Owners who spent more on pets in early 2023 versus a year earlier — pet spending ranked second only to human healthcare among things people refused to cut.
32%
Owners who couldn't afford a vet visit in the last 6 months (2025). Vet visits fell 2.3% in 2024 — the first real behavioral crack.
50%
Share naming cost as the top driver of pet-spending decisions in 2025 — up from just 2% two years earlier, now roughly tied with health concerns.

The cracks under the totals

The macro number keeps rising, but the behavior beneath it is shifting. Owners aren't abandoning pets — they're trading down (grocery kibble instead of fresh), skipping exams, and delaying care. The clearest tell: adoptions slowed while shelters filled, because acquiring a new pet is the easiest step to price out.

What holds up
Core spending on food and essentials for an existing pet — genuinely sticky, defended before an owner's own extras.
What bends
Premium tiers, elective vet care, and new-pet adoption — the discretionary edges give way under inflation.
The single biggest worry

Shelter overcrowding is the canary. Fewer adoptions plus higher ownership costs means slower turnover and growing strain — a signal that pet acquisition, the easiest discretionary step, is now priced out for middle- and lower-income households.

The surprising part

Pet spending totals keep rising — $158B in 2025 — but that growth masks real behavioral cracks. Owners are trading down and delaying care rather than abandoning pets, so the industry looks resilient from the top while, at the margin, inflation forces hard trade-offs the "recession-proof" story glosses over.

In one sentence

Pet spending is sticky, not bulletproof — the macro total climbs while owners quietly trade down, skip the vet, and adopt less, cracking the recession-proof label at the edges.
