Group B · Pet healthcare

Corporate vet care

Your "local vet" is increasingly owned by Mars — the candy company.

The family vet used to be an independent small business. No longer. Private equity and giant corporations have bought up thousands of clinics and rolled them into national chains. Mars — yes, the maker of M&Ms and Pedigree — is now one of the largest pet-healthcare operators on earth, and most owners have no idea. This is the pet economy's overlap with the roll-up.

The core bet

The bet is a classic roll-up: a fragmented market of independent clinics gets bought up, standardized, and run at scale — with pricing power and upselling built in. Buy the boring local vet, consolidate, and extract profit as pets get more expensive to care for.

What the numbers say

2,000+
Vet clinics Mars Petcare operates globally — including Banfield, VCA, and BluePearl in the US, plus international chains. One of the world's largest vet operators.
$9.1B
What Mars paid for VCA Animal Hospitals in 2017 — about 917 hospitals in one deal, the biggest single move in the consolidation.
1,054
Banfield locations (Mars-owned since 2007), mostly inside PetSmart stores — plus 61 BluePearl specialty and emergency sites.
$3B
Combined revenue of JAB's vet arm (Compassion-First + National Veterinary Associates) by 2020 — the other big consolidator, buying both chains in 2019.

Why owners don't notice

The clinics keep their local names. Your neighborhood animal hospital may be owned by Mars or a private-equity firm, but the sign on the door doesn't say so. That's the point: consolidation is invisible, so owners can't vote with their feet even if prices and upselling creep up.

What's newly valuable
Scale, standardized upselling, and control of a fragmented, recession-resistant market with captive, emotional customers.
What's at risk
Independent clinical judgment, when corporate targets push toward more tests and procedures.
The single biggest worry

When a corporate chain owns your vet, financial pressure to upsell services can replace the vet's independent judgment — and because owners don't know their clinic is corporate-owned, they can't easily choose otherwise.

The surprising part

The company famous for candy bars and dog kibble is now one of the world's largest veterinary healthcare operators, running more than 2,000 clinics. Most pet owners have no idea their neighborhood vet is owned by the same corporation that makes their dog's food — a textbook roll-up, hidden behind familiar local signage.

In one sentence

Corporate consolidation — led by Mars and private equity — quietly turned the independent family vet into a financialized chain where profit targets can outweigh clinical judgment.
