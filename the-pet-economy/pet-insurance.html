Group B · Pet healthcare

Pet insurance

Americans insure their dog's health while skipping their own dental.

As vet bills climb and pets become family, pet insurance has grown fast — Trupanion, Nationwide, Lemonade, and now Chewy embedding it at checkout. Yet US take-up is still tiny compared to the UK and Sweden, which is exactly why investors love it: a huge runway. The catch is that vet-cost inflation makes it a hard business to run profitably.

The core bet

The bet is that US pet insurance will eventually catch up to Sweden and the UK, turning today's tiny base into a multi-billion-dollar market. The trick is distribution — embedding a policy into the Chewy checkout so it becomes a default, not a deliberate purchase.

What the numbers say

4.3%
US pet-insurance penetration in 2025 — about 1 in 23 dogs and cats. Compare that with 40%+ in Sweden and 25% in the UK.
$5.7B
US premiums in 2025, up ~20% year over year, covering nearly 7 million pets. Small next to human health insurance, but accelerating fast.
~70–74%
Loss ratios in 2024–25 (Trupanion ~69.8%, industry ~74%). Healthy for insurers, but tight — little room for error if vet costs keep rising.
100,000
Pet policies Nationwide dropped in 2025, citing vet-cost pressure — a sign the loss-ratio math is already straining.

The distribution game

The winners aren't just underwriters — they're whoever owns the moment of purchase. Chewy sells insurance via Lemonade and Trupanion right in the cart; Lemonade bundles it with home and auto. The pitch isn't "here's a rational cost-benefit" — it's "protect a member of your family."

What's newly valuable
Embedded distribution (insurance at the pet-store checkout), the low-penetration runway, and the "pets-as-family" emotional pitch that overrides cost math.
What's risky
Vet-cost inflation runs 2–3× general inflation, squeezing margins faster than premiums can rise.
The single biggest worry

Vet costs are rising faster than insurers can raise premiums without losing customers. When a major carrier culls 100,000 policies rather than hike rates, it's admitting the loss-ratio math breaks unless adoption scales fast enough to spread the cost.

The surprising part

Americans will insure their dog's health while skipping their own dental and vision coverage. That inverts normal risk logic: pet insurance is framed as "family duty" and triggers emotional spending, while human supplemental insurance gets treated as optional. Penetration is driven by story, not spreadsheet — which is powerful, and fragile.

In one sentence

Pet insurance is a "pets-as-family" marker growing 20% a year on emotion, but with under-5% penetration and 70% loss ratios, it stays a margin trap unless adoption scales fast.
