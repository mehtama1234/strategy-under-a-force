Lithium & batteries

A soft grey metal nobody thought about became a strategic prize — because everything electric runs on it.

Electric cars, phones, and grid storage all depend on lithium-ion batteries, so an obscure element turned into a geopolitical chess piece. Prices whipsawed — a huge 2021–22 spike, then a 2023 crash — mining is concentrated in a few countries, and everyone scrambled to lock up supply and, more importantly, the refining that turns rock into battery-grade chemicals. It's called "the new oil," and the label both fits and misleads.

The core bet

Lithium became strategic not because it's genuinely rare, but because a decade-long gap between opening a mine and battery demand created leverage for whoever already controlled supply or refining. China, which refines most of the world's lithium, turned that gap into a chokepoint — so countries now negotiate over battery supply chains, not just prices.

Not a shortage of rock — a shortage of speed

There's plenty of lithium in the ground for decades. The bottleneck is the mismatch in speed: battery demand compounds every year while a new mine takes 7–10 years to build. That produces boom-bust cycles — shortages spike prices and kill demand (2023's crash), then overbuilding crashes prices and bankrupts producers, stalling the next round of mines. It behaves less like a scarce element and more like an OPEC-style control of pace.

Who ends up ahead

Whoever controls refining and locked-up supply — especially China — plus future recyclers who can close the loop.

Who's exposed

Battery and car makers without secured supply; miners caught in the price crashes; anyone betting on stable lithium prices.

The single biggest worry

The real risk isn't running out — it's the whiplash. China controls most refining and has bought stakes in Chilean, Australian, and Congolese supply, so it can influence the pace at which lithium reaches battery makers. A temporary shortage spikes prices and stalls EV plans; a glut crashes them and freezes new mines — and the ten-year mine lead time means each swing echoes for a decade.

The surprising part

The "new oil" analogy breaks in one crucial way: oil gets burned, but lithium can be recycled — a dead EV battery keeps 70–90% of it. So the endgame isn't "we run out," it's "who controls the refining and recycling loop" — which is stickier and more concentrated than any mine, and already sits largely in China.

In one sentence

Lithium became a geopolitical prize not because it's rare but because refining and the decade-long mine lag hand control to whoever moves first — and China already refines most of it.
