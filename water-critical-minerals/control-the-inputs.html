Group D · The mechanism & the moves

Control the inputs

When raw materials become the bottleneck, the smart move is to stop competing on the product and lock up the rock.

Once materials become the binding constraint, the winning strategy flips: stop competing on who builds the best car and start competing on who locked up the lithium, cobalt, and — above all — the refining. Carmakers now sign direct mine deals and buy stakes in mines; governments pass critical-minerals laws and build stockpiles; traders and refiners quietly become kingmakers. The moat moved from the factory to the mine and the refinery.

The core bet

When the constraint is raw materials, competitive advantage shifts from manufacturing skill to owning the inputs — the mine, and especially the chemistry that turns ore into battery-grade material. Governments have turned geological maps into national-security doctrine.

The moat is the middle, not the mine

The surprise is that the moat didn't stay with miners. A miner with ore but no refinery is just a commodity player; a refiner with no mine can buy ore on the spot market. The bulletproof position is owning both the mine and the conversion to battery-grade chemicals. That's why Tesla is buying refining tech and oil majors are building battery-mineral arms — they know the real moat is the midstream, the refining middle.

Who ends up ahead

Vertically integrated players who own mine + refinery; traders and PE firms that moved faster than automakers could build.

Who's exposed

Pure carmakers and tech firms without secured supply; miners with ore but no chemistry.

The single biggest worry

The constraint is refining and politics, not geology. Lithium is abundant; the risk is a refinery going down, a government blocking a shipment, or instability where the supply sits. Automakers who signed offtakes are betting on stable politics in Chile, Argentina, and the Congo — which have proven neither. One coup in the Congo (70% of cobalt) or one water crisis in Chile could ripple across the whole EV industry.

The surprising part

The clever money didn't buy mines — it bought the middle. Commodity traders, private-equity firms, and oil majors pivoting to "energy metals" became the kingmakers by grabbing refining, conversion, and recycling capacity faster than carmakers could build it. Owning the mine makes you a commodity player; owning the chemistry that turns ore into batteries makes you indispensable.

In one sentence

When materials became the bottleneck, advantage shifted from "we build better products" to "we locked up the rock and the chemistry" — and governments turned geology into national-security policy.
