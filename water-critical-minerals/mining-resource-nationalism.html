Group C · The chokepoints & geopolitics

Resource nationalism

Mineral-rich countries stopped selling cheap ore — they want the factory built at home.

Countries sitting on the rock finally noticed they were selling $1,000 of ore that became $8,000 of battery somewhere else. So they changed the rules: Indonesia banned raw nickel-ore exports in 2020 to force smelters onshore — and it worked. Chile and Mexico moved to state-control lithium; African producers now demand local processing. The cheap exporter became the gatekeeper.

The core bet

Mineral-rich nations stopped shipping cheap ore and started banning raw exports, forcing battery and tech firms to build processing plants locally or pay heavy taxes. By holding both the rock and the refinery, they aim to keep the fat margin that used to flow to foreign smelters.

Nationalist optics, foreign-owned factories

The move flips the org chart — the rock stays home — but often not the profit. Indonesia's ban worked because Chinese firms rushed in to build the smelters, so the nickel is Indonesian but the factories are Chinese-owned. Chile got political control without pricing power or technology. Countries captured jobs and optics; the real wealth moved from ore to processing — and processing is already largely owned.

Who ends up ahead

Resource-rich states that attract the factory (Indonesia), and the Chinese and Turkish firms that build and own it.

Who's exposed

States that overbuild into a price crash; buyers who now face export bans; foreign smelters cut out of the ore.

The single biggest worry

Processing plants are capital-heavy, polluting, and technically hard. Indonesia got lucky with eager Chinese investment; Chile and Mexico risk overbuilding while prices stay depressed, and the DRC lacks the capital and power grid to refine at scale. If processed-mineral prices fall faster than the factories can be paid off, resource nationalism becomes a debt trap.

The surprising part

The biggest winners often aren't the mineral countries at all — they're the foreign firms that build the plants. China ended up with 60%+ of global battery-processing capacity even though Indonesia owns the nickel and Chile the lithium. Nationalism moved the rock's location; it didn't move who owns the chemistry.

In one sentence

Mineral-rich countries banned the cheap-ore deal to capture the battery margin — but China built the factories instead, so the rock stayed home while the profit often didn't.
