Rare earths & magnets

The powerful magnets inside EV motors, wind turbines, and missiles are refined almost entirely in one country.

The strong permanent magnets in EV motors, wind turbines, headphones, and guided weapons are made from rare-earth elements. They aren't actually rare in the ground — but mining and especially refining them is dirty and difficult, and China does almost all of it. That handed Beijing a chokepoint it has used as leverage: cutting off Japan in 2010, and restricting gallium, germanium, and rare-earth exports in 2023–2025.

The core bet

Rare earths aren't geologically rare, but the willingness to do the toxic refining is — and China cornered it. That turned a niche group of metals into a geopolitical lever: clean energy and defense both depend on magnets that flow through Chinese refineries, so export limits become statecraft.

The West can mine — but not refine — in time

The bottleneck isn't digging; it's the 18-month-plus lag between deciding you need supply and having a working refinery. If tensions spike and Beijing throttles exports, the West faces a year or two where EV plants, turbine makers, and missile lines compete for scraps — and unlike oil, you can't swap one rare earth for another or buy it on a deep spot market. That's why every major economy is now scrambling to build strategic stockpiles.

Who ends up ahead

China's refiners; the handful of Western alternatives (Mountain Pass, Lynas) if they can scale; magnet recyclers.

Who's exposed

EV, wind, and defense makers dependent on Chinese magnets, with no fast domestic substitute.

The single biggest worry

The bottleneck can't easily leave China. Rare-earth refining is locked in by 30 years of accumulated know-how, cost structure, and a willingness to absorb pollution the West won't. So the world faces a paradox: it wants to decouple from China everywhere — except for the very materials that make decoupling and the clean transition possible.

The surprising part

Rare earths aren't rare at all — neodymium is more common than copper. What's rare is the willingness to bear the toxic cost of refining them. China turned an environmental liability into a geopolitical asset by being the only player willing to do the dirty work at scale, then discovered it held a chokepoint more valuable than an oil field.

In one sentence

China made itself the world's only large-scale rare-earth refiner by absorbing the pollution nobody else would — and now uses that chokepoint to hold clean energy and defense supply chains hostage.
