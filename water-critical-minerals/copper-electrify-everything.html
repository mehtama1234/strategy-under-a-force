Group B · The minerals the future runs on

Copper

You cannot electrify anything without copper — and the world can't dig it fast enough.

Every EV, wind turbine, solar farm, grid upgrade, and AI data center needs far more copper than the fossil-fueled thing it replaces. But a new copper mine takes 10–20 years to permit and build, and the ore we have left is getting poorer. So the green transition runs straight into a copper wall — the quiet shortage that could set the pace for everything else.

The core bet

The world needs perhaps 2–3× more copper by 2040 to electrify transport, grids, and data centers, but supply can barely keep up: mines take a decade-plus to build and ore grades keep falling. Copper is the physical speed limit on the whole electric build-out.

The permitting window is closing

New mines need 5–7 years of environmental review before a single tonne comes out — but the demand cliff arrives around 2027–2029. Big existing mines like Chile's Escondida are fading as ore grades drop, and copper now competes with lithium for the same capital and scarce water in arid regions. One major mine delayed or cancelled, and the whole EV-and-grid timeline compresses.

Who ends up ahead

Miners and countries with permitted copper and long-term supply deals; recyclers who can recover the ~50% still lost today.

Who's exposed

Automakers, grid builders, and renewable projects that assumed copper would always be there at a stable price.

The single biggest worry

The timing is the trap: demand rises now, mines arrive in a decade, and climate pressures (water limits, social opposition, grid strain) make expansion politically harder every year. Copper is the least glamorous critical mineral and possibly the most binding — you simply cannot build the electric economy without it, and it can't be conjured on demand.

The surprising part

While the West debates permits, China quietly won the copper game: it controls ~40% of global refining, locked long-term contracts with Peru, Chile, and the DRC, and pushed industrial recycling past 60%. "Sustainable mining" rules in Europe paradoxically made new mines harder to open there — so the West is set to be a copper price-taker for the next decade, not a shaper of supply.

In one sentence

The green transition needs far more copper by 2035 than the world can dig in time — so whoever locked up supply and recycling in the last few years, chiefly China, sets the pace for everyone.
