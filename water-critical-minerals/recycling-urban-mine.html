Group C · The chokepoints & geopolitics

The urban mine

A ton of old smartphones holds far more gold than a ton of gold ore — the richest mine is our junk drawer.

The richest ore body for some metals is the pile of dead electronics and spent batteries we've already dug up once. Recovering lithium, cobalt, nickel, and copper from old gear (the business Redwood Materials and Li-Cycle are chasing) could ease the crunch without opening new mines. But today we barely recycle most of it, and the big wave of dead EV batteries is only just starting to arrive.

The core bet

"Urban mining" — recovering metals from e-waste and dead batteries — is often cheaper and richer per ton than digging new ore, and could meaningfully cut the need for virgin mining. The catch is timing: the technology works, but the supply of dead batteries and the economics both arrive later than the demand does.

Real, richer — and idle until the 2030s

The economics only work when virgin metals are expensive, but the supply of dead batteries won't flow at scale until the 2030s. That leaves a 5–10 year window where recycling capacity sits half-full and loses money. And even a perfect recycler still depends on refining — much of which is in China — to turn recovered metal back into usable battery chemicals.

Who ends up ahead

Recyclers who survive to the 2030s battery wave; makers who design batteries for easy disassembly.

Who's exposed

Recyclers burning cash in the gap years; anyone counting on recycling to close the supply gap this decade.

The single biggest worry

The timing mismatch is brutal: recycling pays only when virgin metal is dear, but the recycled supply won't arrive until 2030–2035. Ramp too fast and you crash prices while virgin ore is already cheap; too slow and you miss the wave. And China's grip on refining means even flawless recovery may not translate into Western supply security.

The surprising part

The real barrier isn't the chemistry — it's battery design. Modern EV packs are glued and bonded to survive shock and vibration, which makes them harder to take apart than the first generation. Brute-force shredding mixes materials and loses purity. Recycling economics improve a lot if manufacturers design for disassembly — but that's a decade-long bet nobody's sure who will pay for.

In one sentence

The junk drawer is a richer mine than the ground, but it sits mostly idle until dead EV batteries arrive at scale in the 2030s — and China's refining grip still decides who profits.
