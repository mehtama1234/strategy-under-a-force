Group C · The chokepoints & geopolitics

China's refining monopoly

The West can open all the mines it wants — the ore still has to go to China to become usable.

The real chokepoint in critical minerals isn't the mine — it's the refinery. China doesn't just dig; it dominates the messy chemical step that turns raw ore into battery-grade material and finished magnets, for lithium, cobalt, graphite, nickel, and rare earths. You can open a mine in Australia or Chile, but the ore is nearly worthless until it's refined — and that mostly happens in China.

The core bet

China controls the chemistry, not just the rock. Mining without refining is just an expensive hole in the ground; the leaching, purifying, and processing that make ore valuable are concentrated in Chinese plants built over 20 years — so the West can open ten mines and still hit a wall.

Open holes, no chemistry

The Western strategy — open mines in friendly countries and avoid Chinese mining — misses the point. Mines without refineries are expensive holes. When China slowed rare-earth exports in 2010, it cut shipments ~30% overnight and Western buyers hit a wall immediately. That's the asymmetry: China can close a refinery and cripple the world in weeks; the West can open mines for a decade and still be stuck.

Who ends up ahead

China's refiners, and any Western or allied refinery that actually gets built and permitted.

Who's exposed

Countries with ore but no chemistry — miners, EV plants, and defense contractors who can't wait a decade.

The single biggest worry

You can't wait ten years for a refinery when a supply shock hits now. If China restricts refined exports during a crisis, battery makers, carmakers, and weapons lines stall immediately, because there's no substitute and no deep spot market for refined battery chemicals — the ore piling up at the mine can't be used.

The surprising part

China didn't have a natural advantage — it manufactured one. In the 1990s–2000s it deliberately bought global ore, absorbed the pollution the West wouldn't, and built refining at massive scale. Now nobody wants a lithium refinery in their backyard, and Chinese players hold expertise and economies of scale newcomers can't match for a decade. The moat is political and economic, not geological.

In one sentence

China's grip on critical minerals runs through the refinery, not the mine — and the West can't dig its way out of a chokepoint that took 20 years and a tolerance for pollution to build.
