Group A · Water as the new constraint

Chips need water

A single advanced chip factory drinks as much water as a city of half a million people.

The most advanced industry on earth — making the chips that run everything — turns out to be a water business first. A modern fab needs a river of ultra-pure water every day, and as the world races to build fabs (TSMC, Intel, Samsung), water has flipped from an afterthought to the thing that decides where you can even build one. Taiwan's 2021 drought was the first time a dry sky directly threatened the global chip supply.

The core bet

Fabs are water factories that happen to make chips. A single advanced fab can burn through as much water as a mid-sized city, so a reliable, decades-long water supply is now a hard limit on where the world's most strategic industry can physically sit — and climate change is drying out exactly the places the fabs cluster.

You can't truck your way out of a drought

Water isn't like oil — you can't ship it 500 miles cheaply, and you can't build a fab where there's no reliable long-term supply. A fab runs for 30 years; the water has to be there for all 30. Taiwan's monsoons are getting erratic, the American Southwest's Colorado River is over-drawn, and if a chipmaker can't promise stable output, the whole geopolitical bet on building fabs at home wobbles.

Who ends up ahead

Regions with abundant, secure fresh water and utilities willing to guarantee it for decades; water-recycling and reuse specialists.

Who's exposed

Fabs in drought-prone deserts and islands — and the towns and farms nearby that must share a shrinking supply.

The single biggest worry

Recycling is hitting a physics wall. Fabs already reuse 80–95% of their water, so the limiting factor is now the small fraction that can't be reclaimed plus the ultra-pure process water that degrades after a single use. You can't engineer your way out of a regional water deficit — and the fabs sit exactly where the deficits are growing.

The surprising part

The industry that made everything small, light, and efficient still depends on flooding a building with water. A fab with 95% recycling can still run dry, because the un-recyclable slice and the dedicated ultra-pure loop keep drawing down the aquifer. The most sophisticated manufacturing on the planet is hostage to the most basic input there is.

In one sentence

Chipmaking quietly became a water industry — and because you can't move water or engineer away the need for it, water is now the hard limit on where the world's most advanced factories can be built.
