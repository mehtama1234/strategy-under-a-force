Group D · The mechanism & the moves

Why scarcity returned

For a generation raw materials felt infinite and we obsessed over software. Then three things landed at once.

For thirty years, raw materials were cheap and abundant, and the smart money worried about software and services, not stuff. Then three forces hit together: electrifying everything (EVs, grids, heat pumps use several times the minerals), reshoring and decoupling (building supply chains at home instead of wherever's cheapest), and a warming climate stressing water. Together they turned physical atoms back into the binding constraint.

The core bet

The era of ignoring raw inputs ended because three trends converged at once — electrification, reshoring, and climate stress on water. Each alone would strain supply; together they flipped the economy's bottleneck from code back to copper, lithium, and fresh water.

Mines are slower than factories

The binding constraint is now mining, not manufacturing. You can build a battery plant in 18 months, but the lithium and cobalt to feed it take a decade to source — and climate-driven water rationing in the Andean lithium belt means some mines are literally drying up. Carmakers have taken orders they can't fill because the material didn't exist yet when they made the promise.

Who ends up ahead

Owners of mines, refineries, and water; countries that planned ahead and stockpiled.

Who's exposed

Everyone who assumed inputs were infinite — carmakers, grid builders, and rich countries used to outbidding poorer ones.

The single biggest worry

There's no shortcut. To hit net-zero targets the world must mine more lithium in 20 years than in the entire 20th century, and you can't print minerals or substitute them fast enough. Recycling and efficiency buy maybe a decade's reprieve, not an escape — and the mines that exist sit in places where water and politics are already working against expansion.

The surprising part

The scarcity isn't absolute — there's enough lithium in the crust to power ten planets. It's scarcity of the right mineral, in the right place, refined, at the speed we suddenly need. And when China, the US, and the EU all started hoarding at once around 2020, prices spiked and poorer producer nations lost leverage — so they built their own refineries and started demanding a bigger cut. The cost of atoms went up, and who controls them shifted.

In one sentence

Electrify everything, reshore the supply chains, and stress the water all at once, and you rediscover that mines are slower than factories — so physical atoms, not software, became the limit again.
