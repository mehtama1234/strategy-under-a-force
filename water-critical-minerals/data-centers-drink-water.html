Group A · Water as the new constraint

Data centers drink water

Every AI query turns into heat — and much of that heat is carried away by evaporating fresh water.

"The cloud" sounds weightless, but it's a warehouse full of chips throwing off heat, and the cheapest way to carry that heat away is to evaporate fresh water in cooling towers. The AI boom multiplied the compute — and the water bill — overnight, often in dry places (Arizona, Spain) where data centers now compete with towns and farms for the same supply. Computing turned out to be thirsty.

The core bet

Data centers got cheap to build partly because they could dump waste heat into evaporating water — one of the last "free" resources. AI's explosion in compute made that water bill come due, fast, in exactly the water-stressed regions where operators had always been able to build without thinking about it.

A resource that was never a constraint

For thirty years the data-center industry never had to treat water as scarce — build near power and a water source, and flat pricing plus political inertia did the rest. AI changed the math in a season: a single large training run can drink millions of gallons in weeks, forcing a reckoning in dry regions where decades-old deals let tech firms put cheap cooling ahead of townspeople's taps.

Who ends up ahead

Operators who move to water-free cooling early, and the cool, wet, power-rich regions that can host AI without draining anyone.

Who's exposed

Data centers in dry states, and the towns and farms next door now bidding against them for the same aquifer.

The single biggest worry

The bottleneck isn't engineering — lower-water cooling designs exist — it's speed. Regulators and retrofit technology move in years while AI demand for compute moves in months, so the strain arrives long before the fixes do, in places least able to spare the water.

The surprising part

Water quietly became a chokepoint for AI itself. A dry winter in Arizona throttles a hyperscaler's local capacity; a drought in Taiwan chokes the chip supply feeding the whole boom. "The cloud" isn't a metaphor — it's anchored to rainfall, aquifers, and which taps a town chooses to prioritize, in a way electricity never was.

In one sentence

AI turned the weightless-sounding cloud into a very physical machine that competes with farms and cities for fresh water — making cheap cooling suddenly scarce and strategic.
