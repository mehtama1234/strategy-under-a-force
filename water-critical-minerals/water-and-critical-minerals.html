Group E · The big picture

Water & critical minerals

The digital, electric, AI economy turns out to run on very physical, very limited stuff — and whoever controls the refinery controls the chain.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. For a generation we treated raw materials as infinite and cheap while software "ate the world" — but software runs on silicon, and silicon runs on energy, water, and rare minerals. In the 2020s three forces collided: electrify everything, reshore the supply chains, and a warming climate drying the places we mine and refine. Atoms became the bottleneck again.

The core bet

This isn't about ore running out — there's plenty in the ground. It's about the water to process it, where that processing happens, and who owns the refinery. The commodity everyone thought was infinite turned out to be fine; the refinery — the chokepoint — is the truly scarce asset. Whoever controls it controls the electric and AI economy.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Vertically integrated players who own the refining (not just the ore); nations willing to bear the environmental cost (China, Indonesia, parts of the DRC); recyclers and "urban miners"; water-saving extraction tech; and governments that forced refining onshore early.

Losers

Pure miners with no refinery; tech and car firms betting on infinite cheap inputs without supply deals; Indigenous and farming communities in water-scarce zones (Atacama, the Andes, the outback); and countries dependent on Chinese refining with no alternative.

The single biggest worry

The clean economy rests on a paradox: decarbonizing the grid, electrifying transport, and scaling AI all demand far more copper, lithium, cobalt, and rare earths — but mining and refining them is water-hungry, energy-hungry, and toxic. The energy transition says dig faster and process more; the climate transition says use less water and shrink the footprint. Often the same droughts that push us toward renewables also dry up the aquifers the mines need. The clean economy has a very dirty supply chain — and it sits where people have the least power to say no.

The surprising part

The US and Europe spent years chasing mining rights — lithium in Bolivia, cobalt in the Congo, rare earths in Vietnam. But mining was never the constraint; refining is. A ton of lithium ore is worthless until it's refined, and China won not by finding more ore but by being the only place willing to build refineries without environmental review. A new Western refinery takes 5–10 years and billions — by the time it opens, the contracts are written and China has already sold downstream.

In one sentence

Whoever controls the refinery — not the mine — controls the electric and AI economy, because the real bottleneck shifted from ore in the ground to fresh water and the political permission to process it.
