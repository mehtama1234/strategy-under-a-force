Group D · Forces & limits

Who pays

Private profit in the calm years; public loss when the disaster hits.

When insurance retreats, the cost shows up as higher premiums, bigger deductibles, narrower coverage, and outright uninsurance — and it lands hardest on those least able to bear it. Premiums soared in high-risk areas; people take higher deductibles or drop coverage; the uninsured lose everything after a disaster; and ultimately taxpayers backstop the losses through state pools and federal aid. The risk didn't disappear — it just moved onto individuals and the public.

The core bet

The uncomfortable pattern: private insurers collect premiums in the calm years, and when the big storm arrives the uninsured are wiped out and taxpayers cover the rest. It's regressive and socialized — good years private, bad years public.

The bill just moved

Insurers keep premiums and investment income during the ~95% of years when nothing happens. When the tail event hits, they collect reinsurance and the hole is filled by state pools (funded by surcharges on all other policyholders) and federal taxpayers. The risk never left the system — it shifted from private balance sheets to collective ones, and the public bears the cost.

Who pays most
The uninsured (lose everything), the middle class (soaring premiums), and taxpayers (the backstop).
Who's insulated
Higher-income households who can absorb premiums or rebuild out of pocket.
The single biggest worry

The uninsured and underinsured lose everything in a disaster, then compete for scarce FEMA grants, while higher-income households absorb rising premiums or rebuild out of pocket. Inequality widens in lockstep with the retreat.

The surprising part

The insurance retreat quietly privatizes the calm years and socializes the bad ones. Insurers keep premiums when nothing happens; when the big disaster hits, the uninsured are wiped out and taxpayers cover the rest through FEMA and state pools. The risk didn't vanish — it just moved from private balance sheets onto individuals and the public.

In one sentence

The insurance retreat privatizes calm-year profits and socializes disaster losses — hitting the poor and middle class hardest while shifting the bill to taxpayers and the uninsured.
