Group B · Beyond climate

Cyber insurance

The retreat isn't just weather — the biggest cyber risks got carved out as "acts of war."

As ransomware exploded, cyber-insurance losses soared, so insurers sharply raised premiums, demanded strict security controls just to qualify, and added exclusions. Lloyd's moved to exclude state-backed cyberattacks as "acts of war," and a landmark ruling (Merck's $1.4B NotPetya claim) tested whether a cyberattack counts as excluded war. Coverage got narrower and conditional — cyber insurance became a privilege for the well-defended.

The core bet

The bet insurers can't win cleanly: cyber risk is so correlated (one flaw can hit millions of firms at once) and so fast-moving that they can barely price it. So they raise prices, force companies to prove strong security first, and carve out the biggest risks — nation-state attacks — as uninsurable.

What the numbers say

+100–200%
Cyber premium rises on 2021–22 renewals (some sectors +400%) as ransomware payouts ballooned into the billions.
2022
Merck's $1.4B NotPetya claim was ruled not excluded as "hostile act" — but the case pushed insurers to tighten "war" language.
2023
Lloyd's required exclusions for state-backed cyberattacks — effectively declaring nation-state risk uninsurable.
30–40%
Share of small businesses that dropped cyber coverage after premium spikes; big firms shifted to self-insurance plus catastrophe reinsurance.

Insurance as a privilege

Insurers didn't walk away entirely — they did what they did with climate: raise prices, add exclusions, and demand proof you've done the security work (MFA, backups, incident-response plans) before they'll write a policy. The result is that cyber cover became a privilege for well-defended companies, and smaller or riskier firms are effectively shut out.

What's newly valuable
Strong security posture (it's now the price of admission), and firms that can price and transfer the tail.
What's uninsurable
Nation-state attacks and the correlated, systemic events insurers can't diversify away.
The single biggest worry

Cyber risk is perfectly correlated — a single zero-day or supply-chain flaw can hit millions of firms at once — so insurers can't diversify the portfolio the way they do with property. That systemic exposure is uninsurable at any price.

The surprising part

Cyber insurance is retreating exactly the way property insurance did after climate losses: premiums up, exclusions tighter, coverage narrower, and the biggest risks — nation-state attacks — declared uninsurable "acts of war." The same un-poolable math that broke home cover is breaking digital cover.
