Group D · Forces & limits

The mechanism

Why the retreat isn't greed — it's the 300-year-old math of pooling breaking.

Here's why insurers are pulling back, in plain English. Insurance works by pooling many independent risks — most people don't crash, burn, or flood in the same year, so premiums from the many cover the losses of the few. That breaks when risk becomes correlated (a wildfire or a cyber worm hits thousands at once), trending (climate and inflation mean the past no longer predicts the future), or uncertain (you can't price what you can't estimate).

The core idea

Pooling needs risks to be independent and predictable. The modern world is making more risks correlated (everyone hit at once) and trending (getting worse, unpredictably). When that happens, the math that made insurance possible for 300 years quietly fails — so the retreat isn't greed, it's arithmetic.

Three ways pooling breaks

Correlated: a single event (fire, flood, cyber worm) hits many at once, so the losses aren't independent. Trending: climate, repair-cost, and litigation inflation mean last year's data no longer predicts next year's, so pricing is guesswork. Uncertain: you can't price what you can't estimate. Break any one, and the model fails — so insurers rationally withdraw.

Why it's not fixable by scolding
If losses rise 15%/yr but you can only raise premiums 8%, you lose money on every policy — so you stop.
The honest read
The retreat signals risks fundamentally changed, not that insurers got worse at pooling.
The single biggest worry

The death spiral: as healthy risks leave (they won't buy cover they can't afford), only the worst risks remain, forcing prices up again and driving out more healthy risks. Once you can't pool the good with the bad, insurance stops being possible.

The surprising part

Everyone assumes insurers are greedy. The surprise: they're mathematically correct to retreat. If wildfire losses rise 15% a year but you can only raise premiums 8%, you lose money on every policy — so you stop. The real shock is that we've relied on a 300-year-old machine that assumes tomorrow looks like yesterday, and it no longer does.

In one sentence

Insurance retreats not because insurers are selfish, but because climate, inflation, litigation, and cyber-correlation made risks correlated and trending instead of independent and stable — the twin requirements the whole system was built on.
