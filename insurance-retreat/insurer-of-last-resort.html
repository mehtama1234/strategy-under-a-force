Group C · The gaps and who fills them

Insurer of last resort

The "temporary" government backstop quietly became the main insurer.

As private insurers flee, government-backed "insurer of last resort" plans absorb the risk they won't touch. California's FAIR Plan exposure ballooned; Florida's Citizens became one of the state's biggest insurers; the federal flood program (NFIP) is tens of billions in debt because flood risk is uninsurable privately. These plans were meant to be temporary — but they're becoming the primary insurer in risky areas, and they're underfunded.

The core bet

The design: a temporary backstop for the few who can't get private cover. The reality: as carriers exit whole regions, the last-resort plan becomes the main insurer in the riskiest zip codes — holding more exposure than most private carriers, on razor-thin reserves, so a single big event surcharges everyone.

What the numbers say

Florida Citizens policies — among the state's three largest insurers by count after private carriers pulled out.

assessments

Socializing the catastrophe

These plans hold razor-thin reserves (Florida Citizens has ~$10k per policy in reserve against multi-hundred-thousand-dollar homes). So a single major hurricane or fire doesn't just hit their own customers — it triggers assessments on every policyholder in the state, a hidden subsidy from low-risk to high-risk areas. And it risks a death spiral: rate hikes push people out, shrinking the base that must absorb the next loss.

The risk
Underfunded plans as the primary insurer in disaster zones — one event taxes everyone or forces a bailout.
The role
They keep some coverage alive where the private market gave up entirely.
The single biggest worry

A major disaster in a state where the government insurer holds a big share of exposure could trigger cascading assessments — raising premiums for every policyholder overnight, insured with it or not, or forcing a taxpayer bailout.

The surprising part

The "temporary" backstop is now the primary carrier in the riskiest areas — Florida Citizens ranks among the state's largest insurers, and California's FAIR Plan exposure doubled in five years. Yet they run on thin margins, so a single event doesn't just raise their rates; it surcharges everyone in the state, even people with private coverage — a hidden subsidy from safe places to dangerous ones.

In one sentence

Government insurers meant to be temporary are now the primary carrier in disaster zones — and their insolvency doesn't just hurt their customers, it taxes everyone else.
