Reinsurance repricing

Your local insurer's retreat was decided in Munich and Zurich.

Reinsurers — Munich Re, Swiss Re, Berkshire, Lloyd's — are the wholesale backstop, the insurers of insurers who cover the big catastrophe losses. After years of heavy disasters, they sharply raised prices and pulled back capacity for the riskiest layers. Because primary insurers depend on reinsurance, its repricing flows straight down to your home premium — or pushes your insurer to exit entirely.

The core bet

The surprise underneath the crisis: your insurer's retreat wasn't a local decision. It was a global wholesale repricing. When the reinsurers who absorb catastrophe risk raised prices by the most in a generation, every regional carrier standing on them had to raise rates or leave.

What the numbers say

A ripple from 4,000 miles away

Most people think the crisis is local — a Florida or California problem. But the real lever is global. A reinsurer's decision in Munich to charge 40% more for a risk layer means a Tampa carrier instantly can't compete, so it exits. That's why the retreat is synchronized across states and carriers: they're all reacting to the same wholesale reset.

What's newly valuable
Reinsurance capacity itself — repriced up, and the capital-market alternatives (cat bonds) filling the gap.
What it triggers
Primary insurers raising rates or fleeing, all at once, everywhere.
The single biggest worry

If reinsurance stays expensive (or climbs after another bad year), primary insurers keep retreating from high-risk zones — leaving homeowners only underfunded state pools, where a second big disaster triggers assessments on everyone.

The surprising part

Your inability to renew isn't your insurer's choice — it's a ripple from a wholesale market reset an ocean away. Because every regional carrier leans on the same global reinsurers, one repricing in Zurich synchronizes the retreat across dozens of states and companies at once.

In one sentence

Your home insurer raised rates or quit because the global reinsurers behind it repriced catastrophe risk up 35–50% in early 2023 — the biggest hardening in a generation.
