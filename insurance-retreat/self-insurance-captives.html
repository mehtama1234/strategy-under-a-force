Group C · The gaps and who fills them

Self-insurance & captives

Everyone becomes their own insurer — the rich by design, the poor by default.

As coverage gets scarce or unaffordable, businesses and individuals increasingly self-insure — take the risk onto themselves. Big companies set up "captive" insurers (their own in-house insurance subsidiary), a booming trend, to cover risks the market won't price well. Homeowners "go bare" when they can't afford cover or own outright. This shifts catastrophe risk out of the diversified pool onto single balance sheets — fine until a big loss hits.

The core bet

The bet, forced by the retreat: if the market won't insure you, insure yourself. It's rational for a Fortune 500 firm with a strong balance sheet — a captive can absorb a big cyber or catastrophe loss and it's tax-advantaged. It's a disaster for a homeowner who "goes bare" and can't survive a single fire.

What the numbers say

~7,000+
Captive insurers worldwide (~2,000 in the US) — Apple, Microsoft, JPMorgan, and Berkshire all run them; the trend surged after 2020.
~15–20%
US drivers who are uninsured (~26M); an estimated few percent of homeowners underinsured or uninsured by 2024.
~85%
Share of US flood damage that is uninsured — private insurers largely abandoned flood, and few at-risk homes carry NFIP cover.
~$70B
Annual captive-reinsurance premium (up from ~$40B in 2015) — captives now cover risks the traditional market won't touch.

A two-tier system

Insurance didn't disappear — it got privatized and split in two. Instead of everyone paying a modest premium into a pool that covers rare catastrophes, the rich self-insure through captives (rational, tax-advantaged, liquid), while the poor go uninsured because they can't afford climate-real premiums. A big company books a loss as a tax event and rebuilds; a homeowner who went bare is wiped out.

Who can absorb it
Large firms with captives and strong balance sheets — self-insuring is smart for them.
Who can't
Households with no reserves and no diversification — one disaster ends them.
The single biggest worry

A Fortune 500 captive can absorb a $500M loss; a homeowner going bare cannot survive a $1M fire. The shift concentrates uninsurable risk on households with no reserves and no diversification — a single event wipes them out while big firms book it and rebuild.

The surprising part

The response to insurers retreating is that everyone becomes their own insurer — but very unequally. Big corporations spin up in-house "captive" insurers by the thousands; ordinary homeowners simply go without. That quietly moves catastrophe risk out of the diversified pool and onto individual balance sheets, creating a two-tier system where the rich hedge and the poor are exposed.

In one sentence

The insurance retreat sparked a self-insurance boom for corporations but left individuals exposed — moving systemic risk out of the diversified pool and onto balance sheets that can't absorb it.
