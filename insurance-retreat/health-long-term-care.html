Group C · The gaps and who fills them

Long-term care

An entire category of insurance for old age basically died.

Long-term care insurance — meant to cover nursing homes and in-home care in old age — is a market that largely fell apart. Insurers badly mispriced it: people lived longer, kept their policies, and care costs soared. So most big insurers stopped selling new policies, and existing holders faced huge premium hikes. Now almost no one can buy affordable coverage for the biggest predictable expense of aging.

The core bet

Insurers in the 1990s–2000s bet people would buy young, use care on schedule, and let policies lapse. They were wrong on all three: lives got longer, lapse rates were far lower than priced, and care inflation ran hot. Rather than raise prices enough to be solvent, most carriers simply quit.

What the numbers say

~100+ → <15
Insurers selling LTC policies in the 1990s–2000s versus today — the market shrank to a handful of carriers.
+50–200%
Premium hikes on existing LTC policies (2010–2020) as carriers tried to recoup losses. Genworth, Unum, MetLife and others exited or froze sales.
$100k+/yr
Nursing-home cost in major metros by 2023 (up from ~$40k in 2000) — care inflation ran ~4–5%, far above general inflation.
~9% vs 20%
Actual policy lapse rate versus what insurers priced for — people kept the coverage, blowing up the math.

Abandoned, not repriced

This is a rare case of a large, predictable expense — old-age care — that the market actively abandoned rather than price correctly. Middle-class retirees now face a trap: too wealthy for immediate Medicaid, too poor to self-fund years of $100k+ care without decimating savings. So Medicaid now covers ~40% of nursing-home costs, and families spend down assets. This is cost disease (elder care) colliding with the demographic wave.

What broke
Affordable coverage for aging — a whole product line the market walked away from.
What's left
Self-funding, hybrid life/LTC products, and Medicaid after you've spent down.
The single biggest worry

Middle-class retirees face a gap: too wealthy for immediate Medicaid, too poor to self-fund $100k+/yr care. Without affordable LTC insurance they must spend down assets — destroying family wealth and turning elder care into a financial catastrophe.

The surprising part

An entire insurance category simply vanished — insurers mispriced it so badly that selling at any "fair" price would expose them to catastrophic loss, so they just stopped. It's one of the rare cases where a large, predictable expense (aging) was abandoned by the market rather than priced, leaving families to gamble on not needing care, or plan to go broke.

In one sentence

Long-term care insurance collapsed because insurers priced it for shorter lives and cheaper care, then quit rather than fix it — leaving the biggest expense of aging largely uninsurable.
