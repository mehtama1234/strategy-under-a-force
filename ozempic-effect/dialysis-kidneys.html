Group D · Surprising medical ripples

Dialysis — good medicine, complicated business

DaVita and Fresenius braced for the drugs to kill their business. It got weirder than that.

Dialysis companies run the centers where people go when their kidneys have failed — usually because of diabetes and obesity. They get paid for every treatment, three times a week, for the rest of a patient's life. So the business quietly depends on a steady stream of new people reaching kidney failure.

Why the drugs looked like a threat

−24%
A major trial showed Ozempic's ingredient cut kidney-disease events by about a quarter. The drugs protect the kidneys — which should slow the flow of new dialysis patients.

When that fear first hit in late 2023, dialysis stocks got hammered on the idea that weight-loss drugs would eventually starve the business of new customers — a "peak dialysis" moment.

The twist: a double reversal

Slower, not cured — and that changes everything

The drugs slow kidney decline; they don't reverse it. So patients live longer in the earlier stages — and, crucially, live longer once they're already on dialysis (DaVita's own data shows lower death and hospitalization rates for patients on the drugs). Near-term, that means the drugs may extend how long each patient keeps paying, not shorten it. The story flipped from "death of dialysis" to "dialysis renaissance." By 2026 the companies were telling investors the drugs were good for business.

The main worry

The long-term threat is still real — over years to decades, fewer people should reach kidney failure at all, slowly shrinking the pool. But it's a slow-motion erosion, not a cliff. Kidney failure takes years to develop, so today's patient base is stable, and the near-term effect has actually been a tailwind.

The surprising part

A weight-loss drug is a long-term threat to dialysis precisely because it works — keeping diabetic, overweight people healthier is wonderful medicine but shrinks the future pool of kidney-failure patients. The deeper surprise: the same "healthier patients" logic that threatens the pipeline also keeps current patients alive and paying longer. An industry that braced for extinction in 2023 was, by 2026, calling the drugs a tailwind. The threat didn't vanish — it just got pushed far enough into the future to look like good news for now.

In one sentence

Dialysis in a nutshell

The drugs protect kidneys, which should slowly shrink the dialysis business — but because they slow disease rather than cure it (and keep current patients alive longer), the near-term reality flipped to a tailwind. Good medicine, complicated business, deferred threat.
