Group E · The money & the country

Who pays — the bill behind the miracle drug

The drugs work. The real fight is over who can afford them.

Here's the twist at the center of the whole story: the science is basically settled — these drugs work. What decides whether tens of millions of people get them, or just the rich and well-insured, is money. At around $1,000+ a month, almost nobody pays full price out of pocket.

The bill is staggering

$27.5B
What Medicare alone spent on these drugs in one year — five times its level a few years earlier. Covering everyone eligible could cost tens of billions more per year.

So the people who were paying — employers and insurers — started backing out. Several big employers and state health plans dropped weight-loss coverage after the bills exploded.

The government fight

By law, Medicare has been banned from covering drugs used for weight loss. That's now cracking open:

  • A late-2025 deal cut Medicare's price to a few hundred dollars a month, with a $50 copay and obesity coverage starting mid-2026.
  • A government price negotiation set the 2027 price at about $274/month — roughly 70% off the list price.
  • A new "TrumpRx" direct site aims to sell the drugs cheaply straight to patients.

The drugmakers' response

Novo and Lilly are selling directly to patients (LillyDirect, NovoCare) for a few hundred dollars a month — bypassing the insurance fight entirely — and even cutting their official list prices. Their bet: reach far more cash-paying people at a lower price and lock in the market before rivals arrive.

The main worry

The central tension

The health system simply can't afford to cover everyone at $1,000 a month — so real access depends on prices falling hard. But every price cut squeezes the drugmakers' profits. The only way to reach everyone is cheap drugs, and cheap drugs threaten the business built on expensive ones.

The surprising part

The biggest barrier to these drugs isn't the science — it's the bill. The breakthrough already happened in the lab. What's rationing access now is a negotiation over who pays, at what price, for how many people. It's unusual precisely because it's solvable by price alone: drop the cost and use explodes. The whole future of the Ozempic era hinges not on a discovery, but on a fight over money.

In one sentence

Who pays, in a nutshell

The drugs are proven but expensive, so access is decided by insurers, employers, and government haggling over the bill — and the only path to reaching everyone (much cheaper drugs) is the same thing that threatens the makers' record profits.
