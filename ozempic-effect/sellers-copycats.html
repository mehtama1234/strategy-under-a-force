Group A · The makers & sellers

The sellers & copycats — a business built on a shortage

Telehealth firms and copycat pharmacies rode the boom by selling access — until the loophole slammed shut.

Not everyone in the Ozempic gold rush makes the drug. A whole industry sprang up selling access to it — quick online prescriptions and cheap knock-off copies — with online health company Hims & Hers the biggest name.

How the opening appeared

When the real drugs (Wegovy, Zepbound) ran short, a quirk of US law kicked in: pharmacies are allowed to make cheap "compounded" copies of a drug while it's in shortage. So copycat pharmacies churned out cheap versions (~$250–400 a month vs $1,000+), and telehealth firms sold them online with a fast virtual prescription. At its peak, the copy business was worth an estimated $1 billion — roughly a third of the whole market.

The twist: the shortage ended

The loophole closed almost overnight

In 2025 the FDA declared the shortages over — which made the cheap copies illegal again, with hard deadlines to stop. The bottom fell out of the copy business. Then Novo Nordisk, which had briefly partnered with Hims, publicly dumped it — accusing Hims of "deceptive promotion of knockoff versions" — and Hims's stock fell ~34% in a single day (about $10 billion gone).

The drugmakers took the channel back

Novo and Lilly then opened their own websites (LillyDirect, NovoCare) to sell the real drug directly, cheaply — a few hundred dollars a month in cash — squeezing the middlemen out entirely. Why go through a copycat when you can buy the genuine article direct for a similar price?

The main worry

These businesses were built on a shortage and a loophole that both closed. On top of that came a legal pile-on: Lilly and Novo sued copycat pharmacies, and regulators sent dozens of warning letters. A business model that depended on the drug being hard to get had no ground to stand on once it wasn't.

The surprising part

An entire billion-dollar industry existed only because the real drugs were in short supply — it was legal solely during the shortage. The moment the shortage ended, that business didn't just shrink, it became illegal and largely vanished. In a neat irony, the drugmakers' own success — finally making enough — is what destroyed the copycats. Hims lost most of its value from the peak; Lilly tripled its drug revenue.

In one sentence

The sellers & copycats in a nutshell

They got rich selling cheap access while the drugs were scarce — but the whole business was legal only because of the shortage, so when the makers caught up, the copycats collapsed and the drugmakers took the customers back.
