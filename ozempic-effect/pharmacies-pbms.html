Pharmacies & PBMs — the middlemen who pick your drug

The quiet companies between you and the drug decide which one you actually get.

Between the drugmaker and your medicine cabinet sit the pharmacies that fill the prescription and the "benefit managers" (PBMs) that decide which drug your insurance will pay for. The Ozempic boom flooded them with volume — and handed the PBMs enormous power.

The power move

A spreadsheet decides your medicine

Because Wegovy and Zepbound do nearly the same thing, whichever one a PBM marks "preferred" is the one millions of patients actually get. So the PBMs pit Novo against Lilly and pocket the difference. When CVS's PBM dropped Zepbound and made Wegovy preferred (July 2025), roughly 17× more patients got switched off their drug within a month — a coverage decision, not a medical one.

The catch: huge volume, tiny profit

Filling millions of $1,000 prescriptions sounds great, but the margins are thin or even negative on these pricey drugs. And pharmacies and PBMs get publicly blamed for high drug prices. It's a lot of money flowing through their pipes and not much of it sticking.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — being cut out

The drugmakers are now selling straight to patients (LillyDirect, NovoCare, for a few hundred dollars a month) and even contracting directly with employers — bypassing the middlemen entirely. If you can buy the drug direct, who needs the PBM? (The strain is already visible: Walgreens lost billions, closed ~500 stores, and went private.)

The surprising part

The single biggest factor in which weight-loss drug millions of Americans take isn't their doctor's judgment or the drug's quality — it's a secret rebate negotiation inside a benefit manager. When CVS simply picked one drug over the other on a spreadsheet, care for millions of people quietly changed overnight.

In one sentence

The middlemen in a nutshell

They handle a flood of expensive drugs, make little on each, and instead profit by auctioning "preferred" status to Novo or Lilly — while the drugmakers race to sell direct and cut them out.
