Soda & alcohol — the drugs quiet the craving itself

Coca-Cola and the big drinks makers face a threat aimed straight at desire.

Soda and alcohol companies (Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, Diageo, the beer giants) make money because people want sweet drinks and booze. Weight-loss drugs turn down that wanting — and not just for food.

The unexpected part: they curb drinking too

A threat nobody designed

These drugs act on the brain's reward circuit — the general "I want that" system — so they don't just dull hunger, they quiet cravings for alcohol as well. A 2026 trial found heavy-drinking days dropped far more on the drug than on a placebo, and a huge Swedish study found 36% fewer alcohol-related hospital visits. Surveys say most users also cut back on soda. A diabetes injection turned into one of the strangest threats ever to land on beer, wine, and spirits.

How they're responding

Toward zero-sugar sodas, tiny cans, and alcohol-free drinks: Coke Zero and Pepsi Zero grew nicely, mini cans are up, PepsiCo bought a "healthy soda" brand, and Diageo's and Anheuser-Busch's non-alcoholic ranges grew strongly. But be honest — most of this playbook predates the drugs; it's the same health-and-wellness pivot, now doubling as a GLP-1 hedge.

The honest nuance

It's genuinely hard to separate the "Ozempic effect" from a trend that was already building. Americans were drinking less and going sugar-free before these drugs went mainstream (the share of US adults who drink fell from 62% to 54%), driven a lot by younger people moderating. Drinks executives themselves blame weak household spending far more than the drugs, and one careful estimate puts the current drug-driven drag at only about −0.2% a year. The alcohol evidence is strong; the soda link is likely but not yet proven in a trial. So read GLP-1 as a real accelerant on a wave already rolling — not the whole story.

The surprising part

The mechanism is the giveaway: because the drugs work on the brain's general "wanting" system, they don't distinguish between a slice of cake, a soda, and a cocktail. That's bad news for companies whose entire catalog sits in exactly those three buckets — and it's an effect no one was aiming for when they set out to treat diabetes.

In one sentence

Soda & alcohol in a nutshell

The drugs chemically dampen the desire for sugar and booze — a rare threat aimed at wanting itself — landing on top of an already-cooling market, so the makers are racing into zero-sugar and alcohol-free while arguing about how much is really the drug.
