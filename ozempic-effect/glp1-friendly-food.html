Group C · Adapting & selling to the trend

"GLP-1 friendly" food — Big Food's counterattack

If people on the drugs eat less, sell them the little they still eat.

The food giants can't stop the drugs. So instead of only bracing for the hit (see Big Food), they're going on offense: building high-protein, low-sugar "companion" food aimed straight at the tens of millions of people now eating differently.

Why there's a new niche

People on these drugs eat about 20% fewer calories, skip snacks, and crave protein. And because eating too little makes you lose muscle, users worry about protein and nutrients. That fear created a brand-new shelf to sell to.

The products (everyone jumped in)

  • Abbott — Protality (Jan 2024): a high-protein weight-loss shake; the first big-brand response.
  • Nestlé — Vital Pursuit (2024): cheap, portion-controlled frozen meals; added a "GLP-1 Friendly" label after customers asked.
  • Conagra — "On Track" badge (Jan 2025): slapped a "GLP-1 friendly" badge on 26 existing meals — the first to label it openly on the box.
  • Danone — Kate Farms: bought the company and sells $55 protein shakes; plus high-protein yogurt.
  • GNC: built a dedicated "GLP-1 support" section across 2,300+ stores.

The main worry

Is it a real market, or marketing?

Two red flags. First, churn: up to half of users quit the drugs within a year, so a product tied to active medication faces a shrinking base. Second, it's unclear people want special branded food versus just eating less — many brands still hedge with coded "high protein / muscle support" language, a sign they doubt the label sticks. And it's partly just offsetting their own losses elsewhere.

The surprising part

The Danone paradox

The same food giants the drugs were supposed to devastate are now trying to profit from them. The sharpest case is Danone: it bought a protein-shake company and now sells $55 shakes to the shrinking-appetite shopper — making money off the very behavior change that eats into its core grocery business. And a twist from the data: reportedly most buyers of "GLP-1 friendly" food aren't even on the drugs — the label just reads as "healthy," widening the market.

In one sentence

The fightback in a nutshell

Big Food is turning the drugs' downside (people eating less, losing muscle) into a premium protein-food business. The prize is huge if it lasts — but half of drug users quit within a year, so no one's sure it will.
