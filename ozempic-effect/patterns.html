The patterns

Line up every company one weight-loss drug touched, and the same handful of stories keep appearing.

Each page asks one question: what did the weight-loss-drug boom make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this business? Fifteen answers later, clear patterns emerge. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

One drug rippled far past medicine — into food, drinks, hospitals, retail, and a whole country — because it didn't just treat a disease. It changed what people want, what they need, and who has to pay — and every company got hit in a different spot.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

First isn't best — the inventor's crown is fragile

Discovering the market doesn't mean keeping it. The company that invented the category lost it to a rival with better chemistry and bigger factories.

Pattern 02

A pill did your whole job

The cleanest kills happen when one product does your exact thing, cheaper. A shot did what a diet program sold for 60 years — and a whole copycat industry existed only while the drug was scarce.

Pattern 03

The drug attacks desire itself

The rarest kind of threat: it doesn't compete with a product, it dampens the wanting. The same brain circuit that quiets hunger also quiets cravings for sugar and alcohol.

Pattern 04

The feared threat became a customer — if it complemented you

Some who braced for demand destruction found the opposite — the drug got approved to treat their condition, or their users became new buyers. Panic, then tailwind. The catch: it depends on whether the drug completes what you sell or replaces it. Same drug, opposite fates — ResMed's breathing masks thrived while Inspire's implant sank.

Pattern 05

Good medicine, bad business — slowly

When a drug keeps people healthy, it quietly shrinks the pool of sick customers. But slowly — and near-term it can even keep existing patients alive and paying longer.

dialysis & kidneys
Pattern 06

Sell the thing that's killing you

Again and again, the disrupted try to monetize their own disruptor — reselling the drug, or making food and services for the people the drug changed. Right pivot; often small, late, and painful.

Pattern 07

A side effect becomes a market

The drugs strip muscle along with fat — so protein, gyms, and even a new class of drugs sprang up to counter it. One medicine's flaw is another company's product line.

the muscle-loss economy
Pattern 08

The middleman decides, and the bill rules

Between the drug and the patient sit gatekeepers who quietly pick winners — and above it all hangs the question of who can afford the bill. Access is decided by money, not the lab.

Pattern 09

One champion can carry — and crush — a whole country

Lean hard enough on a single company and its good year is the nation's good year… and its bad year is the nation's bad year. Concentration cuts both ways.

The method travels

Same lens, different force

Notice the shapes here echo the AI collection: an inventor losing to a fast-follower, incumbents getting eaten, middlemen who decide, a side effect that becomes a market, one giant that moves a whole economy. That's the point — the way to read any big force is to ask what it made scarce, cheap, risky, or newly valuable, then group companies by their answer. Point it at AI or at Ozempic and the same kinds of stories fall out.
