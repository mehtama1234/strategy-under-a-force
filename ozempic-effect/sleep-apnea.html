Group D · Surprising medical ripples

Sleep apnea — the drug everyone feared became a friend

ResMed's stock got dumped on the drugs. Then the drugs turned into a tailwind.

Companies like ResMed and Inspire Medical sell the machines and implants that treat sleep apnea — a breathing disorder tightly linked to being overweight. So the fear was obvious: if the drugs make people thinner, won't fewer of them need the devices?

The fear

In 2023, investors sold off sleep-apnea stocks on exactly that bet — thinner people, less apnea, fewer machines. ResMed's share price took a real hit on the story.

The twist: it went the other way

From feared threat to tailwind

Two things flipped the story. First, in December 2024 Eli Lilly's Zepbound became the first drug ever approved to treat sleep apnea — but it only cut apnea events by roughly 55–70%, versus a breathing machine's 90%+, so it reads as a complement, not a replacement. Second, ResMed found the drugs were a net positive: its CEO called them a "once-in-a-generation demand-generation opportunity," noting nearly 2 million of its machine users were also on a weight-loss drug — and those patients were more likely to start and stick with the machine. More diagnosis, more device starts.

The main worry

The remaining long-term risk

Very long term, if severe obesity falls a lot, the most serious apnea cases could eventually decline. Near-term it's been a tailwind, but the story is still unfolding — a helpful drug that's also, in theory, chipping away at the root cause of the condition these companies treat.

The twist within the twist: not everyone won

ResMed thrived; Inspire cratered

Here's the part most people miss: the drugs were not good news for everyone in the field. ResMed (which sells the masks) benefited — the drug and the mask work together. But Inspire (which sells a surgical implant) got hammered, its stock down roughly 79% from its peak — because a pill that reduces apnea competes directly with an implant. Same drug, opposite outcomes, depending on whether it complements your product or replaces it.

The surprising part

The drug Wall Street was certain would kill the sleep-apnea business became its best salesman — getting approved for sleep apnea funneled more people into diagnosis and onto machines. The feared assassin turned into a lead generator. (Same shape as the diabetes-device story.) But the divergence is the real lesson: whether a disruptor helps or hurts you comes down to whether it complements what you sell or competes with it.

In one sentence

Sleep apnea in a nutshell

Feared as a casualty, it split in two: the mask makers turned the drug into a lead generator, while the implant maker got hurt because the pill competes with it. A reminder that a disruptor helps you or hurts you depending on whether it completes your product or replaces it.
