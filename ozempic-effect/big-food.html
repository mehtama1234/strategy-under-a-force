Group B · The ones getting eaten

Big Food — when customers eat less, literally

Nestlé, Mondelez, Kraft Heinz and the rest sell snacks and sweets. The drugs kill exactly that craving.

The big packaged-food companies make their best money on snacks, candy, cookies, chips, and sugary food — things people eat a lot of. Weight-loss drugs cut appetite by roughly a quarter, and they hit the urge for junk hardest. That's a direct threat.

The day the fear got real

The "Ozempic trade"

In October 2023, Walmart's US boss said its data showed people on these drugs buy "less units, slightly less calories." Food stocks dropped that same day (Mondelez down ~7%, PepsiCo ~5%). Investors started selling packaged-food shares on a simple bet: if people eat less, these companies sell less.

How they're fighting back

Two moves at once:

  • Make "drug-friendly" food. Smaller portions, more protein, less sugar — like Nestlé's "Vital Pursuit" line aimed at drug users.
  • Publicly shrug. CEOs call the threat tiny ("a rounding error over 10 years") — even as they quietly reformulate.

The honest nuance

Overblown for now, real for later. Only about one in six US adults uses these drugs today, so the actual sales dent so far is small. But the behavior change — permanently eating less, especially junk — is durable, not a fad. If drug use climbs past a quarter or a third of the population, the math turns genuinely serious. The worst part isn't just eating less — it's eating differently (protein up, treats down), which eats away at their most profitable products.

The surprising part

The "GLP-1 friendly" label mostly sells to people not on the drug — reportedly about 77% of Nestlé's Vital Pursuit buyers weren't drug users. "Healthy portion-controlled food" turned out to be aspirational for everyone, quietly growing the market. And notice the tell: the same executives who call the threat trivial in public are the ones reformulating fastest in private.

In one sentence

Big Food in a nutshell

Weight-loss drugs quietly attack demand for the snacks and sweets that make these companies the most money. It's small today, but growing — so they're racing to sell "drug-friendly" food while telling investors not to worry.
