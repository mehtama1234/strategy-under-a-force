Group A · The money behind the lawsuit

Patent trolls

Buy patents you'll never build anything with, then sue the companies that do — because defending costs more than settling.

Patent-assertion entities ("trolls") own patents they don't use, and make money by suing or threatening the companies that (allegedly) infringe. Litigation funders bankroll patent campaigns, and some funds buy patents specifically to litigate them. The economics work because defending a patent suit is so expensive that even weak claims often settle for a fraction of the defense cost — the threat itself is the revenue.

The core bet

The business is asymmetry: the troll risks little (contingency lawyers, funder money) while the defendant faces millions in legal fees even if it wins. So companies rationally pay to make weak claims go away, turning a dubious patent into a revenue stream.

A tax on making things

Once a few defendants pay, their concession signals the rest to settle too, converting a questionable patent into a reliable toll. Litigation funders weaponized this — deliberately buying patents to run enforcement campaigns, essentially imposing a tax on deploying technology. Reforms like the 2017 TC Heartland ruling (which curbed forum-shopping to plaintiff-friendly courts) trimmed the worst abuses, but the model endures.

Who ends up ahead

Patent-assertion entities and the funders behind them — collecting settlements far above the technology's real value.

Who's exposed

Operating companies that build products, forced to pay nuisance settlements rather than fight.

The single biggest worry

The core problem is that a defendant pays millions even when it wins, while the troll — funded by contingency lawyers and outside money — risks nothing. That makes settling weak claims the rational choice, and once it becomes routine, the patent system turns into a shakedown mechanism rather than a spur to invention.

The surprising part

Trolls wrap themselves in a pro-inventor story — and it's partly true, since some do return a share to the small inventors who sold them the patents. But that's a smokescreen: most settlements come not from willful infringement but from the sheer cost of defense. The real innovation was realizing that suing is more profitable than building — patent law turned on its head.

In one sentence

Patent trolls monetize legal uncertainty by weaponizing high defense costs — turning patents into shakedown tools worth far more than the technology or the patent's actual merit.
