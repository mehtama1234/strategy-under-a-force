Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who really benefits

Funder's cut, lawyer's third, admin fees — and the injured person, who bore the harm, is last in line for what's left.

When a funder takes a cut, the lawyer takes a contingency fee (often 33-40%), and admin costs pile up, the actual injured plaintiff can end up with a small slice of a big settlement. In mass torts especially, individual claimants sometimes receive a few thousand dollars while lawyers and funders make fortunes. The person who bore the harm is often the last to get paid and the least to benefit.

The core bet

A single settlement gets claimed several times over — funder cut, lawyer's contingency, admin fees — so the injured party frequently keeps only a fraction. Litigation funding financialized the lawsuit; the upside flows to investors and lawyers, and the plaintiff keeps the scraps.

What the numbers say

33-40%
the lawyer's contingency fee — on a $10M settlement, $3.3-4M before any funder cut or admin fees.
15-25%
a typical funder cut on top of the lawyer's fee, leaving the plaintiff perhaps 30-50% of the gross.
$5-35K
per-claimant payouts in big mass torts, while lead lawyers and administrators collectively take billions.
5-10%
"common-benefit" fees courts award lead counsel on the whole fund — a second lawyer payoff tier.
100%+
the effective interest some plaintiffs pay on consumer legal-funding advances — a desperation tax.

The injured net the least

The funder is not the plaintiff's agent — it's a creditor. Once it signs on, it owns the financial outcome and can pressure a quick settlement before full liability is established, because delay erodes its return. A lawyer facing a funder who's already recouped its money gets asked, "Why spend another $500K chasing 20% more?" — and the plaintiff, who could have waited, is pushed into a deal that meets the funder's hurdle rate, not their own loss.

Who ends up ahead

Funders, contingency lawyers, lead counsel, and settlement administrators — each taking a layer.

Who's exposed

The injured plaintiff, who bore the harm and keeps the smallest share after everyone's cut.

The single biggest worry

The conflict is structural: funder and lawyer may both want to settle at 60 cents on the dollar to lock in their money, even if the plaintiff could hold out for 85. The funder profits either way; the plaintiff loses the upside — and the person the lawsuit was nominally for has the least say.

The surprising part

In many mass torts, the plaintiff effectively pays an interest rate above 100% on the implicit loan the funder provided — $50K advanced, $150K taken from the settlement over two or three years. The plaintiff needed the money immediately for medical bills and lost wages, so they borrowed against their own future payout on terms a credit card would refuse. The funder's profit is the plaintiff's desperation.

In one sentence

Litigation funding turns a lawsuit into a financial asset whose upside flows to investors and lawyers — while the plaintiff who bore the injury is last to get paid and least to benefit.
