Group C · The engines & players

Nuclear verdicts

Juries started handing out $10M, then $100M, then billion-dollar awards — driven by anger, not always by injury.

Jury awards have exploded. "Nuclear verdicts" (over $10M) and even "thermonuclear" ones (over $100M or $1B) are rising fast, driven by anti-corporate sentiment, sophisticated plaintiff tactics, and litigation funding raising the stakes. Insurers call the broad rise in claim costs "social inflation." Bigger payoffs make lawsuits more valuable as assets — and make liability insurance more expensive for everyone.

The core bet

Awards are climbing far faster than actual injuries or inflation, so a single case can pay off enormously. That makes lawsuits worth financing and defending at huge cost — and turns liability coverage into an expensive, risky game.

Anger, not injury

The system is self-reinforcing in a bad way: funders see nuclear verdicts in the headlines and fund riskier, higher-stakes cases; plaintiff attorneys use video, psychology, and "reptile theory" to make juries viscerally angry at corporations; judges often won't cap awards; insurers raise premiums or exit — and when honest businesses can't afford coverage, standards slip.

Who ends up ahead

Plaintiff firms and funders chasing ever-larger payoffs — and the whole litigation-finance apparatus around them.

Who's exposed

Corporate defendants, their liability insurers, and small businesses priced out of affordable coverage.

The single biggest worry

The rise self-reinforces: bigger headline verdicts attract more funding, which funds more aggressive cases, which produces more big verdicts. Insurers treat it as a social problem (jury attitudes, media, tactics) rather than a claims problem — so raising premiums doesn't fix it, it just makes liability insurance look like a rigged game.

The surprising part

The startling part is that nuclear verdicts often aren't about new evidence or law — they're about jury anger divorced from actual harm. A burn case with $2M of medical damages can draw a $50M award because the courtroom was framed to trigger outrage. Plaintiff lawyers literally teach seminars on making juries angry, and it works.

In one sentence

Juries are handing out ever-bigger awards to punish corporate behavior more than to compensate injury — turning litigation into a social-revenge mechanism, and lawsuits into assets worth financing at enormous cost.
