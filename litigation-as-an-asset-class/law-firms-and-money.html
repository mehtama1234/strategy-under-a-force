Law firms & capital

For a century only lawyers could own a law firm. That wall is cracking — and private equity smells a portfolio business.

Traditionally, outside investors couldn't own law firms (rules banned non-lawyer ownership). That's cracking: Arizona and Utah dropped the ban in 2020, private equity is moving into legal services, contingency-fee plaintiff firms already run like investment portfolios of cases, and litigation funding lets firms finance growth and de-risk. Outside capital is betting that a law firm is really a portfolio-of-revenue business, not a professional guild.

The core bet

Rule changes cracked the attorney-ownership monopoly, and outside money sees law firms — especially contingency plaintiff shops with predictable, financeable case outcomes — as scalable, acquirable profit centers.

What the numbers say

Duty shifts from the client to the investor

The real wedge wasn't the law change — it's that plaintiff firms were already running on financial engineering: case screening, portfolio construction, borrowing against future settlements, and exit timing. Private equity simply arrived with spreadsheets and capital. And because most plaintiff partners had no way to sell the firm before, the first buyout offer looks like a lottery ticket — so this is founder buyouts, not hostile takeovers.

Who ends up ahead

Plaintiff-firm founders who cash out, and the PE and Big-Four capital that scales the business.

Who's exposed

Clients, if the firm's duty shifts from zealous advocacy toward servicing investor returns.

The single biggest worry

Outside money cares about return on investment, not "zealous advocacy." When a funder or PE firm owns the upside on a case load, the pressure is toward fast settlements that cash out the investor and away from turning down lucrative-but-weak cases. Regulators wrote the ethics rules assuming lawyers owned lawyers, so there's little machinery to police the new conflict.

The surprising part

The surprise is that PE didn't have to change how good plaintiff firms work — it just recognized them as investment shops that had been improvising the playbook by instinct. Case triage, leverage, exit timing: all the venture-capital moves, done without spreadsheets. Finance arrived to formalize it — and to buy in.

In one sentence

Outside capital entered law by funding what plaintiff firms already did — treating cases like a portfolio — making client-first advocacy scarcer, fast settlements cheaper, and the attorney-client duty newly at risk.
