Group C · The engines & players

Legal tech & roll-ups

AI killed the junior-lawyer rung and made processing a case cheap — so the litigation machine can run bigger and faster.

As law becomes a business with outside capital, it also gets industrialized. Alternative legal-service providers (ALSPs) and the Big Four do commoditized legal work at scale; AI (Harvey, CoCounsel, e-discovery tools) automates research, review, and drafting; and consumer platforms (LegalZoom) productize routine law. Lowering the cost of processing a case is exactly what lets the mass-tort and litigation-asset machine run bigger and cheaper.

The core bet

Law was a bespoke, human-intensive service where attorney time was the scarce resource. Automation handles the repetitive parts, volume economics push work to cheaper labor and software, and outside capital funds it — so the litigation machine gets a hardware upgrade.

Throughput over justice

The volume model creates a perverse incentive: the cheaper a case is to process, the more you can file, and the more you can win by attrition rather than merit. AI in discovery was supposed to level the field, but it concentrates advantage in firms that can afford the infrastructure — firms increasingly owned by roll-ups and private equity, not solo practitioners.

Who ends up ahead

Roll-up firms and funders that can afford the tooling — and the AI vendors selling it.

Who's exposed

Junior lawyers (the disappearing rung), solo practitioners, and defendants swamped by high-volume filings.

The single biggest worry

The system optimizes for throughput, not justice: the real money is no longer in winning hard cases but in processing many fast, settling en masse, and extracting value from the settlement. Cheaper processing doesn't lower litigation rates — it lowers the barrier to initiating litigation.

The surprising part

AI didn't kill lawyers — it killed the junior-lawyer rung. The top partners and the cheapest automated workflows both got richer; the middle (the 5-8-year associate grunt work) vanished. And because per-case cost collapsed, mass torts that used to be marginal became industrialized production lines — you can afford to sue for smaller damages when a case costs $500 to run, not $5,000.

In one sentence

Industrialization made law cheaper to process, which turned litigation into a volume business that attracts outside capital — so the system now runs more cases through faster, not better.
