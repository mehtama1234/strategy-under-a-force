Group B · New asset classes from disputes

The claim as an asset

Once a lawsuit can be funded, it can be valued, bundled into a portfolio, borrowed against, and sold — like a bond.

Once a lawsuit can be funded, it can be treated like a security. Funders build diversified portfolios of cases so a few big wins cover many losses; they sell stakes to other investors, borrow against expected verdicts, and there's a secondary market for claims (buying a share of someone's judgment or a bankruptcy claim). Burford even securitized part of its portfolio. A one-shot bet became a tradeable financial instrument.

The core bet

Pool enough cases and a lawsuit stops being a binary win-or-lose gamble and becomes a predictable cash flow you can price, package, and trade — cheap capital for plaintiffs, returns for patient investors, and a market where a judgment can be cashed out early.

The court doesn't care who funded it

The catch is that the courts care who wins, not who funded it — and funders have almost no control once a case is filed. Judgments get appealed, reversed, or trimmed in ways no bond prospectus predicts. If litigation finance starts visibly shaping how cases settle — pushing to trial or settling early to lock a return — the legitimacy of the whole court system comes into question.

Who ends up ahead

Funders, their investors, and plaintiffs who get cheap capital — plus anyone who can buy or sell a claim mid-case.

Who's exposed

Investors exposed to appeal risk they can't hedge, and a court system whose integrity gets quietly financialized.

The single biggest worry

A pending case is a strange asset: illiquid, binary, and decided by judges and juries who behave unpredictably and change their minds on appeal. There's no deep secondary market to dump a souring case, so a string of high-profile funder-influenced outcomes could trigger regulation that makes the whole market untenable.

The surprising part

The surprise is that lawyers were the last to embrace it. They hated ceding control of strategy or settlement to a funder — yet by 2018-20 major firms folded litigation funding into their budgets as a standard cost line, alongside malpractice insurance. The funders quietly became utilities: lawyers kept control while off-loading the risk, which normalized the whole asset class faster than any sales pitch.

In one sentence

Once you can pool cases and securitize expected verdicts, a lawsuit stops being a one-shot bet and becomes a bond — cheap capital for plaintiffs, returns for investors, and a market where a judgment can be sold before it's even final.
