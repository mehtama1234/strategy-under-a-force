Group C · The engines & players

Litigation-risk insurance

Insurers started wrapping the lawsuit itself — protecting a verdict against appeal — and some policies blew up spectacularly.

A newer corner: insurers now sell products that wrap around litigation. "Judgment preservation insurance" protects a plaintiff's big verdict against being cut on appeal; "adverse judgment" and "after-the-event" policies cover the losing side's costs. This lets funders and companies lock in or hedge the value of a court result — turning an outcome into an insurable, financeable asset. Several big judgment-preservation policies then blew up.

The core bet

Insurance moved litigation from a binary win-or-lose event into a financial instrument with pricing, risk transfer, and leverage. A funder can keep the upside of a verdict and sell the appeal risk to an insurer — until the insurer discovers it mispriced the bet.

Adverse selection on steroids

The fatal flaw: the funder or plaintiff's lawyer knows far more than the insurer about the case's real strength and appeal odds. Insurers priced these policies as if insuring random verdicts, but they were actually insuring cases that sophisticated funders had already cherry-picked as high-value bets — so claims came in far worse than the actuarial tables suggested.

Who ends up ahead

Funders who hedge their portfolios and lock in returns — while the risk is priced cheaply.

Who's exposed

The insurers who underwrote it — several took catastrophic hits and pulled back — and honest pricing overall.

The single biggest worry

Litigation doesn't follow actuarial tables — it's decided by judges and juries who behave unpredictably and reverse on appeal. What looked like a manageable tail risk in 2010 became a catastrophe by 2020, because the cases actually being insured were the high-stakes ones where appeals are most aggressive and $100M+ reversals are possible.

The surprising part

Some funders were accused of using the insurance not as a hedge but as leverage: a policy backing a verdict made it feel "safer" to opposing counsel, who then paid more to settle — turning the insurance into a lawsuit-pressure tool rather than genuine risk transfer. A few judgment-preservation policies became as controversial as the settlements they were meant to protect.

In one sentence

Insurance made court outcomes tradeable and leverageable — but insurers priced random appeal risk while actually insuring cherry-picked high-stakes cases, so a natural-looking hedge blew up in their faces.
