Group B · New asset classes from disputes

Suing countries

Funders bankroll companies suing whole nations for billions — decided by private tribunals, invisible to voters.

A specialized, lucrative corner: funders bankroll investor-state disputes (ISDS), where a company sues a country for billions over expropriation, a canceled project, or a policy change — decided by private arbitration panels, not public courts. Winning awards against sovereigns are traded and enforced worldwide by hunting the country's assets abroad. Examples run from the ~$50B Yukos award against Russia to Burford's ~$16B YPF case against Argentina.

The core bet

Private funders finance claims against governments; the resulting awards become tradeable financial assets — bought, sold, and enforced globally through asset seizure. Sovereign liability itself became an asset class worth hundreds of billions.

What the numbers say

~$50B

~$16B

~1,200+

~$400B

chilling effect
documented policy retreats — tobacco, climate, mining rules — under the threat of ISDS claims, not just their cost.

Overriding public policy

Litigation finance turns sovereign immunity upside down: wealthy investors can sue countries in private tribunals over almost any policy they claim hurt their profits, and win enough to strain a small economy. A funder can buy a claim cheaply, extract a large award, then hunt central-bank reserves, real estate, and airline inventory abroad — while the tribunal answers to no voters and weighs no public interest.

Who ends up ahead

Funders, investors, and claimants who can finance and enforce awards against governments.

Who's exposed

Governments (especially poorer ones) and the citizens whose public policies get chilled or whose treasuries get raided.

The single biggest worry

ISDS lets globally mobile capital effectively tax immobile citizens: a country that raises the minimum wage, bans a toxic chemical, or revokes a polluting concession can face a multi-billion-dollar claim from a panel of investment lawyers — with enforcement that's global even though sovereignty is local, and proceedings that are largely secret.

The surprising part

ISDS wasn't built for this. It began as a Cold-War insurance policy so foreign investors wouldn't face midnight seizures in developing countries. Over 40 years, tribunals read "fair and equitable treatment" so broadly that they overturned rent controls, mining laws, and health rules on the theory that any regulation "frustrates investor expectations" — and funders realized a government's "no" could be reframed as a breach worth billions.

In one sentence

Litigation funding turned distant sovereign immunity into a traded security — letting global investors override governments' policy choices and hunt public treasuries, largely invisible to voters.
