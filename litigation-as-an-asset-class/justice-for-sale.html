Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Justice for sale?

It genuinely lets the little guy sue the giant — and it lets anonymous money decide which grievances get heard.

The honest, two-sided debate. On one side, litigation funding genuinely widens access to justice: an ordinary person or small company can now take on a deep-pocketed defendant they could never have afforded to sue. On the other, critics warn it turns courts into a casino, encourages weak or extortionate suits, lets anonymous outside money (even foreign governments) drive litigation, and pushes settlements for financial rather than just reasons — with a live fight over whether funders must be disclosed.

The core bet

Litigation funding lets the underdog fight the giant — but it also means someone with money decides which grievances are worth litigating, whether the plaintiff wins matters less than the settlement size, and no one may even know who's really driving the suit.

Access and financialization are the same coin

The most honest version: litigation funding is working exactly as designed — "access to justice" and "financializing claims" are two sides of one coin, not opposites. A $5M patent suit only a funded plaintiff could bring is access to justice for the innovator who got ripped off. But it's also a financial product: the funder is buying a piece of a lawsuit the way they'd buy a bond, and the plaintiff is now an asset under management.

Who ends up ahead

Under-resourced plaintiffs who finally get their day in court — and the funders who profit from it.

Who's exposed

Defendants facing an invisible, deep-pocketed opponent — and public trust when nobody can see the real party.

The single biggest worry

The core anxiety is whose interests get a seat at the table. Once a funder enters, the defendant faces a business whose profit depends on the outcome — invisible to the court, unaccountable to the public, and maybe pursuing commercial or geopolitical aims unrelated to justice. If the real funder is a foreign government or a shell company, plaintiff and defendant may both be pawns in a game they don't understand.

The surprising part

The countries that faced this honestly (the UK, Australia) chose mandatory disclosure — the logic being: if justice is going to be financialized, the court gets to see the balance sheet. The US is still trying to have secret funding without that compromise, which is exactly why the fight over disclosure is so heated.

In one sentence

Litigation funding solved rich-versus-poor in court but created a new problem: anonymous outside money now decides which cases matter, and courts often can't see who's really pulling the strings.
