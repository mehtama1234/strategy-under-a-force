Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Uncorrelated returns

Whether Burford wins a case has nothing to do with the S&P 500 — and that, not justice, is why the money poured in.

Here's the core reason Wall Street fell in love with litigation: a court judgment doesn't move with the stock market or the economy. Whether a funder wins a case has nothing to do with whether stocks are up or down, so litigation returns are "uncorrelated" — the holy grail for pension funds and endowments seeking diversification, especially when stocks and bonds fall together. It was financial logic, not justice, that drove the money in.

The core bet

When stocks and bonds move together (as in 2022), traditional diversification collapses. Litigation outcomes depend on case facts and courtroom arguments, not Fed policy — so funds marketed lawsuits as a hedge that sits almost entirely outside the macro economy.

Finance, not justice, drove it

Wall Street's romance with litigation isn't really about fairness — it's portfolio mathematics. A pension trustee doesn't care whether the case is a patent troll squeezing a startup or a firm recovering stolen secrets; they care that the return is ~25% and the correlation is zero. If a verdict can be priced, packaged, and bundled like a mortgage or a bond, it becomes investable.

Who ends up ahead

Pension funds, endowments, and hedge funds that finally found a return uncorrelated with everything else.

Who's exposed

Ordinary plaintiffs with small, certain claims, which get abandoned in favor of the biggest, riskiest bets.

The single biggest worry

The returns depend entirely on case selection and legal outcomes — hard to forecast and prone to concentration. A portfolio weighted toward a few mega-cases can collapse if they settle low or lose; there's no liquid market to offload a bad case mid-stream; and legal risk is fat-tailed, so a single catastrophic loss can erase years of wins. It's not correlated to markets — it's uncorrelated to everything, even itself.

The surprising part

The financial logic quietly distorts which cases get funded. Because the funder's expected return rises with the size and uncertainty of a suit, capital flows to the biggest, riskiest bets and away from small, certain wins for ordinary people — so "access to justice" tilts toward whatever maximizes IRR, not whatever's most deserving.

In one sentence

Litigation became an asset class because court outcomes are the last thing that doesn't move with stocks and bonds — and billions flowed in chasing a return pension funds couldn't find anywhere else.
