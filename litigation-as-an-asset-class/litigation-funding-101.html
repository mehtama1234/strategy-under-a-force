Group A · The money behind the lawsuit

Litigation funding 101

An outside investor pays your legal bills — and if you win, they take a cut; if you lose, they eat the loss.

Third-party litigation funders — Burford Capital, Omni Bridgeway, Longford — pay a plaintiff's or law firm's legal bills in exchange for a slice of any settlement or verdict, and get nothing if the case loses. That "non-recourse" structure turned a lawsuit into an investment. Burford is publicly traded and became the industry's face; the model grew from almost nothing into a multi-billion-dollar business.

The core bet

A funder makes a non-recourse bet that someone else's lawsuit will win big enough to return several times the money put in. It turned litigation into a tradeable asset — cheaper to sue, riskier to be sued, and far more valuable to hold a case funders think will win.

It inverted the asymmetry of legal warfare

For centuries a rich defendant could simply outlast a poorer plaintiff by grinding through legal fees. A funder erases that overnight, bankrolling a plaintiff's dream team to match the deep pocket across the table. That sounds like justice — and often is — but it also means litigation became an asset class where capital, not merit, decides which cases get fought.

Who ends up ahead

Funders and their investors — earning uncorrelated, double-digit returns on winnable cases.

Who's exposed

Defendants (now facing funded opponents) and, sometimes, the plaintiff whose case gets steered for the funder's return.

The single biggest worry

The funder's incentives don't perfectly match the plaintiff's. Funders profit when settlements grow, which can push cases to drag on; and with scale and capital, funders increasingly influence strategy and settlement decisions in ways that favor returns over the client's fastest, best outcome. A plaintiff who mortgages the lawsuit loses negotiating power.

The surprising part

Litigation funding didn't just make suing cheaper — it scales all the way up. Burford's returns came not from tiny injured individuals but from massive corporate and sovereign disputes; the $16B YPF award was effectively a nation-state score. The courthouse became a bond market, and the biggest bets are geopolitical.

In one sentence

Litigation funding turned the courthouse into a bond market — letting investors bet on lawsuits like securities, which made it cheaper to sue, riskier to be sued, and valuable to hold a case a funder likes.
