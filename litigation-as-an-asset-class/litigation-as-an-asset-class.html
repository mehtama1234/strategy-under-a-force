Group E · The big picture

Litigation as an asset class

Because a verdict doesn't move with the market, Wall Street turned the courthouse into a portfolio.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one idea explains them all. The lawsuit became an investable financial asset. Because a court judgment doesn't move with the stock market, its payoff is "uncorrelated" — catnip for investors who need diversification. So a multi-billion-dollar litigation-funding industry grew up (Burford the standard-bearer), mass torts got industrialized like assembly lines, claims got packaged and traded like securities, insurers wrapped the outcomes, and even law firms took outside capital.

The core bet

This turned the courtroom into a casino where "will this pay?" matters more than "is this right?" — and it's working exactly as designed. It genuinely widens access to justice for some, while rewarding funders and lawyers over plaintiffs and letting anonymous money decide which cases get heard.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Litigation funders (Burford and peers) and their investors earning uncorrelated returns; large firms taking outside capital; patent and mass-tort operators; and sophisticated plaintiffs who negotiate good terms.

Losers

Individual claimants who net a fraction after fees; defendants outspent by funded opponents; governments facing sovereign claims; smaller firms without capital; and public trust in a court system going opaque.

The single biggest worry

The biggest tension is that litigation finance simultaneously broadens access to justice — a person without $500,000 can finally sue a corporation because a funder fronts the cost — while turning the courtroom into a place where return-on-investment is king. A plaintiff who'd have won $10M might keep $4M after the funder takes 40% and the lawyer takes 30%. Meanwhile the funder chooses which cases to back, which defendants to sue, and when to settle — based not on the merits or the client's best interest, but on projected IRR. The justice system becomes a product line, and plaintiffs become revenue streams.

The surprising part

The most surprising part is that litigation finance doesn't just make existing cases bigger — it creates cases that wouldn't otherwise exist. Funders scout claims, advertise for plaintiffs, and aggregate them into mass torts, becoming partly the inventors of the disputes they fund. And it means anonymous, even foreign, capital now quietly owns chunks of court dockets: a pension plan, a hedge fund, or a Cayman shell can own the upside of your lawsuit — and the court rarely knows who's pulling the strings.

In one sentence

Litigation finance unlocked untapped legal claims and gave ordinary plaintiffs a shot at big paydays — at the price of letting Wall Street and anonymous funders, not judges or juries or plaintiffs, decide what gets litigated and who profits from justice.
