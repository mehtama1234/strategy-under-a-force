Group B · The new land grab

Warehouses

E-commerce turned the boring warehouse into Amazon's biggest landlord.

Online shopping needs a vast physical backbone, and that made the unglamorous warehouse one of the hottest real-estate classes on earth. Prologis became a giant REIT by owning the distribution centers Amazon and everyone else depend on — especially scarce "last-mile" sites near cities. It sells nothing and touches no inventory; it just owns the land the whole machine runs on.

The core bet

The bet is that e-commerce is a real-estate business in disguise. Fast delivery needs dense, urban land near customers — and cities have very little of it. Own that scarce industrial land and you profit from the entire online-shopping boom without ever selling a product.

The physical internet

Every one-day delivery promise rests on a building near you full of goods. There's only so much land close to cities zoned for logistics, so whoever owns it holds a chokepoint. The "internet" of e-commerce turns out to run on very physical, very scarce dirt.

What's newly valuable
Last-mile urban warehouse land, long leases to blue-chip tenants, and a tight supply pipeline.
What could soften it
An e-commerce slowdown, or automation and new delivery models that need less physical space.
The single biggest worry

The moat is tied to e-commerce growth and cheap capital. If online shopping stalls — or if rates and construction costs squeeze development returns — the warehouse boom cools with it.

The surprising part

The warehouse went from afterthought to one of the hottest asset classes because online shopping created an impossible constraint: you need dense land near customers, and cities barely have any. Prologis invented nothing and sells nothing — it just owns the irreplaceable land that makes the whole e-commerce machine work.

In one sentence

Prologis quietly became a ~$100B+ REIT not by building products, but by owning the scarce urban land e-commerce can't run without.
