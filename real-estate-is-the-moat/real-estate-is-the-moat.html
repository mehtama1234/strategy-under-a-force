Group E · The big picture

Real estate is the moat

The company isn't the business. The land is.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one pattern repeats: many companies that look like operators — a burger chain, a casino, a retailer, a phone carrier, a cloud provider — are really real-estate plays. The durable moat is owning the ground everyone else must stand on. It's hard to replicate, it throws off recurring cash, and it appreciates. The engine is REITs and sale-leasebacks; the vulnerability is leverage and rates.

The one idea

The business is the front; the land is the asset. Be fair, though — it overstates the universal case. Not every company is secretly real estate (pure software and fintech genuinely aren't), and when owners over-financialize the land — PE sale-leasebacks, too much leverage — the moat can flip into a trap.

The whole force in numbers

~$120B
McDonald's real estate — it collects triple-net rent from most of its 43,000 franchisees, earning the bulk of profit as a landlord.
~$680B
Hyperscaler capex committed toward 2026 — much of it land, power, and buildings. Rural land near power has risen 300–500% since 2023.
1.3B sq ft
Prologis warehouse space — with Amazon its largest tenant, and new supply at a decade low.
$40–50B
VICI's value — casino REITs now own more valuable real estate than the operators own in equity.
~$1.8T
CRE debt maturing 2024–26 — the rate reckoning that turns leverage from tailwind to headwind.
Nov 2023
WeWork bankruptcy — proof the land is only a moat while people still want the space.

Winners vs losers

Winners
McDonald's, Prologis, Equinix/Digital Realty, cell-tower and net-lease REITs, hyperscalers grabbing power sites, landowners near the grid.
Losers
Over-leveraged casinos and retailers, office landlords, WeWork, and any operator that sold its land to aggressive PE and now can't carry the rent.
The biggest tension

When rates rise, the whole model can break: cap rates widen, leverage becomes unsustainable, refinancing spikes. Property-rich balance sheets flip into liabilities when the business weakens. And when capital markets reward real-estate yields, every operator is tempted to over-lever to extract cash — turning a structural advantage into a financial trap.

The surprising part

McDonald's isn't the exception — it's the purest version of a pattern that's now the default for capital-intensive operators. And the twist: cloud companies that began as pure software are among the fastest land-grabbers in America, buying rural acreage for data centers faster than developers build office parks. The most durable businesses own the platform and rent to everyone else.

In one sentence

Again and again, the company isn't the business — the land is; the operating story is a front for owning the ground everyone else has to rent.

How this connects to the rest of the series

The moat's engine and its danger both live in sibling collections. Its fragility is the end of cheap money — leverage that reverses with rates. Its consolidation habit (self-storage, and the PE that runs sale-leasebacks) is the roll-up. Owning the ground others must cross to do business rhymes with the toll-booth economy. And the data-center land rush is the physical side of the energy transition.
