Group A · The business was a front for the land

McDonald's

A real-estate company that happens to serve burgers.

The purest example of the whole theme. McDonald's makes much of its money as a landlord, not a burger seller. It owns the land and buildings under most of its franchised restaurants and collects rent — often marked up — plus a slice of each store's sales. The Big Mac is almost the bait; the durable moat is one of the largest property portfolios on earth.

The core bet

McDonald's bet, decades ago, that the money wasn't in food — it was in owning the ground the food is sold on. Put a rent-paying franchisee on McDonald's-owned land, collect base rent plus a percentage of sales, and you have a recurring income stream that holds up even when burger margins don't.

What the numbers say

~70%
Share of restaurant locations on land or buildings McDonald's owns or controls. Franchisees run the stores; they pay the parent rent.
~50–60%
Share of operating profit tied to real estate — rent and the percentage of sales. Food margins are thin; the land is the profit driver.
$40B+
Rough value of the property portfolio, often buried inside "Property & Equipment" on the balance sheet — one of the world's largest landowners.
1950s–70s
CFO Harry Sonneborn reframed the company: "We're in the real estate business, not the hamburger business." That shift unlocked the growth model.

Why it's so stable

A franchisee makes money only when the restaurant is busy — thin food margins, all the operating risk. McDonald's collects fixed base rent plus a percentage of sales, so its cash flow is far steadier than any single store's. That lets the parent cut prices, test menus, or ride out slow years while the rent keeps flowing.

What's newly valuable
The owned land, long 20-year leases with sales clauses, and rent that arrives whether or not the burgers sell.
The hidden dependency
The rent only flows if franchisees stay profitable enough to pay it.
The single biggest worry

If same-store sales collapse or franchisees fail en masse — a deep recession, a shift in eating habits — rent revenue shrinks and defaults rise. The model assumes the tenant stays healthy; a long downturn could force write-downs on the property itself.

The surprising part

The burger is close to a loss-leader. Franchisees often run on razor-thin food margins, making real money only if the store is busy — but McDonald's wins either way, collecting fixed rent plus a cut of sales. Analysts note the real-estate income alone could justify a valuation rivaling the whole company; the food almost rounds to zero.

In one sentence

McDonald's is a real-estate company that happens to serve burgers — not a burger company that happens to own real estate.
