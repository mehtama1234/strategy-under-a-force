Group A · The business was a front for the land

Casinos (VICI)

The dirt under the casino beat the dice on it.

Casino operators split themselves in two: an operating company that runs the gambling, and a REIT that owns the buildings and the Las Vegas Strip land — and collects rent. The land turned out to be the safer, more valuable half. VICI Properties now owns icons like Caesars Palace and the Venetian, and leases them back to the operators who used to own them.

The core bet

The bet: the real estate under a casino is worth more, and is steadier, than the gambling business on top of it. So operators sold the buildings to REITs for cash and rent them back — converting volatile casino revenue into predictable rent for the landlord, who ends up with the more durable business.

What the numbers say

2017
Caesars spun off VICI Properties, which owns Strip icons (Caesars Palace, the Venetian) and leases them back on 15-year deals worth hundreds of millions a year.
GLPI
Gaming and Leisure Properties (spun from Penn in 2013) and MGM's REIT deals put Bellagio, MGM Grand, and Mandalay Bay under REIT ownership with the operators paying rent.
~$40–50B
VICI's market value (2024) — the REITs now own more valuable real estate than the operating companies own in equity.
2020
During COVID shutdowns, casino operators bled losses while REIT rent kept flowing — the landlord got paid either way.

Rent beats revenue

Strip land appreciates on its own, largely independent of how any one casino performs — supply is finite. Operators kept low debt on the real estate (they sold it) and instead borrow against future gaming cash flow, which makes the REIT the safest layer of the whole capital stack. The steady part is the ground; the risky part is the gambling.

What's newly valuable
The finite Strip land, long triple-net leases, and rent that holds up even when the casino floor goes dark.
What's riskier
The operating company — leveraged against volatile gaming revenue, now a tenant on land it used to own.
The single biggest worry

If Las Vegas itself weakens — fewer visitors, a lasting shift away from the Strip — an operator that can't refinance its casino-cash-flow debt could struggle to pay rent, testing even the "safe" REIT layer.

The surprising part

The dirt under the casino is more durable than the gaming license or the brand. A century from now, whoever owns that corner of the Strip will still collect rent, while any single casino's edge erodes with new rivals and changing tastes. Operators discovered they could take sale-leaseback cash today and hand the boring certainty of Vegas land to a REIT.

In one sentence

Casino operators discovered the address was worth more than the dice — so they sold the land, rent it back, and the landlord got the safer business.
