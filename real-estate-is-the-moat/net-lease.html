Group C · Landlord of everything

Net-lease REITs

The invisible landlord behind thousands of ordinary shops.

Realty Income — "The Monthly Dividend Company" — owns thousands of single-tenant buildings leased to the likes of Walgreens, Dollar General, 7-Eleven, and FedEx. The trick is the triple-net lease: the tenant pays the rent plus the taxes, insurance, and upkeep. The landlord just collects a rising check. It's the purest "own the building under the store" business.

The core bet

The bet is that owning the dirt under ordinary shops beats owning the companies inside them. Sign long triple-net leases, diversify across thousands of properties, and collect predictable, escalating rent with almost none of the landlord's usual headaches.

The tenant takes the risk

A triple-net lease flips the usual risk: the tenant absorbs operating costs and market swings, while the landlord is more like a bondholder with a building as collateral. That's why the "boring" rent stream can beat flashier investments — buyers starved for certainty pay a premium for rent that rises on schedule regardless of whether the store thrives.

What's newly valuable
Long triple-net leases, tenant-paid costs, diversification, and predictable escalating rent that reads like a bond.
What's fragile
Single-tenant buildings are hard to re-lease fast if the tenant fails — one big bankruptcy hurts.
The single biggest worry

Retail decay. E-commerce and tenant bankruptcies (Bed Bath & Beyond, Rite Aid) flow up to the landlord — and a single-tenant box built for one 50-year tenant can't be quickly re-leased or redeveloped if that tenant stumbles.

The surprising part

The business works because retail is old-fashioned and fragmented. There's no landlord who can hold Walmart hostage — but there are thousands of small Dollar General stores where the monthly rent check is sacred. Realty Income's steady dividend has beaten the market in many years precisely by being boring.

In one sentence

Net-lease REITs make steady money as the invisible landlord behind thousands of ordinary shops — collecting rent that rises on schedule whether the tenant thrives or withers.
