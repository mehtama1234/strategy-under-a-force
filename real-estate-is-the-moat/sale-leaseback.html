Group D · The mechanism & the limits

Sale-leaseback

Unlock the hidden land value — and turn an owner into a renter forever.

Sale-leaseback is the financial move at the heart of this whole theme. A company sells the real estate it owns — to a REIT or private-equity firm — for a lump of cash, then leases it back and keeps operating there. It unlocks the trapped land value on the balance sheet. But the trade is permanent: you stop owning, and your occupancy becomes a fixed rent you owe a landlord you no longer control.

The core bet

The bet is that land value sitting idle on a balance sheet is worth more as cash today. Sell the buildings, keep operating, and redeploy the money into growth, debt paydown, or dividends. Done well, it recycles capital. Done badly, it strip-mines the business's most durable asset.

What the numbers say

~$14–100B
Annual US sale-leaseback volume — the market hit ~$14B in 2025 (up 18%), and ran far higher in peak years. It's now a core capital tool, not a niche one.
$2.7B
Kroger sold ~87 supermarkets to Realty Income (2018–20) and leases them back — cash for debt paydown, in exchange for 20+ years of rising rent.
Red Lobster
Its real estate was sold (~$900M) years before its 2024 troubles — stores later closed as lease costs became unbearable against falling sales.
PE playbook
Buy a company, sell its buildings to a co-owned REIT, pocket the cash as a distribution, and leave the operating business paying rent forever.

Smart tool or strip-mine?

The same move looks identical on the accounting whether it's healthy capital recycling or financial strip-mining. A growing company selling to fund expansion is using it well. A private-equity-owned business selling its buildings to fund a dividend is leaving a rent bomb disguised as a lease. The land doesn't disappear — it just moves to the REIT, where it appreciates while the tenant eats the rent.

When it's smart
Freeing trapped capital to fund real growth, at a fair rent the business can carry.
When it's predatory
PE sells the land, pockets the cash, and saddles the company with rent on ground it used to own.
The single biggest worry

If you sell the building and revenue later collapses, you're contractually trapped paying rent on property you no longer own — and landlords rarely renegotiate. The buyer keeps the land's appreciation while the tenant bleeds fixed rent on sinking sales.

The surprising part

Sale-leaseback is where the land gets financialized — and it can be genius or a debt bomb, with the same accounting either way. It's the exact mechanism that turned the moat (owned land) into a millstone (a lease that can outlast the business) in retailer after retailer. It's the hinge between this collection's upside and its danger.

In one sentence

Sale-leaseback turns land — your cheapest, most permanent asset — into cash today and a rent obligation that can outlive the business itself.
