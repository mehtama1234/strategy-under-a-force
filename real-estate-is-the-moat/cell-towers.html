Group B · The new land grab

Cell towers

The phone carriers you pay are just tenants on rented steel.

Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile largely don't own the towers their networks run on — they rent space on towers owned by REITs like American Tower and Crown Castle. One tower can host several carriers, so each extra tenant is almost pure profit. The real long-term winner of the mobile boom is the landlord who rents the same steel to three rivals at once.

The core bet

The bet is that owning the vertical real estate — the towers — beats owning the network. The heavy cost (land, steel, install) is sunk once; every additional carrier bolted onto the same tower is nearly all margin. So the tower company earns fatter, steadier returns than the carriers do.

What the numbers say

~40,000
Towers American Tower operates globally — it became the largest tower REIT, with revenue crossing $11B and margins around 70–75%.
2.0–2.5
Tenants per mature tower — one tower hosting multiple carriers. Each added tenant lands on top of a fixed cost base.
70–75%
Tower-REIT EBITDA margins — versus 30–40% for the carriers themselves. The landlord's business is the more profitable one.
2–3%
Built-in annual rent escalators, plus the 5G buildout (2018–23) that filled towers faster than new ones went up, tightening capacity.

A double real-estate play

The towers often sit on leased land, so the landowner collects ground rent, and the REIT collects carrier rent on top — a double-layered real-estate business. You call your carrier to complain about coverage, but the antenna on the pole belongs to a REIT, and your carrier is just renting the right to broadcast from it.

What's newly valuable
Multi-tenant towers, long escalating leases, and the near-pure-profit economics of adding tenants to existing steel.
What limits it
Once towers are full and 5G buildout slows, growth leans on inflation-tied rent bumps.
The single biggest worry

Saturation. When towers are fully tenanted and the buildout cycle ends, tower REITs face a revenue ceiling — and if carriers cut capex or share networks more efficiently, tenant growth stalls.

The surprising part

The networks you rely on are built on rented steel the carriers don't own. You pay Verizon; Verizon pays a Boston REIT (American Tower) and a Kansas City REIT (Crown Castle) for the right to hang antennas. A single tower can throw off steadier, more predictable money than the carriers' own infrastructure divisions.

In one sentence

The mobile networks you pay for run on rented steel owned by REITs — the carriers are tenants, and the landlord kept the better business.
