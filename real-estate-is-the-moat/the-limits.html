Group D · The mechanism & the limits

The limits

When the land turns toxic — empty offices and the WeWork bust.

The skeptical page. "Own the land and you own the moat" fails when the land is the wrong land at the wrong time. The clearest case is the office: remote work emptied towers just as rates spiked, and a mountain of prime downtown real estate turned from a safe asset into a liability. WeWork — which bet the whole company on leasing offices — is the cautionary tale.

The core bet — inverted

Every other page shows land as the durable asset. This one is the counterexample: land is only a moat if people still want to be on it. When demand for a category of space collapses, owning it becomes the fastest way to lose money — the moat fills with water.

When ownership is the trap

Owning real estate is only safe if the space stays useful. Offices show the reverse: fixed costs, falling rents, and buildings that are expensive to convert to housing. WeWork made it worse by signing long, fixed leases and renting them out short-term — it took all the downside of the land with none of the ownership upside, and the mismatch broke it.

Where the moat fails
Offices, weak malls, and any space demand abandons — where owning means paying to hold a depreciating, hard-to-repurpose asset.
What still holds
Land that can be repurposed (warehouses, data, housing) or sits where demand is genuinely scarce.
The single biggest worry

"It's all really real estate" tempts owners to over-invest in land and over-leverage it. But land is only a moat while people want to stand on it — and a demand shift plus a rate shock can turn the prized asset into the thing that sinks you.

The surprising part

The same instinct that makes real estate a moat — hold the scarce, durable ground — is what trapped office owners and WeWork. They believed the land would always be wanted. When it wasn't, the buildings didn't quietly hold value; they became expensive liabilities nobody could easily offload or convert.

In one sentence

Land is a moat only while people still want to be on it — empty offices and WeWork show how fast the prized asset can turn toxic.
