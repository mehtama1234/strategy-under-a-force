Group C · Landlord of everything

Farmland

Billionaires and pension funds who own the dirt but never touch a tractor.

Farmland became a prized asset for the wealthy and for big funds. Bill Gates is the largest private farmland owner in the US; pension funds and REITs pile in too. They don't farm it — they rent it to farmers and hold the land as a scarce, income-producing, inflation-proof asset. It raises a real question about who owns the ground that grows food.

The core bet

The bet is that productive farmland is finite, income-generating, and inflation-proof — a real asset that pays rent while appreciating. Unlike stocks, the land itself is scarce, and rising food prices tend to lift both the rent and the land value. You never need to know how to farm; you just own the dirt.

What the numbers say

Rent without the risk

Most institutional farmland is leased to the actual farmers who work it. The owner collects a 2–4% rent yield, indifferent to corn yields or cattle health, and waits for scarcity to lift the price. It's real-estate arbitrage: acquire the land (or hold it through decades of consolidation), raise the rent, and let finite supply do the rest.

What's newly valuable
Scarce, income-producing land as an inflation hedge — steady rent plus appreciation, with the farmer taking the operating risk.
The concern
Concentrated ownership of the land that grows food, and pricing power over the farmers who rent it.
The single biggest worry

If a handful of billionaires and funds own the dirt that grows the nation's food, they hold a chokepoint: farmers can't easily leave, rents can climb, and food costs rise when landlords do. Consolidation squeezes the family farm.

The surprising part

The wealthiest farmland owners never farm. Gates, pension funds, endowments — they're indifferent to the crop. They want the land to appreciate and pay rent, and they lease it to the people who actually work it. It's pure real-estate strategy dressed as agriculture.

In one sentence

Farmland became a moat for the wealthy because it's scarce, income-producing, and inflation-proof — and whoever owns the dirt owns the farmer's future.
