Group D · The mechanism & the limits

The rate reckoning

The same cheap money that built the moat is what makes it fragile.

Real estate is a leveraged, rate-sensitive game. A decade of near-zero interest rates (the 2010s) inflated property values and let owners borrow cheaply. The 2022–24 rate hikes flipped it: values fell, refinancing turned painful, and a wall of commercial-real-estate loans came due. The moat depends on cheap debt — and cheap debt went away. This is the real-estate face of the end of cheap money.

The core bet

The bet, mostly unspoken, was that low rates were permanent. Cheap debt amplifies real-estate returns — until it reverses. Then the very leverage that made owning land so powerful works in reverse, turning the moat into a trap.

What the numbers say

Leverage cuts both ways

A 3% mortgage turned a 9% property gain into a 12% return on equity. A 7% mortgage turns the same property into a negative real return. Real estate didn't get riskier because something broke — it got riskier because the one assumption it rested on, permanently cheap money, flipped, and leverage amplified the losses instead of the gains.

Who's exposed
Over-levered owners, office landlords, and anyone facing a 2025–26 refinancing at double the old rate.
Who's insulated
Low-debt REITs, long-fixed-rate borrowers, and cash buyers who can pick up distressed assets.
The single biggest worry

The maturity wall is concentrated in 2025–26, and many borrowers must inject 25–33% fresh equity just to avoid default — math that doesn't work when values fell and rents stalled. Wide refinancing gaps could trigger forced sales that push values down further.

The surprising part

The moat was always fragile because it depended entirely on cheap debt staying cheap. The leverage that made land such a powerful advantage is the exact thing that made it dangerous. "Safe" real estate suddenly faced a refinancing crunch — not because the buildings changed, but because the interest rate did.

In one sentence

The real-estate moat was built on the illusion that near-zero rates were permanent — and when they reversed, the leverage that created the moat became the trap.
