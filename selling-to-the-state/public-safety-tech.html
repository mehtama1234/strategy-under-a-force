Group B · The compliance-industrial complex

Public-safety tech

Give away the camera, sell the subscription forever — the playbook for police, courts, and prisons.

A large industry sells technology to police, courts, jails, and prisons: body cameras and evidence software, 911 dispatch, license-plate readers, court case-management, and prison phone networks. The buyers have stable, multi-year budgets and legal duties to keep records — and once a vendor becomes the standard, the data lock-in makes them nearly impossible to replace. The money isn't in selling hardware once; it's in charging subscriptions and per-transaction fees forever.

The core bet

Police, courts, and jails are permanent, well-funded buyers, and once your camera, software, or network is the standard, you own recurring revenue and the data lock-in that follows.

What the numbers say

~$40B
Axon's market cap; it went from selling stun-guns to cloud evidence software billed as a subscription to ~20,000 agencies.
~5,000
Poles carrying Flock Safety license-plate readers across U.S. cities since 2016, each on an annual fee.
$2–6/min
What prison-phone firms charged families for calls in the 2010s — $50–100+ a month for a few short calls.
0% → ~75%
Body-camera adoption by U.S. police, from near-zero in 2013 to about three-quarters by 2022 after Ferguson.
2015
The FCC began capping prison call rates — the kind of price control that threatens these models.

The hardware is the wedge; the software is the profit

Axon gave away or deeply discounted the cameras to lock departments into its evidence cloud — the razor-and-blades trick, aimed at government. In prisons the twist is darker: the customer (the government) doesn't pay; the inmates' families do, making a captive, regressive revenue stream out of people with no alternative.

The single biggest worry

Civil-liberties and surveillance backlash is the real threat — license-plate readers enable mass tracking, evidence clouds enable facial recognition, and reform movements or price caps (like the FCC on prison calls) can cut into the model or force customers to drop a vendor.

The surprising part

The revealing part is who actually pays. When the government buys, the vendor wins a sticky subscription; when the payer is an inmate's family, the same lock-in becomes a tax on the poorest people in the system. Either way the vendor's moat is the same — control the data and the standard, and the buyer can't leave.

In one sentence

Vendors to police, courts, and prisons give away the device to lock in the software, turning permanent public budgets — and sometimes captive families — into recurring revenue protected by data lock-in.
