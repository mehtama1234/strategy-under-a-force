Group A · The customer that never churns

The new defense tech

For 70 years, three primes got the money. Now startups sell drones and software straight to the Pentagon.

For decades, three companies — Lockheed, Boeing, Raytheon — took almost all the Pentagon's weapons money. Now, for the first time, the U.S. military buys software and drones directly from venture-backed startups. An ~$850 billion budget is opening to founders because software and autonomy don't follow the old cost-plus hardware rules that made the primes rich — and Ukraine and great-power competition lit the fuse.

The core bet

The biggest, most reliable customer on earth is finally open to buying from startups, not just the old primes — and software and autonomy are where the new entrants win.

What the numbers say

$850B+
The annual U.S. defense budget — the single largest buyer of weapons and services on earth.
~$14B
Anduril's 2024 valuation (later higher); it raised over $1.2B and sells drones and AI targeting software.
$100B+
Palantir's market cap; roughly half its revenue is government data-fusion software.
$5B+/yr
Venture money flowing into defense tech since 2022, surging after Russia invaded Ukraine.
3–7 yrs
The 'valley of death' — how long a working prototype can wait to become a funded program; many startups die in it.
10–15 yrs
How long a traditional weapons program takes from concept to deployment.

Software breaks the old 'bigger = pricier' rule

Hardware got more expensive the bigger it grew, which suited the primes. Software and autonomy don't: a $200M drone program can do the work of a $20B one if the AI is good enough. That's why Silicon Valley — which spent decades refusing military work — now races toward it, and why the primes can't simply out-startup a startup.

The single biggest worry

The procurement system was built for Lockheed and Boeing — clearances, bonding, factories, years of losses — so a 50-person startup can collapse waiting for a signature. And using AI in warfare draws real ethical and political backlash that can defund a whole segment overnight.

The surprising part

The cultural whiplash is the story: engineers once booed defense work and threatened to walk over drone AI; now the same founders and investors call it patriotic and profitable. Underneath the flag-waving is a genuine technical shift — algorithms, not airframes, are where the new money and the new leverage sit.

In one sentence

The U.S. military is shifting from buying hardware from three primes to buying software and autonomy from venture-backed startups, remaking who gets rich and who dies waiting for a contract.
