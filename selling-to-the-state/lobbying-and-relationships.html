Group C · Why it's hard — and why that's the moat

Lobbying & relationships

When your customer is the government, access is a product you can buy — and it pays like nothing else.

When the buyer is the government, part of 'selling' is influence: lobbying, campaign money, hiring ex-officials through the revolving door, and knowing the right people. It's a legal, multi-billion-dollar industry that shapes who wins contracts and which programs get funded. The product you're really buying is a seat at the table where the rules get written.

The core bet

When your customer is the government, relationships and influence are a competitive advantage you can buy — lobbyists, ex-officials, and access help shape the rules and the contracts in your favor.

The highest-return spend in the building

Dollar for dollar, lobbying can out-return almost any other corporate investment, because it can unlock contracts, tax breaks, and favorable rules worth many times its cost. Most companies don't think of it as buying a product — but a literal seat where amendments are drafted and contracts allocated is exactly what it is.

The single biggest worry

It carries real reputational and legal risk — corruption scandals, bribery probes, and public backlash when people see contracts flowing to access rather than merit. It can also entrench weak incumbents over better competitors, degrading what the public actually gets.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable part is how rational it is. Because the returns are so high, opting out is a competitive disadvantage — so the game pulls everyone in, and the result is a system where the best-connected, not necessarily the best, tend to win the public's money.

In one sentence

When government is the customer, access and relationships become a buyable product with an unusually high return — which is why the best-connected, not always the best, win the contracts.
