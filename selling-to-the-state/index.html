Selling to the State

Government is a miserable customer to win — and a wonderful one to keep. The pain of the sale is the moat.

The 39th force. The government is the biggest buyer in the economy, it never goes bankrupt, its budgets survive recessions, and it almost never switches vendors. Winning a public contract is slow, bureaucratic, and gated by certifications and procurement rules — but that pain is exactly what locks competitors out and keeps winners in for decades. A wave of govtech modernization, a defense-tech boom, and an ever-growing compliance machine turned "selling to the state" into some of the most durable money in business. The one idea: the barriers everyone complains about are the moat — endure them once and you become a near-permanent toll-taker on public money.

Seeded by the Inc. 5000 data "Regulated-Industry Software" is the 3rd-biggest fast-growing theme on the list — 634 companies, ~$33.7B represented — packed with firms selling software and services into government and regulated sectors. This collection follows that money.
The one question, asked of everyone

What did "selling to the state" make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? The ability to survive procurement and hold the certifications became scarce and valuable; the incumbent's revenue became almost churn-proof; and a great product without the paperwork became worthless. The danger: the same barriers that protect the incumbent lock out better competitors, harm the public when capture beats quality, and rest on politics that can cut a program — or reform the moat away — overnight.
