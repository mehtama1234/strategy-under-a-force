Group B · The compliance-industrial complex

Compliance software

The more rules there are, the more they sell — so complexity isn't a problem to fix, it's the market.

Every new law and regulation creates work — reporting, filings, audits, anti-money-laundering checks, data privacy. A whole industry of compliance software profits from that complexity: each rule Congress or a regulator adds is a new sales call and a recurring subscription. The market grows automatically as the regulatory state grows, and the customer — who resents the cost — has no choice but to pay.

The core bet

Rules only ever multiply, and each new one is a tax that compliance software helps companies pay. So the vendor's market expands automatically as regulation expands.

What the numbers say

$11–12B
Size of the global compliance/GRC software market (2023), growing 10–14% a year — about double the software-industry average.
$15–20B
What U.S. banks spend annually on anti-money-laundering and know-your-customer compliance — from one rule area alone.
450+
Significant new federal and state regulations added per year, each triggering fresh compliance work.
2018
Europe's GDPR privacy law took effect, instantly creating a billion-dollar-plus compliance subsector.
2020
California's CCPA and the wave of state privacy laws that followed launched another compliance boom.

A private tax-collection system that profits from red tape

The industry doesn't make rules simpler — it profits from their complexity. That quietly aligns a large business against simplification: a cleaner rulebook would shrink the market. Regulatory bloat isn't a problem these vendors want solved; it's their entire reason to exist.

The single biggest worry

The one thing that could crater demand is deregulation or genuine simplification — the model only works while rules keep piling up. And it's a cost center customers resent, so any chance to spend less on it, they take.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable insight: regulation accidentally created a private tax-collection industry. Every rule meant to protect the public also mints a recurring fee for a software vendor, and the vendor's incentive is for the rulebook to keep thickening — which is the opposite of what the public was promised.

In one sentence

Compliance software turns a law's complexity into a recurring subscription that grows with every new rule — so its business depends on red tape never getting simpler.
