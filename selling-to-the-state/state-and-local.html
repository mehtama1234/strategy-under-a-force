Group A · The customer that never churns

State & local

Ninety thousand separate governments — each tiny and hard to win, each loyal almost forever.

Beyond the federal government sit roughly 90,000 separate buyers — states, counties, cities, school districts, special districts — each with its own budget, procurement, and politics. Each one is hard to land and small on its own, but pays on time forever and rarely switches. The real prize is building a machine to sell to all of them cheaply; whoever does owns the stickiest revenue in business.

The core bet

There are tens of thousands of tiny government buyers, each hard to win but permanent once won. Whoever builds the machine to reach them all cheaply owns a huge, sticky, recession-resistant market.

Cooperative purchasing turns one win into hundreds

The magic trick is the 'cooperative contract': win once with a state or a big purchasing consortium, and hundreds of smaller towns can order your software off the shelf without a fresh bid. That flips the tiny-buyer, tiny-deal problem — one win cascades to 50–200 others, and the least glamorous customer (a county recorder, a water utility) turns out to be the most loyal.

The single biggest worry

Fragmentation makes selling expensive — no single contract covers two cities, and every government negotiates differently. Local budgets also swing with tax revenue and politics, so a downturn or a factory closure can cut IT spending before it touches payroll.

The surprising part

The counter-intuitive part is that the smallness is a feature. Each buyer is too small to attract big competitors and too risk-averse to ever switch, so a vendor that quietly wins the boring back office of thousands of towns builds a revenue base that's nearly impossible to dislodge — one dull county office at a time.

In one sentence

Ninety thousand tiny governments are each hard to win and permanent once won, so the vendor who can sell to them all cheaply — usually via cooperative contracts — owns the stickiest revenue there is.
