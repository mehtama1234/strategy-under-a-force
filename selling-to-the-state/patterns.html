The patterns

Line up every corner of selling to government — govtech, defense, benefits, compliance, procurement, certifications, lobbying — and the same handful of mechanics repeat, all flowing from one fact: the pain of the sale is the moat.

Each page asks: what did "selling to the state" make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions repeat.

The one thing to take away

Government is a miserable customer to win and a wonderful one to keep. Winning takes years of procurement, certifications, and relationships — but that pain is exactly what locks competitors out and keeps winners in for decades. So the scarce, valuable thing isn't a great product; it's the ability to endure the barriers. Whoever does becomes a near-permanent toll-taker on public money — and the same red tape everyone hates is precisely what makes the contracts worth billions.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

The barrier is the moat

Procurement, certifications, clearances — the things that make the sale agonizing are the same things that keep rivals out once you're in.

Pattern 02

Winning is hard; being fired is nearly impossible

Long contracts, high switching costs, and 80–90% renewal rates mean the revenue barely churns once you've landed it.

Pattern 03

The paperwork can be worth more than the product

A mediocre tool with FedRAMP beats a brilliant one without it, because agencies buy the certification as much as the software.

Pattern 04

The embedded incumbent wins, not the best product

Profit pools with whoever is already wired in — the integrator, the benefits contractor, the certified vendor — because ripping them out is unthinkable.

Pattern 05

Give away the wedge, sell the subscription

Discount the camera or the device, then own the recurring evidence-cloud or data subscription and the lock-in behind it.

Pattern 06

One win can cascade to hundreds

Cooperative purchasing lets a single contract spread to hundreds of small governments off the shelf — turning a tiny-deal market into scale.

cooperative contracts
Pattern 07

Access is a buyable input with a high return

Lobbying and the revolving door shape the rules and the contracts, and dollar for dollar out-return almost any other corporate spend.

the seat at the table
Pattern 08

The safest customer is the most volatile

The threat isn't a competitor — it's the sponsor changing the rules: a shutdown, a cost-cutting drive, or a reform that automates the moat away.
