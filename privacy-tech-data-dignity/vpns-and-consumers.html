VPNs & the privacy market

A multi-billion-dollar market sold on privacy — that mostly just moves your trust from your ISP to a company you know even less about.

Consumer VPNs (NordVPN, ExpressVPN, Surfshark) became a multi-billion-dollar market sold on privacy and security, and promoted relentlessly by YouTubers. But many are quietly owned by a few conglomerates, overstate what they protect, and don't so much give you privacy as relocate it — from your internet provider to the VPN company, which is often less regulated and harder to audit.

The core bet

The consumer VPN sold the promise that paying a company for encryption gives back the privacy the internet took. But it inverts the trust problem: instead of trusting your ISP with your traffic, you now trust a for-profit VPN with every incentive to log or monetize it.

You must trust the privacy seller

The paradox is baked in: to hide from your ISP you hand everything to the VPN, and if it keeps logs (many do despite "no-log" claims), sells data, gets hacked, or receives a government order, your whole browsing history is exposed — often with less legal friction than an ISP faces, because VPNs aren't regulated as carriers. Marketing claims like "zero logs" are largely unverifiable.

Who ends up ahead

VPN conglomerates and the influencers who sell them — packaging paranoia into a monthly subscription.

Who's exposed

Users who overestimate the protection, and whose traffic now flows through a less-accountable chokepoint.

The single biggest worry

The VPN becomes a single chokepoint that collects exactly what you were trying to hide. Unlike big tech, VPNs face no standard audit, no required transparency reports, and small penalties when caught lying — so a "privacy" purchase can quietly become a new, harder-to-see surveillance relationship.

The surprising part

The privacy-product market didn't create demand for privacy so much as commercialize an existing paranoia and amplify it with influencer marketing. Most users were never really at risk from ISP snooping (metadata, not content) — the VPN turned a niche business tool into a mass-market lifestyle product, selling the anxiety of digital paranoia as a $10-a-month subscription.

In one sentence

The VPN market succeeded by selling the feeling of privacy — while mostly replacing your ISP's visible surveillance with a new, less-regulated, harder-to-audit one.
