Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Data dignity

Your data trains their AI and targets their ads — so shouldn't you own it and get paid? The math says: it's worth pennies.

A movement argues that since your data is valuable — used to train AI and target ads — you should own it and be paid when others profit. The ideas: data dividends, data trusts and unions that bargain collectively, and browser tools that let you sell or withhold data (Jaron Lanier's "data dignity," Glen Weyl's "data as labor"). Critics say individual data is worth almost nothing, and that this "fix" just legitimizes selling privacy.

The core bet

The pitch: if your data is an asset with real value, you should own it, control it, and get compensated. It's an appealing answer to surveillance — treat data like labor. But it keeps foundering on a hard economic fact.

What the numbers say

Worthless alone, captured together

The fatal paradox: individual data is worth almost nothing in isolation — cents. It's only valuable aggregated across millions. So paying a "fair share" means either pennies too small to matter, or concentrating bargaining power in unions and platforms that take their own cut — recreating surveillance capitalism one layer up. Demand $100 for your data and the platform just trains on the 99% who opt in cheaply, or works around you.

Who ends up ahead

AI labs (who license from a few publishers, not billions of users) and any new gatekeeper the unions become.

Who's exposed

Individuals — whose data is worth pennies alone — and the dignity ideal, which risks normalizing selling privacy.

The single biggest worry

The scheme risks legitimizing the sale of privacy itself — turning a right into a transaction, and making "I sold my data" a normalized move for the poor while the wealthy still avoid surveillance. You need the platform far more than it needs you, so the leverage is inverted from the start.

The surprising part

The shock is that platforms have more incentive to route around you than to negotiate. Faced with data-scraping fears, Reddit and X didn't pay users — they locked their APIs and sold direct deals to AI labs, cutting out the person entirely. The most valuable data (your real-time attention, your social graph) is most valuable precisely when you don't know you're giving it up, so transparency and payment paradoxically destroy the value.

In one sentence

Data dignity foundered on math: individual data is worthless alone, collective bargaining just creates new gatekeepers, and platforms would rather scrape or automate you out of the deal than pay you.
