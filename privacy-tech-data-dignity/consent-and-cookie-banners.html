Group B · The compliance & deletion industry

Cookie-banner theater

A law meant to protect privacy gave us a billion pop-ups engineered to make you click "Accept All."

GDPR and ePrivacy rules mean nearly every website now throws a cookie-consent pop-up at you. In practice these became theater: designed with dark patterns to nudge you to "Accept All" and bury the reject option, so people click yes just to make it go away. A "consent management platform" industry (OneTrust, Cookiebot, Usercentrics) sells the banners — and regulators are now cracking down on the very designs they enabled.

The core bet

The law required consent for tracking, so sites had to ask; that created a market to automate the asking. But the design quickly became a permission-washing machine — nested menus, tiny reject buttons, sticky footers — that makes informed refusal nearly impossible.

Rejection got harder on purpose

Over time the banners made refusal harder, not easier — smaller fonts, deeper menus, auto-checked boxes — because "just make it annoying to refuse" works. That's not a bug; it's the consent-platform business model. The infrastructure built to enforce consent actively weaponizes choice architecture to circumvent it, so the pop-ups are arguably more manipulative than no banner at all.

Who ends up ahead

Consent-platform vendors and the websites that keep collecting the same data with a compliant-looking veneer.

Who's exposed

Every user annoyed into clicking "yes" — and the idea of consent itself, hollowed into a click.

The single biggest worry

The system made non-consensual collection the default despite the law: you see a banner, the reject button is three clicks away in gray text, you tap "Accept All" in frustration — and that click now counts as valid legal consent. Regulators chase individual fines while the underlying industry has monetized the workaround.

The surprising part

The whole apparatus produces privacy outcomes nearly identical to the pre-GDPR world — same data collected, plus a $2B industry and a universal annoyance on top. The "consent" pop-up creates the illusion of choice while engineering it away, which is why regulators are now trying to abolish the thing their own law required.

In one sentence

A law meant to protect privacy created a $2B consent-washing industry that profits by making refusal annoying enough that you accept — satisfying regulators with a thin layer of theater.
