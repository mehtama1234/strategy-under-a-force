Group B · The compliance & deletion industry

The compliance economy

GDPR turned privacy into a legal mandate — and spun up a $15B industry that helps companies prove they're not evil.

Privacy laws — GDPR in Europe (2018), CCPA/CPRA in California (2020) — forced every company to manage consent, handle data requests, and avoid huge fines. That created a booming market for compliance software (OneTrust, TrustArc), privacy lawyers, consultants, and Chief Privacy Officers. Privacy became a business input and a big cost center, whether or not people's privacy actually improved.

The core bet

Regulation turned privacy protection from a legal checkbox into a whole industry of vendors, consultants, and risk managers — one that grew faster than consumer privacy itself did. Every company now has to document compliance, which is a different thing from collecting less data.

Proving it, not doing it

The compliance machine decoupled from actual privacy. Companies now focus on proving compliance through documentation, cookie banners, and vendor contracts rather than reducing data collection or building privacy-first systems. You can check every box, pay for premium tooling, and still hoover up invasive data — because the law rewards disclosing practices, not restricting them.

Who ends up ahead

Compliance vendors and privacy lawyers — a recurring-revenue business backed by a legal mandate.

Who's exposed

Startups and small firms priced out of compliance, and users whose privacy didn't actually improve.

The single biggest worry

Privacy law accidentally created a two-tier system: only large companies can easily afford compliance ($50k–$500k/yr in tooling, or in-house teams), while giants like Google and Meta treat fines as a rounding error. The unintended winner is the compliance-software vendor, whose business is now more defensible than most of its customers'.

The surprising part

The lasting irony: the most durable businesses to come out of the privacy movement aren't privacy for users — they're OneTrust and TrustArc, selling the tools that let everyone else prove they're not evil. The mandate created recurring revenue for the paperwork, not protection for the person.

In one sentence

Privacy law built a $15B+ industry of consultants and software that profits from helping companies prove they're compliant — not from making them collect less data.
