Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Privacy as a luxury

The free version comes with total surveillance; real privacy now takes money or tech-savvy — so it sorts by wealth.

Privacy is shifting from a public good into a paid tier. The "free" experience comes with tracking and ad targeting; actual privacy — avoiding profiling and data sale — increasingly requires money or technical skill. An expensive iPhone protects you more than a cheap Android; "pay to remove ads" tiers, VPN and deletion subscriptions, and Meta's "pay or consent" all mean the same thing: privacy now sorts by wealth.

The core bet

The baseline is surveillance; escape is a feature you buy. That two-tier structure means the poor and less technical absorb the tracking, manipulative ads, and predatory targeting, while those who can pay opt out.

The poor absorb the risk

The real harm isn't abstract: free-tier users see more manipulative ads, predatory lending, and targeted misinformation — ad networks charge premium rates for low-income audiences because they're easier to convert. Algorithms trained on that engagement reinforce loan sharks, gambling, and health fraud aimed at exactly the people who can least afford to be scammed. Privacy becomes protection, and if you can't pay, you stay exposed.

Who ends up ahead

Apple, premium-device makers, and paid privacy services — selling protection to those who can afford it.

Who's exposed

The poor, the elderly, and the less technical — who get the surveilled "free" version and absorb the risk.

The single biggest worry

We may be building a world where privacy is a premium — the wealthy escape surveillance, the poor are its raw material — which is the opposite of a universal right. On-device AI, the most genuinely private option, is locked to $1,000+ phones, pricing real privacy out of reach for billions.

The surprising part

The surprise is how quietly it normalized. When Meta introduced "pay or consent," users didn't revolt — they paid or accepted. Apple's privacy-first posture became a luxury selling point, not a moral baseline. Companies learned that saying "privacy costs money" is less damaging than forcing people to discover it through dark patterns — because we've collectively decided surveillance is the default and escape is a purchase.

In one sentence

Privacy used to be a right everyone had, badly; now it's a product only the wealthy or tech-savvy can afford — so surveillance falls hardest on those who can least escape it.
