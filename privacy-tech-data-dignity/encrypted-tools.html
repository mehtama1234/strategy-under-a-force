Group A · Privacy as a product & a weapon

Encrypted tools

Signal, DuckDuckGo, Brave, Proton — real businesses built on the promise of not spying on you.

A wave of privacy-first tools went mainstream: Signal (encrypted messaging, a nonprofit), DuckDuckGo (private search), Brave (a browser that blocks trackers), Proton (encrypted email and VPN from Switzerland). They built real organizations on the opposite of the Google/Meta model — not tracking you. And end-to-end encryption spread into WhatsApp and iMessage, so private-by-default messaging reached billions.

The core bet

These companies flipped the tech business model: instead of monetizing your data, they made not tracking you the product. They bet that enough people feared surveillance to pay for or support alternatives — and that every new breach or scandal would grow that fear.

What the numbers say

~40–70M
Signal users — a nonprofit founded in 2010, kept alive by a $50M donation from WhatsApp's co-founder and grants.
~100M
DuckDuckGo daily searches — profitable since ~2014 on privacy-respecting contextual ads, not behavioral tracking.
~50–70M
Brave monthly users — blocking trackers by default, monetized via a token-reward model and paid subscriptions.
~100M
Proton accounts — freemium encrypted email plus VPN and password manager, never any ads.
+350%
Signal's download spike after a single 2021 WhatsApp privacy-policy scare — each scandal is free growth.

It never needed to win the majority

Privacy became a defensive moat, not an offensive one. Google and Meta largely wrote off the privacy market, so these tools compete by making tracking look obviously bad — every privacy scare helps Signal rather than hurting it. And mainstream encryption spread almost by accident, because regulators and researchers made unencrypted messaging indefensible.

Who ends up ahead

Privacy-first tools that captured trust — plus the high-value users (journalists, activists, dissidents) who prove the model.

Who's exposed

The tools' own sustainability — donations and small paid tiers don't scale like ad networks.

The single biggest worry

The core tension is funding. Donations and micro-payments don't scale like advertising: Signal survives on a one-time $50M gift, and Proton and Brave cap out at maybe 5–10% paying users because most people won't pay for privacy. The fear is that privacy tools either stay small and niche, or slowly compromise to survive — or get bought and shut down by a surveillance giant.

The surprising part

The surprise is that privacy never had to win a majority of users — just enough high-value ones (journalists, activists, security-conscious professionals) to prove the model works and justify staying independent. The real product these companies sell is the one thing Google and Meta give away free: trust.

In one sentence

Privacy-first tools turned surveillance anxiety into a sustainable business by charging directly — in money or donations — for the trust the ad giants gave away free.
