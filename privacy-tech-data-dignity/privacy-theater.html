Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Privacy theater

Much of the "privacy" on offer protects you from other companies — but not from the one selling it.

A lot of the "privacy" on offer is partial, performative, or self-serving. Apple markets privacy but still collects plenty and takes ~$20B a year from Google to be the default search; "incognito mode" didn't do what people thought (Google's 2024 settlement); consent pop-ups are dark-pattern theater; and many privacy features shield you from other companies but not the platform itself. Privacy is often a marketing angle as much as a technical fact.

The core bet

Privacy tech is marketed as protection but often works as theater — a way for a company to look different while capturing the same data through a different door. The model is less "we don't collect" and more "we collect in ways we can call private, legitimate, or defensible."

Protection from them, not from us

The tell is the asymmetry: iOS blocks third-party cookies but enables Apple's own first-party tracking; a private browser blocks ads but monetizes your attention directly; encrypted apps hide content but keep metadata. The privacy feature protects you from competitors, not from the platform — because the platform is the one you're paying, and it still needs your data.

Who ends up ahead

The companies deploying privacy features — gaining brand value, legal defensibility, and investor appeal.

Who's exposed

Users who feel protected while they aren't — and stop asking the harder questions.

The single biggest worry

The danger isn't that privacy tech fails — it's that it succeeds at making people feel protected while they're not. Someone who trusts "private" search but still gets targeted ads, or thinks incognito is anonymous, is being trained to lower their guard. When privacy theater works, it doesn't just fail to protect — it conditions the next generation to trust the people who profit most from surveillance.

The surprising part

The counterintuitive truth: privacy tech's real value is to the company, not the user. A privacy feature makes a firm defensible in court, credible in marketing, and attractive to investors — while it still collects whatever isn't yet blocked. Apple's privacy marketing is worth tens of billions in brand and loyalty; the actual protection is a rounding error by comparison. Privacy tech is mostly a business-to-regulator product, and consumers are the audience.

In one sentence

Privacy tech is often marketing that works better than the privacy it provides — making companies more valuable and users feel safer than they actually are.
