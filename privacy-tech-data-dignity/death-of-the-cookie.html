Death of the cookie

Killing the tracker that followed you for 25 years didn't set you free — it handed the giants an even bigger advantage.

The third-party cookie — the little tracker that followed you across the web for 25 years — is dying. Safari and Firefox blocked it years ago; Google promised for years to kill it in Chrome, then in 2024–25 wavered and reversed. Its decline reshuffles the whole ad industry — but not toward privacy. The winners are those with first-party data and logged-in users (Google, Meta, Amazon, retailers); the losers are independent ad-tech and publishers.

The core bet

The cookie's death was framed as privacy winning. It wasn't. It means the giants who own login gates and shopping carts keep tracking you, while everyone else loses the tools to compete — so "privacy" here mostly entrenches the incumbents.

What the numbers say

2017 · 2019
Safari (ITP) and Firefox blocked third-party cookies — barely denting Google and Meta, who own logged-in sites.
2020 → 2025

~$13B
Amazon's 2024 ad revenue — retail media, which needs no cookies because it owns the purchase data.
~5% → ~15%
retail media's share of digital ad spend as the cookieless winner; independent ad-tech stalled or shrank.
−20 to −40%

Privacy as oligopoly cover

The cookie's death was a Trojan horse for centralization. Small publishers and ad networks can no longer stitch together behavior without relying on Google's own Privacy-Sandbox tools — which Google controls and can sunset — while Google, Meta, and Amazon accumulate first-party data (login, cart, search, email) that no cookie rule touches. Fewer middlemen compete; the platforms grip tighter.

Who ends up ahead

Google, Meta, Amazon, and retailers with logged-in, first-party data — untouched by cookie rules.

Who's exposed

Independent ad-tech, and the open-web publishers whose ad rates collapsed as demand fled to walled gardens.

The single biggest worry

"Privacy," as packaged by the browsers, became regulatory cover for oligopoly: the platforms accumulate first-party data no rule can reach, small publishers must build costly data warehouses or fold, and the ad ecosystem gets less competitive and more concentrated — the opposite of what "killing the tracker" was sold as.

The surprising part

The twist: Google, which supposedly led the cookie-killing effort, didn't kill it — it turned the cookie into a consumer "choice" prompt most users click through. Britain's competition regulator effectively called Google's bluff (the Privacy-Sandbox tools were worse for advertisers), so Google kept cookies while looking privacy-conscious. It wasn't privacy winning; it was the giants realizing they no longer needed the cookie anyway.

In one sentence

The third-party cookie died not because privacy won, but because Google, Meta, and Amazon own the login gates and carts that made it redundant — so its death entrenched them and gutted everyone else.
