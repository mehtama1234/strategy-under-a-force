Group A · The product is a front for the loan

Car dealers & automakers — a bank that happens to make cars

On the lot and at head office, the real profit isn't the metal. It's the loan on the metal.

A modern carmaker is, quietly, a bank that also builds cars. At the dealership, the highest-margin department isn't sales — it's the "F&I" office (finance & insurance), which arranges the loan, the lease, gap insurance, and the extended warranty. And at head office, the captive lender (Ford Credit, GM Financial, Toyota Financial) is often the most profitable division of all.

The money is in the financing

2–3×
How much more a dealer's F&I office makes per car (~$2,000–3,000) than the gross profit on the vehicle sale itself. F&I is now ~20–30% of dealer gross profit.
  • Ford Credit has, in some years, been 20–40% of Ford's entire pre-tax profit — the lender out-earning the factories.
  • Leasing is ~30–35% of US sales (up from ~15% in 2005) — locking in recurring payments.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

If rates stay high or used-car values crash, the loan book blows up: more borrowers go "underwater" (owing more than the car is worth — already ~25–30% of trade-ins), defaults spike, and the finance arm becomes a liability overnight. You can't manufacture your way out of a bad loan portfolio.

The surprising part

If you own Ford or GM stock, you partly own a bank, not a carmaker. Ford Credit runs on classic bank economics — a 2–3% spread between what it borrows at and what it lends at. That finance arm is so profitable that, spun out, it would likely trade at a bank's valuation (15–18× earnings) versus the automaker's ~5–6×. The market treats the whole company as a risky carmaker — but a big chunk of it is a lender.

In one sentence

Car dealers & automakers in a nutshell

Carmakers and dealers earn their real profit from originating and holding auto loans, leases, and add-ons — so the vehicle is increasingly the customer-acquisition device for a lending business.
