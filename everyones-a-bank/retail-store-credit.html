Group A · The product is a front for the loan

Retail store credit — a subprime lender with a shop attached

The store-branded credit card often makes more money than the clothes on the racks.

Many big retailers make more profit from their store credit card than from selling merchandise. Macy's, Kohl's, Nordstrom, and Target run branded card programs (usually via Synchrony or Citi) that earn 25–30%+ interest and fees. The clothes are low-margin; the card income is high-margin — and it quietly props up struggling stores.

The card is the business

~40%
Share of Kohl's gross profit that came from its Synchrony credit card in recent years. Macy's card income has, at times, rivaled its entire operating profit.
  • Store cards charge 24–30% APR; finance charges are ~60–70% of the card program's revenue.
  • Card holders spend 2–3× more, so the card also drives store traffic — a double benefit.
  • Over 60% of store-card balances are held by subprime borrowers.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The card income masks how weak the actual retail is — and it's fragile. When defaults rise (store-card delinquencies hit ~4–5.5%, above bank cards) or rates fall, the card profit shrinks and the retailer discovers the shop underneath was in free fall. Kohl's operating income has halved when its card income wobbled.

The surprising part

The model is upside down: a Macy's or Kohl's is closer to a subprime lending operation with a department store attached than the reverse. A 25% APR on a $2,000 balance paid over two years earns far more than selling a single coat. The merchandise is the loss-leader that gets people to borrow — and the borrowing is the business.

In one sentence

Retail store credit in a nutshell

Retailers became de facto subprime lenders — selling clothes at thin margins while earning 25–30% APR on card balances that prop up dying stores, until defaults spike and the prop gives way.
