Group D · Finance eats industries

Private credit — banking moved out of the banks

The biggest lenders now aren't banks. They're asset managers — using your retirement money, with lighter rules.

After the 2008 crisis, regulators made it harder and costlier for banks to make risky loans. So the lending simply moved out of banks — to investment firms like Apollo, Blackstone, Ares, and KKR, which now make the loans banks used to. They call it "private credit." It's the same business — borrow from savers, lend to borrowers, keep the spread — but with much lighter regulation.

The shadow banks

$1.5–2T
The private-credit market (2024–25), up from ~$500B in 2015. Non-bank lenders are now roughly 40–50% of the credit market, versus ~15% in 2008.

To get cheap, permanent money to lend, these firms bought or merged with insurers — Apollo bought Athene; others followed — so they can use your annuity and insurance premiums as their lending capital.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — no safety net

Unlike banks, private-credit funds have no capital requirements, no stress tests, no deposit insurance, no central-bank backstop. If a recession hits and loans default, there's no buffer — and a wave of redemptions could force fire-sales into a 2008-style crunch, but with no built-in circuit breaker.

The surprising part

The eye-opener: Wall Street's shadow-bank system is built on your retirement money. Pensions, annuities, and insurance premiums — money that's supposed to be safe — now fund leveraged loans made by firms with no systemic guardrails. Regulators saw it coming and issued warnings for years, but it lives in a gray zone between (lightly-regulated) asset management and (heavily-regulated) banking — so it grew bigger and riskier precisely because nobody had the authority to stop it, and it has never been tested by a real downturn.

In one sentence

Private credit in a nutshell

Lending quietly left banks for private-equity firms that use retirement money to make the risky loans regulators forbade banks to make — a $2 trillion shadow-banking system that has never faced a real recession.
