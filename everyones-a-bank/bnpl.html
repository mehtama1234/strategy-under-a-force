Group B · Tech becomes a bank

Buy now, pay later — a loan dressed as a payment button

"Split it into 4 easy payments" is a friendly way to say "here's a loan." And the debt is invisible.

Buy Now, Pay Later (Klarna, Affirm, Afterpay) splits a purchase into four interest-free payments at checkout. It looks like a payment feature. It's really a lending business. It exploded during the 2020–21 pandemic, and its cleverest — and most worrying — trick is that the debt it creates is largely invisible.

How it makes money

  • Merchants pay 2–8% per transaction (more than card fees) because BNPL lifts cart sizes 20–30% and completes more checkouts.
  • BNPL firms earn on merchant fees, late fees, longer-term interest loans, and data (Klarna has 150M+ shopper profiles and is becoming a shopping app).
  • Klarna filed to IPO (delayed to 2025, ~$35B) partly amid rising defaults.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — invisible "phantom debt"

BNPL loans mostly don't report to credit bureaus — so a person can owe thousands across Klarna, Affirm, and Afterpay at once and no single lender (or regulator) sees the full picture. It's a shadow credit system quietly building up among young, often subprime borrowers. The US CFPB moved in 2024 to treat BNPL like a credit card.

The surprising part

BNPL sells itself as a guilt-free convenience — but it functions as a lender targeting the exact shoppers traditional cards reject, and it's engineered to make you overspend. The frictionless "pay in 4" button nudges price-sensitive buyers to say yes to things they couldn't otherwise afford. The real money is in late fees, the data, and the debt — dressed up as a payment option.

In one sentence

Buy now, pay later in a nutshell

BNPL is credit-card lending disguised as a checkout convenience — optimized to lift merchant sales and load young shoppers with debt that credit bureaus (and regulators) can't even see.
