Group C · The enablers & bottlenecks

The skilled-trades shortage

The whole rebuild runs into one wall: there aren't enough electricians, welders, and plumbers to do it.

This is the binding constraint on everything else. Decades of pushing everyone toward college, an aging workforce retiring, and few young entrants have left a large and growing shortage of skilled tradespeople. Construction spending is at record highs and wages are climbing fast, but there simply aren't enough hands to do the work — so whoever controls trained crews, trains them, or automates around them holds the real power.

The core bet

The scarce thing isn't demand or money — it's hands. With not enough electricians, welders, and plumbers, whoever owns or trains skilled crews holds the bottleneck, and trades wages and pricing power keep climbing.

What the numbers say

rising
Electrification (EVs, solar, heat pumps, data centers) is driving new electrician demand exactly when supply is tightest.

The trades became a debt-free path that can out-earn college

A journeyman electrician can make $60,000–$90,000+, and a master electrician can clear $150,000 — often with no student debt. That flips the old 'college or bust' story, though it hasn't yet reversed the enrollment trends that caused the shortage. The scarcity is also exactly why private equity loves the trades: scarce labor means pricing power.

The single biggest worry

The shortage directly caps how much can actually be built — projects slip by months or years waiting for crews, and costs blow out as contractors charge premium rates. Tighter immigration enforcement can shrink the labor pool further, making a structural problem worse.

The surprising part

The surprise is a status inversion: the jobs a generation was told to avoid now offer security, high pay, and no debt, while the bottleneck they create is what gates the trillion-dollar rebuild. Every glamorous project — the AI data center, the chip fab, the solar farm — waits on a tradesperson who is suddenly the most valuable worker on site.

In one sentence

There aren't enough trained electricians, welders, and plumbers, they're aging out faster than they're replaced, and that shortage — not money — caps how much of the rebuild can actually happen.
