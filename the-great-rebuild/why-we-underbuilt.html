Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why we under-built

The boom isn't new demand appearing — it's forty years of deferral finally coming due.

This is the why. After the 2008 crash, homebuilding collapsed and never fully recovered; public infrastructure investment fell for decades as a share of the economy; restrictive zoning spread; factories moved offshore; and flat electricity demand let utilities stop expanding. A generation of building we chose not to do all became unavoidable at once. The 'boom' is really the bill for that deferral arriving.

The core bet

The rebuild boom isn't fresh demand — it's a 40-year backlog of things we chose not to build finally becoming impossible to ignore. Necessity, not innovation, is driving it.

What the numbers say

Cheap globalization let us not build — until it didn't

For decades it was cheaper to buy from abroad than to build at home, so America deferred: fewer factories, less infrastructure, a tired grid. Now that offshoring has hit its limits and supply chains are reshoring, the old option is gone. The catch-up isn't a burst of ambition — it's the end of an excuse.

The single biggest worry

The same forces that caused the under-building — NIMBY opposition, restrictive zoning, high permitting costs, short-term budgets — are all still here. Add the labor shortage and materials inflation, and the catch-up could stall halfway, leaving the backlog only partly cleared.

The surprising part

The reframe worth holding onto: this 'boom' is really a debt repayment. We spent forty years enjoying cheap imported goods instead of building, and the savings were an illusion — the deferred bill just compounded. Now it's due all at once, which is exactly why everything feels like it's happening simultaneously.

In one sentence

For forty years America bought cheap from abroad instead of building at home, and now that choice has expired and the whole deferred bill is due at once.
