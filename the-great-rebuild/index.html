The Great Rebuild

We stopped building for 40 years. Now everything needs building at once — and the bottleneck isn't money, it's hands.

The 38th force. After the 2008 crash, homebuilding collapsed and never fully recovered; the grid went 20 years without growing; factories moved offshore; bridges and pipes aged quietly. Now the bill for a generation of under-building comes due all at once — a housing shortage, reshored factories, AI data centers, a strained power grid, and a $1.2 trillion infrastructure law — and it all runs through construction, the skilled trades, materials, and engineering. The one idea: when everything must be built at once but there aren't enough skilled hands, the money pools at the choke points.

Seeded by the Inc. 5000 data "Construction & Built Environment" is the 4th-biggest fast-growing theme on the list — 547 companies, ~$42.1B represented — spanning homebuilding and remodel, specialty trades, building materials, disaster restoration, and engineering. This collection follows that money.
The one question, asked of everyone

What did the great rebuild make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? The electrician and the crew became the scarce thing; the gravel pit and the equipment fleet became mini-monopolies; and the person who owns the bottleneck got rich while the person swinging the hammer got wages. The danger: it's cyclical and subsidy-fed, the projects bid up the same scarce labor against each other, and permitting can strangle the whole catch-up.
