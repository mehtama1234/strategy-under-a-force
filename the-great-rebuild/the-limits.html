Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The limits

What could stall or spoil the super-cycle — and why the biggest threat is a signature, not a market.

This is the skeptical piece. The rebuild faces hard ceilings: the skilled-labor shortage caps how much can be built; permitting and review stretch timelines for years; costs are badly inflated; much of the demand rests on one-time government subsidies and low-ish rates that could reverse; and there's real risk of overbuilding, especially in data centers. Betting on the boom means betting those constraints don't all tighten at once.

The core bet

The ceiling on the rebuild isn't ambition — it's the binding constraints of labor, permits, cost, and policy. Bet on the boom and you're betting all four stay loose at the same time.

The boom's worst enemy is itself

Every fab, data center, and solar farm competes for the same crews, driving wages 20–40% above trend and eating project margins mid-build. And the thing most likely to end it isn't a market crash — it's policy: one decision to pause or kill CHIPS or the IRA credits would dry up profitability on dozens of projects overnight.

The single biggest worry

A single reversal — rates spiking to 8–9%, subsidies cut, or a recession — could flip 'everything everywhere at once' into stalled, half-built sites: labor suddenly freed but projects frozen, and unfinished assets turning into liabilities.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable insight is how synchronized the risk is. The same forces that started every project at once — cheap-ish money and a burst of subsidies — could withdraw at once, so the boom is far more fragile than its scale suggests. It was switched on by policy, and it can be switched off the same way.

In one sentence

The rebuild is real but capped by scarce labor, multi-year permits, inflated costs, and one-time subsidies — and a single rate shock or policy reversal could turn the surge into stalled, half-built sites.
