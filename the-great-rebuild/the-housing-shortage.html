Group A · What needs building

The housing shortage

We're millions of homes short — so you either build one or fix the one you're stuck in.

The scarce thing here is housing supply itself. After the 2008 crash, America simply stopped building enough homes, and a decade later the shortfall turned buying a house into a bidding war. Whoever can actually get a home built — or help the millions who can't afford to move renovate the one they already own — is selling the only thing people can get.

The core bet

Housing became a shortage, and a shortage rewards whoever can add supply. Homebuilders that can secure land, clear permits, and finish houses capture buyers who have no other option — and when new homes are out of reach, the money flows into remodeling instead, because fixing your kitchen is the only move left.

What the numbers say

3–4M
Estimated shortfall of homes in the U.S. as of the mid-2020s, after a decade-plus of under-building.
478k
Single-family housing starts in 2009 — down from about 1.7M a year before the 2008 crash, and slow to recover since.
~$470B
Annual U.S. home-improvement and remodeling spend (Harvard's Joint Center estimate) — a boom fueled by people who can't move.
3% vs 7%
The 'lock-in effect': owners with a 3% pandemic mortgage won't sell into 7% rates, freezing the resale market.
100k+
Unfilled construction jobs — the trades shortage caps how fast anything gets built.
~400%
The lumber-price spike of 2020–2021 — one of many material shocks that whipsawed builders.

Why the remodel boom is the shortage in disguise

When nobody can afford to trade up, they renovate instead — so the shortage doesn't just help homebuilders, it created a giant remodeling industry as a side effect. Meanwhile the big public builders quietly became land-and-finance machines: they assemble lots, wait for zoning wins, pre-sell at a markup, and hand the actual hammering to subcontractors. The house is almost a by-product of the land deal.

The single biggest worry

If mortgage rates fall back and stay there, or a recession hits jobs and wages, buyers vanish and the remodel boom freezes — and builders holding land and inventory eat the loss. The shortage only stays a shortage while people keep trying to move and money stays cheap enough to borrow.

The surprising part

The strangest twist: the shortage decoupled the homebuilder from homebuilding. The most profitable players don't necessarily build the most — they control land and financing, or they sell the remodel that's the only option left. A contractor redoing a bathroom isn't competing with new construction; they're selling to someone who has nowhere else to go.

In one sentence

We didn't build enough homes for fifteen years, so now whoever can add a house — or fix the one you can't replace — is selling the only thing available.
