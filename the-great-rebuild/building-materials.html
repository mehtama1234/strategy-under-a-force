Group B · The fragmented trades boom

Materials & distribution

You can't ship a rock very far — which quietly makes a gravel pit one of the best businesses in America.

Every home, road, factory, and data center needs concrete, aggregates (sand, gravel, crushed stone), steel, and thousands of products — sold through distributors. These are unglamorous but powerful businesses: heavy materials are too costly to ship far, so a quarry near a job site is a local monopoly, and the distributor sitting between maker and builder collects a toll on everything that goes up.

The core bet

Materials are heavy and local — shipping rock more than ~50 miles kills the margin, so a well-placed quarry or cement plant is a mini-monopoly. And the distributor between maker and builder taxes every project.

What the numbers say

~50 mi
The rough distance beyond which trucking aggregates stops paying — the invisible fence that creates local monopolies.
~$20B
Approximate size of the U.S. aggregates market; cement adds several billion more.
25–30%
Operating margins at the big aggregates firms — roughly triple the 8–10% on general construction work.
15–25%
Price increases aggregates producers pushed through in 2021–2023 — and held, because you can't stop building.
$6B+
Revenue at a single major building-products distributor (e.g. SiteOne), showing how the 'last mile' consolidates too.

The boring gravel pit is the moat

Captive customers, low price sensitivity, and inflation that flows straight to the bottom line make a quarry in a growing metro a moat that compounds for decades with almost no reinvestment. 'You can't ship a rock' isn't a joke — it is the business.

The single biggest worry

It's still construction, so it's cyclical: a housing or commercial bust can cut volumes 30–40%. And permitting a new quarry is a 5–10 year fight with neighbors, which is a double-edged sword — hard for you, but it also protects the incumbents who already have one.

The surprising part

The counter-intuitive part: the least exciting asset in the whole rebuild — a hole in the ground full of gravel — is one of the highest-quality businesses, precisely because geography hands it a monopoly and inflation hands it pricing power. Whoever owns the pit owns a private tax on construction itself.

In one sentence

Heavy materials can't travel far, so whoever controls the quarry or depot near the job owns a local, inflation-proof toll on everything that gets built.
