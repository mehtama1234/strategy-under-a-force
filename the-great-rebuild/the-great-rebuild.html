The great rebuild

Everything must be built at once — but the bottleneck is hands, not money, so the money pools at the choke points.

Pull the whole collection together and one through-line runs through all of it. After forty years of under-building — too few homes, a tired grid, deferred bridges, factories sent offshore — America has to build everything at once: housing, fabs, data centers, transmission, roads. But you can't buy your way out with money alone. The binding constraint is skilled hands and heavy materials, so whoever controls the scarce inputs captures the upside while everyone else gets a bill.

The core bet

We stopped building for forty years and now must build it all at once — but the constraint is skilled labor and materials, not money, so profit pools at the choke points: the quarry, the crew, the permit, the equipment fleet.

Winners and losers

The rebuild sorts cleanly into who owns the scarce thing and who pays for it.

Who ends up ahead

Owners of scarce inputs — aggregates and materials, equipment-rental fleets, PE-backed trade platforms, engineering firms, and permit expertise — plus skilled tradespeople who, for the first time in decades, can set their own price.

Who's exposed

Homebuyers and taxpayers absorbing 30%+ markups from constrained labor and materials; projects that can't secure crews or permits in time; and late-arriving speculators who bet on cheap labor that never returns.

The single biggest worry

The boom competes with itself for the same electricians and concrete, driving wages well above trend — and much of it rests on one-time subsidies and rates from an earlier era. If those wind down or reverse, the labor queue evaporates but the projects are only half-built.

The surprising part

The tidy irony to end on: the sexiest growth stories of the decade — AI data centers, EV plants, solar farms, chip fabs — all bottleneck on the least fashionable things imaginable. An electrician who can pull a 1,000-amp feed. A quarry that can ship gravel. A transformer with a two-year lead time. The macro story isn't 'AI is coming'; it's 'can you find someone to wire the building?'

In one sentence

After forty years of under-building, a multi-trillion-dollar wave of construction hit at once — but the real bottleneck is skilled hands, not money, so profit pools at the choke points while everyone else gets the bill.
