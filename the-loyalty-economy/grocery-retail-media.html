Group D · Monetizing the relationship

Grocery & retail media — the ads outsell the groceries

Kroger and Walmart discovered that selling ads against your shopping data beats selling the food.

Supermarkets run on razor-thin margins — a few pennies on the dollar. So Kroger and Walmart found a far richer business hiding inside their loyalty cards: selling ads to the brands whose products sit on their shelves, targeted using the shopping data of tens of millions of loyalty members. It's called "retail media," and it's wildly more profitable than groceries.

The high-margin business inside the low-margin one

~4.5×
How much higher the margin is on a retail-media ad than on selling the actual groceries. Kroger's loyalty program has 60M+ members; its 84.51° data arm sells ads against their behavior. Walmart Connect runs at a ~$3B+ scale.

A brand like a shampoo maker pays Kroger to show its product first when you search — and Kroger marks up that ad slot at several times the margin of selling the shampoo itself.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — you're the product on the shelf

Retail media only works because the store tracks and sells your shopping behavior. That's a low-friction form of surveillance most shoppers don't notice — and as privacy rules tighten (and "loyalty-tax" pricing, where non-members pay more, draws scrutiny), the data engine underneath it faces regulatory risk.

The surprising part

This is the direct offline cousin of the attention economy. When Apple and the death of tracking cookies made it harder for advertisers to follow you around the web (see the attention-economy page), the most valuable thing became first-party data — data a company collects from its own customers. And who has the richest first-party data of all? The store that knows exactly what you actually bought. The grocer quietly became an ad company.

In one sentence

Grocery & retail media in a nutshell

Grocers turned their loyalty-card data into a retail-media ad business that earns ~4.5× the margin of the food — becoming, in effect, ad companies that happen to sell groceries.
