Group D · Monetizing the relationship

Subscriptions everywhere — you rent now; you don't own

Companies discovered that renting you a thing forever beats selling it to you once.

The purest form of "own the relationship" is the subscription. A recurring monthly fee is worth far more to a company — and to Wall Street — than a one-time sale: it's predictable, it locks you in, and it generates constant data. So subscriptions spread from software to everything, and the option to simply buy a thing started disappearing.

From product to rental

  • Software led the way. In 2013, Adobe stopped selling boxed Photoshop and made Creative Cloud a subscription-only rental. Its stock became a steady climber; Microsoft 365 did the same.
  • Then physical goods: razors (Dollar Shave Club, sold to Unilever for ~$1B in 2016), meal kits, pet food.
  • Then hardware you already own: BMW tried charging ~$18/month for heated seats (2022) — the hardware was already in the car — and faced such backlash it reversed course.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — fatigue and dark patterns

Subscriptions compound. Software, streaming, storage, car features, memberships — the monthly bill quietly balloons. And companies made canceling deliberately painful (buried buttons, phone-only cancellation), so regulators had to step in with "click to cancel" rules (the FTC pushed hard ~2024). Subscription fatigue is now a real, measurable backlash.

The surprising part

The quiet inversion: you increasingly rent instead of own — because companies removed the option to buy. Adobe didn't offer a cheaper permanent license alongside the subscription; it ended permanent licenses. BMW didn't ask whether you'd like to buy heated seats; it decided to charge monthly. The relationship isn't a nice add-on to the product anymore — it is the product, and it's designed to be extractive: price hikes, lock-in, and a cancel button that hides.

In one sentence

Subscriptions everywhere in a nutshell

Companies discovered that renting you something forever beats selling it once — so they turned products into subscriptions and quietly removed the option to just own the thing.
