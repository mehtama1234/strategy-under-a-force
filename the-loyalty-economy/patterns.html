The patterns

Line up everyone the loyalty economy touched, and one theme dominates: the product is the bait, and the relationship — the data, the points, the float — is the real business.

Each page asks one question: what did the loyalty economy make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions keep showing up. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

The sale used to be the end of the story. Now it's the doorway. Once a company enrolls you — app, points, membership, a stored balance — it monetizes the relationship in ways the sale never could: frequency, data, ads, float, points sold to banks. Again and again, that relationship is worth more than the product — and whoever owns the customer wins, while whoever's stuck selling a commodity in someone else's channel loses.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

The product is the bait; the relationship is the business

The burger, the coffee, the flight, the fast shipping — all loss-leaders to get you enrolled. The money is in what happens after: the recurring, data-rich bond that the sale opens the door to.

Pattern 02

The loyalty program is worth more than the business

The most extreme form. An airline's miles are sometimes valued higher than the whole airline; a hotel's card business rivals its rooms; a casino's real asset is its player database. The bolt-on became the main event.

airline mileshotel loyaltycasinos
Pattern 03

Own the customer, or someone else will

If a middleman sits between you and the buyer, they own the relationship and the data. So companies fight to go direct — Nike quit wholesale, Domino's pushed everyone onto its app — because owning the customer beats renting shelf space.

NikeDomino'sAmazon Prime
Pattern 04

First-party data became the new moat

When tracking cookies died, the valuable thing became data a company collects from its own customers. Loyalty programs are how you get it — and the richest of all is the store that knows exactly what you bought. (The offline mirror of the attention economy.)

retail mediaMcDonald'scasinos
Pattern 05

Frequency is the real prize

Points don't just retain customers — they change behavior. A loyalty member visits 2.5× more; a Prime member spends 3× more. The program's job is to turn an occasional buyer into a habit.

McDonald'sStarbucksAmazon Prime
Pattern 06

Sell the same thing twice

The relationship yields a second, richer revenue stream. Airlines sell miles to banks and fly you; grocers sell ads and groceries; Starbucks holds your prepaid cash and sells you coffee. The core product funds a hidden second business.

airline milesretail mediaStarbucks
Pattern 07

Recurring beats one-time — so buying disappears

A membership or subscription is worth far more than a single sale, and Wall Street pays up for it. So companies turned products into memberships and subscriptions — and quietly removed the option to just own the thing.

CostcosubscriptionsAmazon Prime
Pattern 08

Someone quietly foots the bill

The rewards aren't free. Cash-payers and the poor subsidize the affluent's card points; non-members pay a "loyalty tax"; subscribers face fatigue and hide-the-cancel-button dark patterns; and everyone pays with their data. The cost is real — just invisible in the price.
