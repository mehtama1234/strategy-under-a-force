Group E · The big picture

The loyalty economy — the whole force in one page

One idea ties every page together: the product is the bait; the relationship is the business.

Step back from the twelve stories and one shift explains them all. For a century, commerce was transactional: you bought the product and the sale ended. Now the real business is the relationship. Companies enroll you — an app, points, a membership, a stored balance — to turn a one-off purchase into a recurring, data-rich bond that they can monetize again and again.

The one idea: the relationship is the profit center

The whole force in a sentence

Once a company owns the relationship, it makes money in ways the sale alone never could: frequency (you come back more), data (it knows and targets you), retail-media ads (it sells your attention to brands), float (it holds your prepaid cash), and points sold to banks (a financial business bolted on). Value flows to whoever owns the customer, the app, and the data — and drains from whoever's stuck selling a commodity through someone else's channel.

Where the frame holds — and where it bends

The frame is fair for premium brands and platforms — Starbucks, Amazon, the airlines. But it bends: for commodity sellers, "loyalty" often collapses into a race-to-the-bottom discounting game that just trains customers to wait for deals. Some of it is old-fashioned couponing rebranded. And the whole thing rests on customer data, which means privacy backlash and regulation are real threats — as is plain "subscription fatigue."

The scale of the shift

~$50B+
Retail-media ad market and growing ~20–30%/yr — high-margin ad money built entirely on loyalty-and-shopping data.
worth more
Airline frequent-flyer programs are sometimes valued at more than half the entire airline — the loyalty program worth more than the business it's attached to.

Who wins, who loses

Win

Companies that own the customer, app, and first-party data (Starbucks, Amazon, Walmart, airlines); banks and card issuers; the biggest platforms selling ads against loyalty data.

Lose

Commodity sellers stuck in someone else's channel; small retailers with no scale to build this; cash-payers who subsidize others' rewards; and the privacy of customers.

The biggest tension

The open question

Loyalty is built on a mild regressive transfer: everyone's prices fund the rewards of the engaged and affluent, while non-members quietly pay more. It also concentrates power — whoever owns the customer relationship (your airline, your Amazon, your grocer) extracts the most value, while suppliers lose pricing power. And it all runs on personal data, so the regulatory backlash — over privacy, dark patterns, and "loyalty-tax" pricing — is the thing that could force it to change.

The surprising part

The loyalty program is often worth more than the business it's attached to — miles worth more than the airline, a coffee chain's most bank-like asset being its gift-card balance. And this is really the offline mirror of the attention economy. When tracking cookies died and it got harder to follow people around the web, first-party data — what a company learns from its own customers — became the new moat. What started as a humble discount incentive quietly became the main event: the airline sells miles, the grocer sells ads, the coffee shop runs a bank, and the product is just the thing that gets you in the door.

In one sentence

The loyalty economy in a nutshell

The script flipped: the product became the loss-leader that gets you in the door, and the relationship — the data, the points, the payment habit, the float — became the profit center, often worth more than the business selling the product.
