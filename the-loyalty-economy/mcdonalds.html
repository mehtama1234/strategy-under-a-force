Group A · The rewards flywheel

McDonald's — the burger is bait; the loyalty visit is the prize

It was already a real-estate company that sells burgers. Now it's a data company too.

McDonald's launched MyMcDonald's Rewards in the US in 2021 to turn itself from "a place you grab lunch" into "a place that knows you." The app collects your data, nudges you with personalized deals, and — crucially — gets you to come back far more often. That's the whole game.

The frequency multiplier

26 vs 10.5
Visits per year for a loyalty member vs a non-member — about 2.5×. Loyalty doesn't just keep customers; it makes each one buy far more often.
  • ~210 million 90-day active loyalty users by end-2025 (up ~19% in a year), across 70 markets.
  • Loyalty sales grew $30B (2024) → $37B (2025) — now ~26% of ~$140B in systemwide sales. Target: 250M members and $45B by 2027.

What McDonald's really is now

Three businesses in a trench coat

It was always a real-estate and franchise company that happens to sell burgers — it owns ~70% of its restaurant buildings and takes rent (10–15% of sales) plus royalties (4–5%), pocketing ~14–20% of every franchise dollar at near-zero operating risk. The loyalty push adds a third layer: a first-party customer-data company that tracks your habits and upsells you through the app.

The surprising part

Here's the elegant bit. A loyalty member who visits 2.5× more generates 2.5× more rent and royalties for McDonald's — but the franchisee bears the extra cost of making the food. The app is the wedge; the data is the prize; someone else flips the burgers. Loyalty also insulates McDonald's from cheaper rivals: once you're enrolled and earning points, you tilt toward the golden arches out of habit and convenience, not just price.

In one sentence

McDonald's in a nutshell

McDonald's is using loyalty and the app to make customers visit 2.5× more and hand over their data — multiplying its rent and royalties without lifting a burger, and quietly becoming a data company wearing a fast-food brand.
