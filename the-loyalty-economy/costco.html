Group B · Pay to belong

Costco — the membership IS the profit

It sells the groceries at almost no markup. The profit is the annual fee you pay to walk in.

Costco looks like a store, but it barely makes money selling you things. It sells goods at razor-thin margins — close to cost — on purpose. The actual profit comes from the annual membership fee you pay just for the right to shop there. The relationship is the business; the products are almost incidental.

The upside-down economics

~50%+
Share of Costco's operating profit that comes from membership fees (~$65–130/year), on a ~$250B revenue base. Strip out the fees and the retail operation makes almost nothing.

Renewal rates run around 90%+. The whole model is a bet that if it keeps prices unbeatable, you'll happily pay every year for the privilege — and keep renewing.

Why it works

The virtuous loop

Because the fee is the profit, Costco has no incentive to mark up the goods — so it passes savings straight to members, which makes the membership feel worth it, which drives renewals, which funds the low prices. The customer's loyalty and the company's profit point in the same direction — a rare, clean alignment in the loyalty economy.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The model lives or dies on trust in the value. The membership fee is the most visible price a member pays, so a big or clumsy fee hike (or any sense that the deals have slipped) risks a backlash and cancellations — the one number members watch most closely.

The surprising part

Costco is the loyalty economy in its purest, most honest form. Most companies dress up loyalty as "rewards" while quietly monetizing your data or float. Costco just charges an explicit fee to belong — and in return genuinely lowers your prices. It proves the core idea starkly: the recurring membership relationship, not the sale of any product, is where the money is.

In one sentence

Costco in a nutshell

Costco sells goods at near-cost and makes its profit almost entirely from the annual membership fee — the clearest proof that in the loyalty economy the relationship, not the product, is the business.
