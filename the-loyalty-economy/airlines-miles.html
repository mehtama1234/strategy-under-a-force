Group C · Loyalty worth more than the business

Airline miles — credit cards with wings

The frequent-flyer program is often worth more than the airline that owns it. That's not a metaphor.

Here's the most extreme version of the loyalty economy. An airline's frequent-flyer program is frequently valued higher than the entire airline. How? The airline doesn't just give you miles for flying — it manufactures miles at near-zero cost and sells them in bulk to credit-card banks, which hand them to cardholders as rewards. The flying is almost a side business.

The numbers are staggering

$31.7B vs ~$20B
Delta's SkyMiles program valuation vs Delta's entire market cap. Delta made ~$8.2B from its Amex card partnership in 2024 alone — roughly half its operating profit.
  • American's AAdvantage was appraised at $19.5–31.5B in 2020 — when American's whole market cap was ~$6.6B — and pledged as collateral for loans.
  • United's MileagePlus was valued at ~$21.3B securing a $5B loan (2020).
  • The four biggest US airlines earned ~$14B combined operating profit in 2024 — nearly all of it from card fees. Passenger flying roughly broke even.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

It's a lucrative treadmill. Miles get quietly "devalued" over time (a flight that cost 25,000 miles slowly needs more), and the whole thing leans on interchange — the ~2–3% swipe fee behind card rewards. If regulators cap those fees (as Europe did), the money that funds the miles shrinks, and the model wobbles.

The surprising part

The airline can sell the same mile twice — once to a bank in bulk, once to you as a promise of a future trip. And a mile costs almost nothing to create. So the "airline" is really a points-and-payments company that flies planes on the side — a credit card with wings. When you fly, you're generating the raw material for a financial-services business that dwarfs the airline's actual job of moving people.

In one sentence

Airline miles in a nutshell

Airlines make miles at near-zero cost and sell them in bulk to credit-card banks — so the loyalty program is often worth more than the whole airline, which mostly flies planes to feed a points business.
