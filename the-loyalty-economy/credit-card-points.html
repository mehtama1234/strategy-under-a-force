Group D · Monetizing the relationship

Credit-card points — everyone's prices fund the big spenders' rewards

The engine behind almost every loyalty program — and a quiet transfer from the poor to the rich.

Credit-card points are the hidden power source of the whole loyalty economy. Every time you swipe, the merchant pays the bank a fee (~2–3%, called "interchange"). The bank keeps some and hands some back to you as points or cash back. It feels like free money — but someone pays for it, and it isn't the bank.

How it works

  • Interchange (~2–3% per swipe) is the funding source. Merchants bake it into their prices, so everyone pays it — including people who use cash.
  • Amex is the purest version: premium annual fees (the Platinum went from $695 to $895 in 2025) plus interchange from big spenders, funding lavish rewards.
  • Airlines and hotels sell their miles in bulk to these banks — so the co-brand card is the thread tying every loyalty program together.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — a hidden regressive transfer

Because interchange is baked into prices, cash-payers and lower-income users subsidize the points of affluent cardholders. One estimate: the lowest-income households pay ~$21/year in higher prices to fund ~$750/year of rewards for the highest earners — a documented transfer from poor to rich, invisible in the sticker price. And regulators capping interchange (as Europe did) could collapse the rewards model.

The surprising part

Amex doesn't really need to sell you anything — it's a toll collector on a road it owns. Unlike most Visa/Mastercard cards (where the bank and the network are different companies), Amex runs both sides, so it takes a bigger slice of every swipe — which is why it can afford the most lavish perks. And points get quietly "devalued" over time (a hotel night that cost 50,000 points in 2015 might cost 75,000 now) with no disclosure required. The "free" rewards are neither free nor stable.

In one sentence

Credit-card points in a nutshell

Card points are funded by a ~2–3% swipe fee baked into everyone's prices — a quiet, regressive transfer that showers rewards on big spenders while cash-payers and the poor foot part of the bill.
