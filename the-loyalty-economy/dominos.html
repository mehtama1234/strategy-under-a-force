Group A · The rewards flywheel

Domino's — a tech company that happens to sell pizza

It stopped competing on the pizza and started competing on the ordering.

Domino's said the quiet part out loud: it calls itself "a tech company that happens to sell pizza." Its comeback wasn't about better dough — it was about making ordering so easy (app, one-click reorder, live tracking, loyalty points) that the experience, not the food, became the reason to choose it.

The digital pivot

~20% → ~70%
Share of US Domino's sales that are digital, roughly 2010 to 2023. Most orders now flow through its own app and website — where it owns the customer and the data.

Ordering tech plus a loyalty program that rewards repeat orders became the moat. A huge slice of employees are software and analytics people, not pizza people.

Why "own the order" matters

The app is the business

When you order direct through the Domino's app instead of a third-party delivery site, Domino's keeps the customer relationship, the data, and the margin — no marketplace taking a cut or hiding who you are. Loyalty points and saved orders make reordering frictionless, so you don't shop around. The pizza is the same; the habit is the product.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the moat is copyable

A software-and-convenience edge is real, but it's not permanent — rivals can build good apps too, and third-party delivery platforms (DoorDash, Uber Eats) sit between many restaurants and their customers, trying to own that relationship instead. Domino's advantage lasts only as long as its ordering stays meaningfully easier.

The surprising part

Domino's stock massively outperformed the market for a decade — not because pizza got better, but because it reframed itself as a technology and logistics company. It proved that in the loyalty economy, an "old" fast-food brand can win by treating the ordering system and the customer data as the real product, and the food as the thing that happens to arrive.

In one sentence

Domino's in a nutshell

Domino's won by competing on the app, not the pizza — pushing ~70% of sales through its own digital ordering to own the customer, the data, and the repeat habit.
