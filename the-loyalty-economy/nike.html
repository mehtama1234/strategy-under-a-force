Group B · Pay to belong

Nike — it quit the stores to own you directly

Nike walked away from the retailers that sold its shoes — to own the customer, the data, and the relationship.

For decades Nike sold its shoes through other people's stores — Foot Locker, department stores, Amazon. The problem: those middlemen owned the customer relationship and the data. So Nike made a big bet: pull back from wholesale and sell direct through its own apps, website, and stores — trading a wider shelf for a direct bond with the buyer.

The direct pivot

>40%
Share of Nike's revenue now from "Nike Direct" (its own apps, nike.com, and stores), up sharply as it deliberately cut wholesale relationships to control the customer.

Nike's membership (its apps + loyalty tier) is the tool: it turns an anonymous shoe buyer into a known member with an account, a purchase history, and a reason to come back.

Why own the customer?

The data and the margin

Selling direct gives Nike two things wholesale never did: the full margin (no retailer taking a cut) and the data (who you are, what you buy, what you browse) to personalize and market to you. It also controls the brand experience end to end, instead of seeing its shoes dumped on a discount rack.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — owning the customer is expensive

Going direct is costly and risky. You give up the vast reach of other people's stores, you have to run your own apps and logistics, and if the direct channel stumbles you've burned the wholesale relationships you'd need to fall back on. Nike has had to walk some of it back and rebuild retail partnerships.

The surprising part

Nike voluntarily shrank its own distribution — walking away from guaranteed sales on other companies' shelves — because it decided the customer relationship and the data were worth more than the reach. That's the loyalty economy in a sentence: a brand would rather sell to fewer people it owns than to more people it doesn't.

In one sentence

Nike in a nutshell

Nike pulled out of the retailers that sold its shoes to sell direct through its own apps — betting that owning the customer, the data, and the full margin beats the wider reach of someone else's shelf.
