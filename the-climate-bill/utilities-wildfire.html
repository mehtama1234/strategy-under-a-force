Utilities & wildfire — how power lines became a liability bomb

A hotter, drier world turned the electric grid into something that can bankrupt the company that owns it.

Hotter, drier conditions turned power lines into a liability bomb. When a utility's equipment sparks a wildfire, the utility can be on the hook for the damage — even billions. That single fact flipped power companies from safe, boring infrastructure into some of the most exposed businesses on the map.

The bankruptcies

~$30B
PG&E's wildfire liabilities after its equipment sparked California's deadly Camp Fire (2018). It filed for bankruptcy in 2019 — often called the first "climate-driven" corporate bankruptcy.
  • Hawaiian Electric faced billions in lawsuits and a stock collapse after the Maui/Lahaina fire (2023).
  • "Inverse condemnation" in California can make a utility pay for fire damage even without negligence — strict liability, no "act of God" defense.

The defensive scramble

To survive, utilities now do two expensive things: shut off power during high-fire-risk weather ("Public Safety Power Shutoffs," darkening hundreds of thousands of homes), and bury and harden the grid — PG&E alone committed to burying ~10,000 miles of line ($20B+). Those costs get passed to customers through higher bills.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

One more mega-fire sparked by a utility — Camp Fire scale or bigger — could push a mid-sized utility into insolvency before insurance and ratepayer funds can absorb it, destabilizing a regional power market and forcing a state bailout.

The surprising part

The strangest part is who ends up holding the bill. Not the lightning, not an arsonist, not "an act of God" — the utility. Strict liability rules out the usual defenses, insurance falls short, and ratepayers will only tolerate so much. So the company caught in the middle responds by cutting power (which hurts customers) and hiking bills (which hurts them more) — a kind of hidden climate tax delivered through blackouts and rate increases, landing hardest on the poorest customers.

In one sentence

Utilities & wildfire in a nutshell

A hotter, drier climate turned power lines into a liability bomb that bankrupted PG&E and now makes utilities the ones holding the wildfire bill — paid for through blackouts and higher bills.
