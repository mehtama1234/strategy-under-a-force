Group A · The risk-pricers

Property insurers — the canary that puts a number on the risk

Insurance is the first system forced to price climate risk in real dollars. So it's where the future shows up first.

Of everyone in this collection, insurers feel it first — because their whole job is to put a dollar number on risk, every year. As fires, floods, and storms worsened, they did the math, found whole states no longer profitable to cover, and responded bluntly: raise premiums, then simply stop writing policies. Insurance is the canary in the coal mine.

The retreat

  • California: State Farm — the biggest US home insurer — stopped writing new policies (May 2023), then began non-renewing ~72,000 existing California policies (2024). Allstate pulled back too.
  • Florida: a full-blown crisis — several insurers went insolvent or left, and premiums hit ~$2,500/year, roughly double the national average.
~400k → 1.3M
Policies dumped onto Florida's state-run "insurer of last resort" (Citizens), 2021 to 2024, as private insurers fled.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — uninsurable means unsellable

Here's the chain that matters: you can't get a mortgage on a house you can't insure. So when insurers leave, homes quietly become unmortgageable, then unsellable — and their value erodes before the next disaster ever hits. Climate strikes the property's worth through the insurance channel, not the flood.

The surprising part

The insurers didn't really solve their climate problem — they handed it to the public. The risk they dropped landed on state-run backstops (California's FAIR plan, Florida's Citizens), which can't raise prices fast enough to cover it. So the next big hurricane or wildfire season sends the bill to taxpayers, not shareholders. The companies quietly moved the liability from their balance sheet onto everyone's.

In one sentence

Property insurers in a nutshell

Insurers are the first to price climate risk in hard dollars, so they're the first to flee — and by leaving, they quietly de-value the homes in harm's way and shove the catastrophe risk onto states and taxpayers.
