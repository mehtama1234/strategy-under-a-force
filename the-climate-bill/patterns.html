The patterns

Line up everyone a hotter planet touched, and one theme dominates: the risk shows up as a bill — priced first by insurers — long before the flood.

Each page asks one question: what did a hotter planet make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions keep showing up. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

A stable climate was a hidden subsidy — you could build, farm, and insure assuming the weather stayed put, and never had to price the risk. A hotter planet cancels the subsidy and sends the bill. Value flows to whoever can price, defend against, or escape the risk; it drains from whoever sits in harm's way. And you feel it first not as a flood, but as an insurance premium or a grocery price.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Insurance is the canary — the bill comes before the flood

Insurance is the first system forced to put a hard number on climate risk, so it moves first. The premium spikes, then the insurer leaves, long before the water actually arrives. Watch insurance and you're watching the future get priced in real time.

Pattern 02

Uninsurable → unmortgageable → unsellable

The risk travels through paperwork, not water. You can't get a mortgage on a home you can't insure, so when insurers flee, the home quietly loses its value — before any disaster. Climate hits the balance sheet through the insurance channel first.

property insurerscoastal real estate
Pattern 03

The bill lands on whoever can't pass it on

Insurers dump risk onto state backstops and taxpayers; utilities get stuck with wildfire liability and pass it to ratepayers; carbon prices hit consumers' heating bills. The cost keeps rolling downhill until it hits someone who can't hand it off.

taxpayersratepayersconsumers
Pattern 04

Selling defense against the risk is the winning business

The clearest winners don't sit in harm's way — they help others price it, defend against it, or model it. Cooling, water tech, resilience building, risk data, and the insurers who reprice fast all sell shelter from the same storm.

risk datacoolingwaterresilience
Pattern 05

Adaptation is a luxury good — it widens the gap

Defending yourself costs money, so the rich harden, insure, and relocate while the poor absorb the hit. From backup generators to AC to drought-proof seeds, the same force that creates the defense market also makes climate a driver of inequality.

resiliencecoolingfarmingwater
Pattern 06

The frequent medium disaster, not the rare monster, breaks things

It wasn't the headline hurricane that repriced insurance — it was the "secondary perils," the floods, wildfires, and hailstorms that weren't even modeled a decade ago. Death by a thousand mid-sized disasters, not one big one.

reinsurerswildfire
Pattern 07

The map moves — crops, wine, skiing, safe living shift poleward

Warming relocates the zones where you can grow, ski, or comfortably live — toward the poles and up the mountains. Yorkshire rivals Bordeaux; the Great Lakes market themselves as havens; old strongholds hollow out.

farmingtourismmigration
Pattern 08

People and markets lag the physics

The change is real, but the response is slow and even backwards: coastal prices stay sticky, people keep moving toward the heat, and carbon schemes stay too weak to bite. The crash and the reckoning keep getting deferred — until the bill forces the issue.

Seven forces, one method

The point of doing all seven

This is the seventh collection built the same way — after AI, Ozempic, the decoupling, the end of cheap money, the energy transition, and the demographic cliff. Each force has its own signature: AI's is "you can only trust what you can verify," Ozempic's is "one force, many industries," the decoupling's is "the policy backfires and the walls leak," cheap money's is "the tide went out and revealed who was swimming naked," the transition's is "a fuel you rent forever becomes a machine you build once," the cliff's is "for 200 years there were always more people coming — that just stopped," and the climate bill's is "a stable climate was a hidden subsidy — and the bill is coming due, priced first by insurers." But the method is identical: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group by the answer, and read the patterns. Point it at anything big and the stories fall out.
