Group A · The risk-pricers

Climate-risk data — when the score itself moves the market

Once climate risk became a real cost, everyone needed a number for it. A whole new industry grew to sell those numbers.

Once climate risk turned into a real dollar cost, everyone — insurers, banks, mortgage lenders, real-estate firms, big companies, regulators — suddenly needed to answer a precise question: how exposed is this exact building, farm, or port to flood, fire, and heat? That created a new industry that does one thing: put a number on the risk.

The players and the shift

  • The incumbents — Moody's RMS and Verisk — built catastrophe models for insurers and rushed to climate-adapt them.
  • The newcomers — Jupiter Intelligence, First Street Foundation — sell granular, street-level physical-risk scores.
  • The big change: around 2021, First Street's flood and wildfire scores appeared on Zillow and Realtor.com — putting a risk grade in front of ordinary home buyers for the first time.
  • Regulators and banks now demand climate-risk disclosure and stress-test loan books against it.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — model risk cuts both ways

A model that under-counts risk leaves banks and insurers exposed when losses hit. A model that over-counts can crash a housing market instantly — buyers flee, prices drop, refinancing fails — and that cascades into mortgages, city tax bases, and pension funds. The repricing can move faster than the financial system can absorb.

The surprising part

The models don't just measure the risk — the act of measuring creates the repricing. Homeowners in Florida or California didn't suddenly get riskier the day First Street published a score; they became priced as risky the moment the score showed up on a listing. The number becomes a market signal that can trigger a cascade. And there's a scaling shock underneath: these models were built to answer "insure this one warehouse," not "price every home in America" — stretching them that far introduces brand-new uncertainty.

In one sentence

Climate-risk data in a nutshell

When climate risk became a real cost, a whole industry grew to score it — and because a bad score on a home listing reprices the house on the spot, the model stopped being a tool and became a market-mover.
