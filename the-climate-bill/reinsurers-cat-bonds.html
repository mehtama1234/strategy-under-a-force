Group A · The risk-pricers

Reinsurers & cat bonds — the ones who set the ceiling

Behind your home insurer stands a bigger insurer. When it repriced, everyone below had to follow.

Reinsurers (Munich Re, Swiss Re, Hannover Re) are the insurance behind the insurance — they cover the insurers against catastrophes. As disasters got bigger and more frequent, they repriced hard and pulled back from the riskiest layers. And because a few of them quietly set the price of catastrophe risk for the whole world, when they retreat, your local home insurer has no choice but to follow.

The hard reset

+30–50%
Jump in reinsurance prices at the January 2023 renewals — the steepest in a generation, after a decade of cheap rates.
  • Catastrophe bonds — where insurers pass disaster risk to investors who earn fat yields (~6–8%) but lose their money if a named disaster hits — hit record issuance (~$15–17B a year, 2023–24) as capital markets became the new shock absorber.
  • Insured catastrophe losses now routinely top $100 billion a year (they did in 2017, 2021, 2022…).

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the spiral

If reinsurers keep retreating, capital markets demand ever-higher cat-bond yields to fill the gap; higher yields make disaster cover more expensive; insurers raise premiums or quit — and eventually whole regions become uninsurable at any sane price. Each step feeds the next.

The surprising part

The damage came from an unexpected direction. It wasn't the giant, headline hurricanes — it was the "secondary perils": floods, wildfires, and hailstorms that weren't even on reinsurers' radar a decade ago. A single bad wildfire season or European summer flood can now rack up $20–40 billion in losses and redraw the risk map overnight. The frequent medium-sized disaster, not the rare monster, turned out to be the thing that broke the models — and forced reinsurers to rebuild them from scratch.

In one sentence

Reinsurers & cat bonds in a nutshell

Climate change made frequent medium disasters the new normal, forcing the reinsurers who set the world's price for catastrophe to reprice and retreat — which pushed the risk onto capital markets and left whole regions facing unaffordable or vanishing cover.
