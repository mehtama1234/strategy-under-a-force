Group C · The adaptation winners

Resilience & building — defending against disaster is a growth industry

Sea walls, fireproofing, backup generators, hardened grids. As disasters worsen, "resilience" became a market.

As storms, floods, and fires intensify, physically defending against them turned into a growth business — sea walls and flood barriers, fireproofing, elevating houses, backup power, and hardened grids. Repeated disasters make avoidance impossible, and new building codes make preparation mandatory.

The market is real

+70%
Jump in Generac's sales in 2021 (backup home generators), as California blackouts and the Texas winter storm triggered a run on backup power. Its stock soared, then cooled.
  • Flood defense: US federal spending roughly quadrupled (~$5B → $15B, 2019–22); the global flood-barrier market is growing ~8% a year.
  • Grid hardening: trillions budgeted worldwide (~$1T in the US alone) as weather-driven outages multiply.
  • The payoff is huge: FEMA found resilient building features cost ~1.5% more but return ~$11 for every $1 spent — yet only ~21% of hazard-prone US areas require them.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — adaptation is a luxury good

Resilience is becoming something the rich can buy and the poor can't. Wealthy homeowners and nations harden themselves; low-income and minority communities face far higher disaster death tolls with no defense. The gap doesn't just persist — it widens, edging toward a "climate apartheid" where recovery takes some people weeks and others generations.

The surprising part

The defenses meant to reduce risk often just move it. A sea wall that protects one stretch of coast speeds erosion on the next; barriers that block water make flooding worse behind them; hard defenses crush the mangroves and marshes that used to absorb surge naturally. It's called "maladaptation" — and it locks us into doing the same thing bigger and bigger (higher walls, more generators) instead of rethinking why we're so exposed in the first place.

In one sentence

Resilience & building in a nutshell

Defending against a wilder climate became a booming business — but one that enriches the rich faster than it protects the poor, and often just shoves the danger onto the next stretch of coast.
