Group D · The big shifts

Migration & havens — people are still moving toward the risk

In theory, a hotter planet should send people to cool, safe places. In practice, they're doing the opposite.

As heat, fire, flood, and drought worsen, you'd expect cool, water-rich, disaster-safe places to become more desirable and dangerous ones less. A few US cities are even marketing themselves as "climate havens." But the real story is a paradox: Americans are still moving in huge numbers toward the most climate-exposed places.

The havens — and the paradox

Marketing safety

Duluth, Buffalo, Cincinnati, Vermont, and the Great Lakes region (cool, lots of fresh water, few disasters) began pitching themselves as "climate havens" around 2019–2021.

Moving to danger

Yet Phoenix (extreme heat), Florida (hurricanes), and Texas (heat, floods) keep gaining people — for jobs, cheap housing, and no state income tax.

~216M
People the World Bank estimated could be displaced within their own countries by climate impacts by 2050 — mostly in the Global South.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The largest displacement won't be tidy US moves — it'll be mass migration in the Global South (Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Central America) over the next 20–30 years, creating refugee and geopolitical pressure on richer nations. That's disorderly and destabilizing, not a planned relocation.

The surprising part

People are moving toward climate risk, not away from it — for now. Today's economics (a tech job in Phoenix, cheap housing in Texas, no income tax in Florida) simply beats tomorrow's climate projection in how humans actually decide. The "climate haven" idea is still niche; most people can't pick a home based on 2050 forecasts. The reversal only comes when the risk shows up as a bill people can't ignore — unaffordable insurance, water rationing, grid failures. The physics is ahead of the map; the money and the moving vans lag behind.

In one sentence

Migration & havens in a nutshell

A hotter planet should be reshuffling where people live, but for now they're still chasing jobs and cheap housing into the hottest, riskiest places — because today's economics beats tomorrow's climate until the bill arrives.
