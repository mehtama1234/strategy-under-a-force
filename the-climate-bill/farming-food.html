Farming & food — you feel it first in coffee, cocoa, and orange juice

Heat and drought are cutting yields in the world's best farmland — and you taste it at the checkout.

Heat, drought, and erratic weather cut crop yields and are shifting where things can grow. For most people, the clearest way you feel a hotter planet isn't a flood — it's your grocery bill. Specific foods spike as the regions that grow them get hit. Call it "heatflation."

The price shocks you can taste

record cocoa
Cocoa hit an all-time high near $12,000/tonne (late 2024) after heat and disease wrecked West Africa's crop (Ghana, Ivory Coast). Your chocolate bar got smaller and pricier.
  • Coffee (arabica) topped $3/lb in 2024–25 — the highest in over a decade — after drought in Brazil and Vietnam.
  • Olive oil roughly tripled after Spain's drought cut output to a 40-year low (2023–24).
  • Orange juice hit records as Florida groves were battered by storms and disease.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — many breadbaskets failing at once

Individual crop failures are survivable. The nightmare is several major growing regions failing in the same season — coffee, cocoa, wheat, rice together. The world holds only ~3 months of grain stocks as a buffer, and no amount of trade can absorb a 20–30% global supply shock. Water makes it worse: farming uses ~70% of fresh water, and key aquifers are draining.

The surprising part

Rich countries are quietly adapting faster than the headlines suggest — seed companies are planting heat-tolerant varieties, growing zones are creeping toward Canada and Russia, and big traders hedge with futures and geographic spread. But adaptation costs money — new seeds, new irrigation, crop insurance — and the world's poorest two billion (subsistence farmers in Africa and South Asia) can't afford it. So climate quietly widens the gap: it's a price hike for the rich world, but a food-security collapse for the poor.

In one sentence

Farming & food in a nutshell

Heat and drought are hitting the world's best crop regions at once, spiking the price of everyday foods — a manageable grocery bump for rich countries that can adapt, but a threat to survival for the poor who can't.
