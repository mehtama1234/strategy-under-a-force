Group B · Who's doing it, and how

Lending to buyouts

Most of these loans fund private-equity buyouts — and increasingly the buyer and the lender are the same club.

The great majority of private-credit loans finance leveraged buyouts: a private-equity firm buys a company with borrowed money, and increasingly that money comes from a private-credit fund — sometimes run by the same firm or a close partner. So the same industry both buys companies and lends the debt to buy them, a tightening loop where the lender has every reason to say yes.

The core bet

Private credit and private equity became a self-reinforcing machine. The PE firm levers up a company; a private-credit fund provides the debt; and when the two are intertwined, there's no arm's-length party negotiating terms or pushing back on the price. It powers the buyout machine — and hides the conflict inside it.

The buyer controls the lender's incentives

When a buyout starts to fail, the borrower effectively controls the lender's incentives. The private-credit lender can't easily foreclose, because the same sponsor may manage that fund's capital, and blowing up the deal tanks the fund's returns and reputation. So instead of a clean default, the lender extends the maturity, loosens the covenants, or rolls in more debt — quietly keeping zombie companies alive.

Who ends up ahead

The big firms that own the whole chain — buying, financing, working out, and selling, all in-house.

Who's exposed

Outside stakeholders and, ultimately, the funds’ investors — nobody's left to say "that price is insane."

The single biggest worry

If the economy weakens and defaults hit across a portfolio of intertwined, PE-backed companies at once, the private-credit fund becomes an unwilling equity owner in a fire sale it can't liquidate. The cozy loop that made buying cheap in good times becomes a chain reaction with no outside party holding the brake.

The surprising part

Private credit was sold as freeing borrowers from bank-covenant tyranny. Instead the biggest PE firms just built their own captive lenders — so one mega-firm can now buy a company, finance it with its own fund, manage the workout, and decide when to sell. It looks like competition (five lenders) but works like alignment (the buyer and lender are friends), with no external stakeholder to object.

In one sentence

Private equity persuaded the market to let it become its own lender — turning the buyout into a self-financing loop where the buyer's and lender's interests perfectly align, until they don't.
