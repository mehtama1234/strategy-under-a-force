Group B · Who's doing it, and how

Insurance is the fuel

The masterstroke was buying the insurers — turning retirees’ annuity savings into permanent fuel for private loans.

The quiet engine of the whole boom is insurance. Life insurers and annuity companies collect premiums today and pay out decades later, sitting on enormous pools of long-term money — a perfect match for long-term private loans. So the big firms bought or built insurers: Apollo merged with Athene, KKR bought Global Atlantic, Blackstone and Brookfield followed. Retirees’ savings became "permanent capital."

The core bet

Alternative managers discovered that owning an annuity book gives them decades-long money that never runs for the exits — the ideal funding for illiquid loans. By acquiring insurers, they solved the eternal problem of matching assets to liabilities, and kept the management fees flowing on top.

A conflict hidden inside the structure

The setup is elegant and uncomfortable: retirees’ annuity money — bought with life savings and promised a fixed return — funds the manager's own riskier loans. The manager wants high-yielding assets to boost profits; the insurance subsidiary is supposed to want safety to meet its promises. When those two pull apart, the policyholder's security rides on the affiliate's credit discipline.

Who ends up ahead

The managers — who gain a huge, stable, low-cost funding base that can't be pulled out from under them.

Who's exposed

Annuity holders and retirees, whose money funds affiliated loans priced inside opaque, hard-to-see networks.

The single biggest worry

The conflict is structural, not occasional. If affiliated loans sour — which can take years to surface — the insurer may face capital erosion or liquidity strain, and regulators have limited tools to force quick action. The people bearing the risk are ordinary annuity holders who never chose it.

The surprising part

Insurance oversight is state-based and deferential to internal models, not market prices. So affiliated private-credit deals funded by insurance capital often never face the pricing discipline that outside investors would impose — the true leverage and concentration stay hidden from regulators and from the very policyholders whose savings are at stake.

In one sentence

Insurance turned retirees’ annuity savings into a permanent private-credit funding moat that regulators can barely see, because it happens inside opaque affiliate networks shielded by state insurance law.
