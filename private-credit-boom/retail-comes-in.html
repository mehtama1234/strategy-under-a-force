Group D · The mechanism & the moves

Retail comes in

Having tapped the pensions and insurers, the giants now want your 401(k) — and the gates are already appearing.

The institutional pool is nearly tapped out, so the big firms turned to ordinary savers. They built semi-liquid "perpetual" and "interval" funds (Blackstone's BCRED and its non-traded peers) and pushed regulators to let private credit into 401(k) retirement plans. It's sold as "access for everyone" — but it puts illiquid, hard-to-value loans into the hands of people who may need their money back, behind redemption limits ("gates").

The core bet

Private credit's next frontier is retail money. By wrapping illiquid loans in funds that feel liquid, the giants aim to unlock the vast 401(k) pool. The catch is a fundamental mismatch: multi-year loans behind a promise of quarterly cash — a promise that holds only until everyone asks at once.

A liquid promise on an illiquid asset

The whole pitch rests on "managed liquidity" — monthly or quarterly redemption windows with strict caps. In calm times it works. But the underlying loans are 3–7 year commitments that can't be sold quickly without steep haircuts, while retail savers believe they can get out each quarter. That gap is the entire risk.

Who ends up ahead

The managers — who unlock a vast new pool of fee-paying capital and harvest fees up front.

Who's exposed

Retail savers, who get the downside of illiquidity but were sold the accessibility of a mutual fund.

The single biggest worry

When panic hits — a downturn, rising defaults, or just fear — redemption requests spike past the caps, funds slam the gates, and ordinary investors are locked out of their own money for months. A wave of gated funds hitting millions of savers at once would amplify a crisis and force managers to fire-sale loans or freeze accounts — exactly the run the lock-up was supposed to prevent, just relocated to retail.

The surprising part

BCRED — marketed as the safest alternative because it holds senior secured loans to "quality" businesses — gated its own fund in June 2026 despite every reassurance. That single event confirmed the skeptics: there's no such thing as truly liquid private credit. The moment retail owns meaningful sums, any stress hits a hard ceiling, and the savers who were promised stock-market-style access instead get their cash locked away.

In one sentence

Having saturated institutional money, private-credit firms are selling hard-to-sell loans to retirement savers as "democratization" — betting the gates won't slam until after the fees are harvested.
