Group B · Who's doing it, and how

The giants

A handful of firms — Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, Blue Owl — quietly became the new banks of Wall Street.

The names behind private credit are the big "alternative asset managers." They made their reputations buying companies (private equity); now their largest growth engine is lending to them. Five firms — Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, and the younger Blue Owl — control roughly half of a ~$1.5–1.8 trillion market, each managing hundreds of billions in credit.

The core bet

These managers realized lending is more scalable than owning: you can manage a handful of buyouts, but thousands of loans. They sit between retreating banks and capital-hungry borrowers, earn a fat spread plus fees, and fund it with sticky, long-term money. That's where the trillion-plus went.

What the numbers say

~$550B

~$500B
Blackstone's credit — which overtook its famous real-estate arm in fee-earning assets by 2023.
~$300B+
Blue Owl's credit — the youngest of the five and the fastest-growing.
~4–6% over SOFR
the median spread these firms charge, versus ~0.5–1% for old-style bank loans.

They systematized leverage

These managers deliberately avoid the front-page drama of owning companies — but they're everywhere, lending to the same mid-sized firms private equity owns, financing roll-ups, and covering cash shortfalls at portfolio companies. They aren't running from leverage; they're systematizing it. Lending is infinitely scalable in a way that owning never was — and that scalability is the whole story of the boom.

Who ends up ahead

The five giants — collecting fees and spread on hundreds of billions each, valued richly by the stock market.

Who's exposed

Traditional banks displaced from lending; and investors if the fat yields compress or the loans sour.

The single biggest worry

As money floods in and competition heats up, managers race to deploy capital into ever-riskier borrowers just to keep the yields up. Much of it is lent to already-indebted companies, so a recession — or even a mid-cycle stumble — could force large write-downs across a highly concentrated set of firms, with no liquid market to sell into.

The surprising part

The genius wasn't lending skill — it was scale and fees. A firm earns management fees on the whole pool whether or not each loan is brilliant, and the stock market values those steady fees at rich multiples. So the incentive is to gather and deploy more capital, fast — which is exactly how five firms came to hold roughly half of a trillion-dollar-plus lending market.

In one sentence

A few alternative-asset giants became the new banks — capturing the spread, the duration risk, and the credit losses that regulated banks no longer wanted to hold.
