Group A · Why the banks retreated

Banks retreated

Rules written to make banks safer worked — by pushing risky lending out of banks and into the lightly-watched shadows.

After the 2008 crash, regulators made banks hold much more capital against risky loans (Basel III, Dodd-Frank, the Volcker Rule). Holding a mid-sized company's buyout loan became expensive and unattractive, so banks simply backed away from that business. The lending didn't stop being needed — it moved to private-credit funds that face almost none of those same rules.

The core bet

Post-2008 rules made banks safer by making risky loans costly to hold — but demand for those loans didn't shrink, it relocated. Private-credit funds, free of bank capital rules and public disclosure, stepped into the gap with money that had nowhere else to go.

A bank in everything but the rules

Private credit didn't just fill a gap — for some deals it became preferable. Because these funds face no capital rules and no public disclosure, they can do things a bank can't: hold a deeply illiquid loan for years, wait out distress without quarterly-earnings pressure, and keep a struggling loan marked at par where a bank would be forced to write it down. Regulation didn't just move the volume; it created an incentive to do the riskier deals off in the shadows.

Who ends up ahead

Private-credit funds — unregulated "banks" that lend the same money with higher fees and no capital charge.

Who's exposed

Banks (pushed out of a profitable business) and, in a crisis, everyone — because the shadow system has no Fed backstop.

The single biggest worry

The rules worked at their stated goal: banks are safer and less leveraged. But credit demand didn't vanish — it moved into a ~$2 trillion system with no discount window, no deposit insurance, and no lender of last resort. If a cluster of funds takes losses in the next downturn, there's no central bank to stabilize them the way there is for banks.

The surprising part

The regulation succeeded and backfired at once. By caging risk inside the banks, it birthed a parallel banking system running on the very same pension and insurance money — doing higher-risk deals, with less transparency, and no safety net underneath.

In one sentence

Rules meant to cage bank risk instead built an unregulated shadow-banking system on the same money — higher risk, less disclosure, and no backstop.
