Group C · The risks & the dark corners

What happens in a downturn

Private credit grew up in a sunny economy. It has never lived through a real recession at $2 trillion.

The whole industry expanded during a mostly benign, growing economy and, until 2022, near-zero rates. It has simply never been tested by a deep recession at its current ~$2 trillion size. Higher rates since 2022 already squeezed borrowers hard — floating-rate loans got dramatically more expensive overnight — and more borrowers now pay interest with fresh debt instead of cash. The warning signs are already blinking.

The core bet

Private credit is a $2 trillion bet on stable borrower cash flows, placed during a 15-year bull run it never had to survive. A real recession — with default cascades and forced selling into an illiquid market — is the great untested unknown.

The gate, not the default, is where it breaks

Even a modest rise in defaults — from 2% to a recession-normal 4–5% — could trigger a liquidity crisis: funds promised investors quarterly or annual redemptions but hold loans nobody will buy in a downturn. Borrowers default → funds can't sell → gates come down → redemption requests pile up → forced haircuts on any exit. Unlike banks, these funds have no central-bank backstop and no required capital buffer.

Who ends up ahead

Funds with genuinely patient capital and low leverage that can wait out the cycle without gating.

Who's exposed

Investors who believed they could redeem, and borrowers who suddenly can't refinance in a frozen market.

The single biggest worry

A $200–400 billion forced sale of private-credit loans into a recession could crater valuations industry-wide, forcing mark-downs on portfolios still carried at peak prices — with no Fed backstop and no capital buffers to absorb it. And because the marks were smoothed for years, the drop, when it comes, could be violent.

The surprising part

Private credit was supposed to be safer than banks — no runs, better underwriting. Instead it copied the fragility without the circuit-breakers: the funds use leverage themselves, chase riskier deals as competition heats up, and assume they can hold to maturity. The size and opacity mean a local shock can become a systemic one in hours if one big fund gates and contagion panic follows.

In one sentence

Private credit's $2 trillion bet on easy cash flows and easy exits has never met a real recession — and it's the redemption gates, not the defaults, where the crisis is most likely to ignite.
