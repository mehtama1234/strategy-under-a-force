Group E · The big picture

The private-credit boom

Lending moved out of the regulated banks into a $2-trillion parallel system — priced by a model, not a market.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. Since 2008, corporate lending migrated out of regulated banks and into a ~$2 trillion system of private-credit funds — fueled by insurance and pension money hunting yield, lending mostly to private-equity buyouts, run by a handful of giants, and now reaching for ordinary retail savings. The deep change: risk moved from bank balance sheets, where it's marked to market and subject to runs, into opaque pools where it's modeled, not priced.

The core bet

This isn't a shortage of credit — it's a relocation of it. The bank got disintermediated, and the question underneath everything is whether the new system is safer because the capital is patient and can't run, or riskier because the risk is hidden, concentrated in a few firms, and never tested in a real crisis.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

The managers (fees on assets, carry on gains); PE firms (cheap, flexible, quiet capital); insurers and pensions (yield without daily volatility); and borrowers who get money banks won't lend.

Losers

Regulated banks (pushed out of mid-market lending); retail savers pulled in near the top; distressed companies with one lender who owns them; and the pension and annuity holders who bear the tail risk they never chose.

The single biggest worry

The safety of the whole system rests on a philosophical bet: that patient, informed capital can manage tail risk better than regulated banks with a taxpayer backstop. The bull case — no runs, no fire sales, restructure quietly through the cycle — is real in calm times. The bear case is that it has never been stress-tested at scale: there's been no deep recession since private credit passed a trillion dollars, the low default data is a curated sample from a benign era, and the assets are opaque and illiquid, so if one mega-fund seizes, nobody knows how far the damage spreads through the pensions and insurers behind it.

The surprising part

The boom is, at bottom, a bet that risk is handled more safely by a few enormous, concentrated firms than by a dispersed banking system — a reversal that looks a lot like too-big-to-fail in new clothing. The other twist: it works best when growth is slow and rates are high, which keep yields fat and money flowing. A sharp rate cut could compress returns, send retail money fleeing, and expose the fragility — the opposite of what most people would guess.

In one sentence

Since 2008 lending migrated from regulated banks into giant, semi-opaque private-credit funds fueled by insurance and pension money — a system that's either safer because the capital is patient, or riskier because it's concentrated and has never been tested in a real crisis.
