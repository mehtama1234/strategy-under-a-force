Group C · The risks & the dark corners

Safer, or just hidden?

Is private credit genuinely safer than bank lending — or did the risk just move somewhere darker?

Here's the honest two-sided question. The optimistic case: this money is patient (locked up for years, so no bank run), losses fall on sophisticated investors rather than taxpayers, and one lender can quietly restructure a troubled loan instead of forcing a fire sale. The pessimistic case: the risk didn't shrink — it just moved to a place with less disclosure, less oversight, and no track record in a crisis.

The core bet

Private credit claims to remove bank-run risk with patient, locked-up capital and to keep losses away from taxpayers. That bet rests on two untested assumptions: that illiquidity really does prevent fire sales in a panic, and that opacity defers pain rather than multiplying it.

Patient in the calm, untested in the storm

Private credit really may be safer in normal times: a single lender can nurse a struggling company along, where a scattered bank syndicate turns twitchy and forces a bloodbath. But that same patience breeds moral hazard — sponsors hold onto turkeys longer because there's no daily repricing or margin call. The result is a portfolio of the "living dead": companies not technically in default but economically insolvent, on maturities extended a cycle or two.

Who ends up ahead

In calm times: borrowers who get nursed along, and investors spared the panic of a public sell-off.

Who's exposed

In a real storm: pension and insurance investors, whose "locked-up" losses will still land in retirement accounts.

The single biggest worry

The lock-up cuts both ways. The moment one mega-fund signals illiquidity — gating redemptions — investors realize they own something they can't sell in a downturn, and panic follows even if the loans aren't yet bad. There's no lender of last resort, so the losses don't disappear — they crystallize all at once, and wash into the pension and insurance money behind the funds.

The surprising part

The safety and the danger are the same feature. Because there's no forced repricing, deterioration stays hidden, so the true loss rate in a downturn may not be the benign 2–3% — it could be 10–15%, because the accounting concealed the decay until the dam broke. Private credit may not have reduced risk so much as delayed and concentrated it.

In one sentence

Private credit didn't remove risk so much as delay recognizing it — handing the eventual bill to pensions and insurers, who can't stage a bank run but also can't easily absorb the loss.
