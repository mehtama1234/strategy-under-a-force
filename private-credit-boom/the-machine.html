Group D · The mechanism & the moves

The machine

Gather money that can't be pulled out, lend it at a fat spread, and collect fees on the whole pile.

Strip away the mystique and the business is a simple, powerful machine. Step one: gather long-term money from pensions, insurers, and increasingly rich individuals — money locked in for a decade. Step two: lend it privately to companies at high floating rates. Step three: pocket the spread between what you pay investors and charge borrowers. Step four: collect management and performance fees on the entire pool. The magic ingredient is capital that can't run.

The core bet

Private credit is permanent (or near-permanent) capital lent out at a spread, with fees layered on top. Because the money is locked up for 10+ years, there's no redemption pressure to force discipline — the manager compounds leverage and earns fees on assets whether or not each loan is brilliant.

Fees reward deploying, not picking well

The permanent-capital structure removes the natural brake. If a public fund stumbles, redemptions force discipline; a private-credit fund can ride out losses for a decade, so loans made in a frothy year might not face consequences for eight more. As dry powder piles up and spreads compress, managers race to deploy into ever-weaker borrowers — because the fee machine rewards deploying often, not deploying well.

Who ends up ahead

The managers — whose fee-related earnings are steady, scalable, and prized by the stock market.

Who's exposed

Investors, if a decade of loosely-disciplined lending surfaces its losses all at once in a downturn.

The single biggest worry

The permanent capital that makes the machine so profitable also removes the discipline that would stop bad lending. Hundreds of billions in illiquid loans to unrated companies could hit stress simultaneously, with no liquid secondary market to offload them — and the managers, paid on assets gathered rather than risk taken, have limited incentive to slow down.

The surprising part

The real genius isn't credit skill — it's fee arbitrage on money that can't leave. Investors decided they'd rather have stable, predictable fees than volatile returns, and regulators allowed decade-long lock-ups because pensions and insurers have long horizons. The upshot: a mediocre lender with permanent capital beats a brilliant one who must return money every year.

In one sentence

Private credit turned the certainty of fees on locked-up capital into an asset more valuable than the loans themselves — a machine that profits faster from deploying money than from picking good credits.
