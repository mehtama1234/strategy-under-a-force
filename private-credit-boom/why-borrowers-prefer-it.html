Group D · The mechanism & the moves

Why borrowers prefer it

Companies pay a point or three more to borrow privately — and choose it anyway. Here's why.

Odd as it sounds, borrowers often pay a higher rate to a private-credit fund than they'd pay in public markets — and still prefer it. What they're buying isn't cheap money; it's certainty (a deal that closes in days and is guaranteed to fund), privacy (no public disclosure of their numbers), flexibility (custom terms, the option to pay interest in more debt when cash is tight), and a single lender to renegotiate with instead of a scattered crowd of bondholders.

The core bet

Borrowers pay a 1–3% premium because private credit sells what public markets can't: speed, certainty, secrecy, and a single flexible relationship. When the alternative is a months-long public roadshow, that certainty is easily worth a couple of extra points.

Certainty became the scarce good

The trade is speed-and-secrecy for liquidity. Custom covenants tied to the borrower's actual business (a software firm's revenue, a manufacturer's seasonal swings) replace the standardized terms public markets demand. And if trouble hits, there's one relationship manager to call, not a faceless crowd — so restructurings happen quietly and fast.

Who ends up ahead

Borrowers who value speed, privacy, and flexibility — especially PE-owned and sensitive-industry companies.

Who's exposed

Those same borrowers if their single lender freezes — with no syndicate to step in, they're stuck.

The single biggest worry

The dark side of a single-lender relationship is total dependence. If that lender hits trouble, marks the loan down, or decides to exit, there's no backup syndicate. When funds gated or slowed new lending in recent stress, their borrowers got stuck — unable to refinance, forced into fire sales, or hit with worse terms when capital reopened. A public bond keeps trading; a private loan just stops moving.

The surprising part

Private credit even makes leverage feel cheaper psychologically, because the privacy removes the shame. No quarterly earnings call where analysts grill you on debt ratios; the lender knows the stress and accepts it, the market never sees it. So companies — especially PE sponsors — borrow more aggressively in private than they ever would in public, layering on leverage the market would punish as a distress signal.

In one sentence

Borrowers pay more for private credit because speed, privacy, and a single trusted relationship made certainty the scarce good — worth a few extra points when the alternative is a public roadshow and thousands of faceless investors.
