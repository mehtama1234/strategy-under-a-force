Group A · Why the banks retreated

The yield hunt

A decade of near-zero rates forced pensions and insurers — the most cautious money there is — to reach for risk.

This is the fuel behind the whole boom. A pension fund legally needs to earn around 7% a year to pay future retirees; through the 2010s, safe government bonds paid closer to 2%. Insurers had the same squeeze. Private-credit funds dangled 8–12% yields, and the cautious institutions poured in — betting that locking money up for years was worth the extra return.

The core bet

Near-zero interest rates created an unbearable gap between what conservative institutions had to earn and what safe bonds paid. Private credit's 8–12% yield was the escape — so pensions and insurers took on illiquid, hard-to-exit loans they'd never have touched in a normal-rate world.

What the numbers say

~2% vs ~7%
what Treasuries paid in the 2010s versus what a typical pension needed to stay solvent — an impossible gap.
8–12%
the yields private credit offered — 6–10 points above safe bonds.
~2–3%
the "illiquidity premium" — the extra return paid just for locking money up for 5–7 years.
5% → 15%
the rise in pension allocations to private credit from 2010 to 2023, roughly tripling.
$50B → $300B+
US life insurers' private-credit holdings, 2015 to 2023 — matching long-dated promises with high-yield loans.

When the geometry forces the gamble

The perverse result: the institutions most afraid of risk became the ones taking the biggest bets. It wasn't greed — it was arithmetic. At 2% Treasury yields the math of their liabilities simply didn't work, so zero rates didn't just encourage risk-taking, they effectively mandated it for the very funds whose whole purpose is stability.

Who ends up ahead

The fund managers earning fees on the inflows; borrowers getting abundant capital; retirees if the loans pay off.

Who's exposed

Pensions and insurers if the yields prove to be hidden credit risk, not free lunch — and the retirees behind them.

The single biggest worry

The 8–12% may have been partly an illusion — an illiquidity premium that was really compensation for hidden credit risk in borrowers with no public-market discipline. When stress hits and borrowers can't refinance, forced defaults will reveal that private-credit portfolios are far harder to exit than assumed — possibly right when pensions need the cash to pay retirees.

The surprising part

Zero rates transformed the guardians of retirement and safety into the market's most aggressive risk-takers. The money that was supposed to be the steady, boring anchor of the financial system went reaching into the least liquid, least transparent corner of it — not out of appetite, but out of necessity.

In one sentence

A decade of near-zero yields forced pensions and insurers to abandon safe bonds and pour nearly a trillion dollars into 8–12% private loans — betting illiquidity was worth the premium.
