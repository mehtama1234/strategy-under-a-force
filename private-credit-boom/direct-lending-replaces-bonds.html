Group A · Why the banks retreated

Direct lending

A single fund can now write a multi-billion-dollar loan in days — no banks, no ratings, no public disclosure.

Traditionally a company borrowing big either got a syndicated loan (arranged by banks and sold to many lenders) or issued bonds in public markets — both slow, rated, and disclosed. Now one private-credit fund, or a small club of them, can write a $1–5 billion loan directly and privately, in days. The mega private loan went from exotic to normal, and private lenders ate the middle of the corporate debt market.

The core bet

Private funds discovered they could hold the whole of a giant loan that banks were forced to chop up and sell — faster, quieter, and at a higher spread. By funding deals directly, they took share from both the syndicated-loan market and public bonds, and made themselves indispensable to sponsors who want speed and secrecy.

Speed and secrecy, for a price

Private credit won not by being smarter but by being faster, private, and certain. Banks had to syndicate because capital rules stopped them from holding a $5B loan; private funds had bottomless appetite and no such rules, so they'd take the whole loan, charge a point or two more, and sponsors happily paid — to skip three months of public roadshow and keep their numbers secret.

Who ends up ahead

The funds writing the whole loan and keeping the spread; sponsors who value speed and confidentiality.

Who's exposed

The syndicated market and public bondholders; and lenders who now hold concentrated, untradeable risk alone.

The single biggest worry

Concentration and illiquidity. These loans are held to maturity by secretive funds, so nobody sees where the risk is piled up until stress hits. And when a sponsor can't refinance, the single lender — with no syndicate to share the loss — absorbs it all alone, and can become the reluctant owner of a troubled company overnight.

The surprising part

The irony: banks created the private-credit industry themselves. It was regulation that forced banks to syndicate loans they couldn't hold — so when funds appeared that could hold them, and close in days without a public filing, borrowers switched. The lenders got deregulated; the borrowers got their secrecy.

In one sentence

Private credit ate the middle of the debt market by trading the syndicate's liquidity for speed and secrecy — and borrowers took the deal because certainty beat a three-month public roadshow.
