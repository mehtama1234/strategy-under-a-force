Group C · The risks & the dark corners

Opacity & marks

These loans almost never trade — so their value is a fund's own smooth opinion, not a market price.

A stock or a public bond gets a fresh market price every second. A private-credit loan almost never trades, so there's no market price at all — the fund itself estimates ("marks") the value, usually smoothly, and rarely marks it down even when the borrower is clearly struggling. Critics call it "volatility laundering": returns look calm and stable precisely because nobody is forced to price the loans honestly.

The core bet

With no market to price them, private loans are worth whatever the fund says — and funds have every incentive to say "still fine." The result is artificially smooth returns that hide deterioration until it's severe, at which point the mark-down comes all at once.

The whole model runs on never saying no

When an economy weakens and borrowers miss payments, the rules let funds amend loans — extend the maturity, waive covenants, cut the rate — rather than mark them down, quietly turning temporary trouble into permanent underperformance carried at par. Three years of smooth marks can hide three years of decay, so when reality finally forces the write-down, the drop is sharp enough to trigger a rush for the exits.

Who ends up ahead

The funds — which get to report stable, attractive returns that make raising the next fund easy.

Who's exposed

Investors relying on those marks; and everyone, when the delayed losses finally crystallize at once.

The single biggest worry

Smoothed marks create a giant hidden gap between what the industry says loans are worth and what they'd fetch if sold today. Some managers have admitted a fifth to a third of their book would need real haircuts to sell within a month — yet marks assume none. When funds are finally forced to face reality, everyone tries to sell into the same thin market at the same time.

The surprising part

Private credit's calm is an artifact of having no forced seller — no exchange, no daily close. A public fund holding a similar loan must mark it to the best price every day; a private fund marks it once a year and smooths away the noise. The bigger the stress, the wider the gap between the reported "mark" and what the loan could actually sell for.

In one sentence

Private-credit funds keep losses invisible by rarely marking down and amending troubled loans instead — so the risk looks calm until the day the mark-downs are forced and everyone sells at once.
