Group D · The machinery & the backlash

First-party data — the cookie died, so owning your audience won

This is the WHY behind the whole collection. A privacy change set off a land grab.

Here's why every company suddenly built an ad network. For years, advertisers followed you around the web with third-party "cookies" and phone tracking IDs. Then that broke — and the game inverted. Whoever owned first-party data (data from their own logged-in customers) won. And a privacy change meant to protect you accidentally set off a corporate land grab.

The break

  • Apple's App Tracking Transparency (April 2021) let iPhone users block tracking — costing Meta ~$10B in a year and gutting "follow you everywhere" targeting.
  • Google's third-party cookie saga: promised dead by 2022, delayed repeatedly, and in 2025 Google said it won't kill them after all — but the scare already reshaped the industry.
  • The winners: companies that know what you actually do — Amazon (what you buy), a bank (what you spend), a retailer (your loyalty history).

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the walled gardens win

Advertisers can no longer see or own their own customer data — Google, Meta, and Amazon hold it inside "walled gardens" and now capture ~74–83% of digital ad spend. If you advertise on Meta, you reach Meta's audience, not yours. Data fragmentation means worse targeting, higher costs, and total dependence on a few platforms.

The surprising part

Apple's privacy feature accidentally accelerated corporate data consolidation. Instead of weakening ad targeting, it handed dominance to whoever had the most of their own customer data: retailers, banks, and the ad giants. So every company raced to build its own walled garden and sell ads inside it — turning a privacy crisis into a land grab. This is the direct cause of the retail-media, bank-ad, and loyalty-ad explosions in this whole collection. (It's the same move as the loyalty economy.)

In one sentence

First-party data in a nutshell

When third-party tracking broke, first-party data became the moat — and the winners were the platforms, retailers, and banks that could build their own ad networks behind a customer login.
