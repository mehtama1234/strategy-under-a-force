Group E · The big picture

Everyone's an ad business — the whole force in one page

One idea ties every page together: ads are near-pure margin, so any company with an audience becomes an ad seller.

Step back from the twelve stories and one idea explains them all. Advertising is near-pure profit. Once you've built an audience — shoppers, riders, viewers, a screen, or data — showing them an ad costs almost nothing and drops straight to the bottom line. So every company with an audience is becoming an ad seller.

The one idea: the product assembles the audience

The whole force in a sentence

It started with pure media (Google, Meta), spread to retail (Amazon, Walmart, Instacart), and is now everywhere: Uber, your bank, your TV, your car, the gas pump, the airline seatback. The "real" product increasingly exists to assemble the audience the ads are sold against. More and more, the true product is you — sold to advertisers, not just on social media, but everywhere. And it's powered by the death of the tracking cookie, which made first-party data the moat.

Where the frame holds — and where it bends

The frame is fair — but with limits. Not every company has enough audience or data to run an ad network (it only works at scale). Ads can degrade the core product and drive customers away. The ad-tech middle skims 30–50%, so "pure margin" only holds if you own both supply and demand. And there's regulatory and ad-recession risk. It's a powerful force, not a free lunch.

The scale of the shift

~$160B+
Retail-media ad market (2025), the fastest-growing ad segment (~3× the growth of total ad spend). Connected-TV ad spend (~$38B) now tops linear TV in some measures.
~$700B
Global digital ad market — and ads are ~80% of both Google's and Meta's revenue. Even Chase's ad unit drove ~$12B in customer spend in its first year.

Who wins, who loses

Win

Anyone with a first-party audience + data (Amazon, Walmart, Uber, Chase, streamers); the big platforms; the ad-tech middle that skims a cut.

Lose

The open web and publishers; small advertisers priced out; and users — worse experience, more tracking, "enshittified" products.

The biggest tension

The open question

The whole thing depends on the audience staying captive and tolerant. If ads degrade the product too far — paywalls, creepier targeting, worse content — people churn and the audience moat evaporates. Add regulatory risk (privacy laws, antitrust) and ad-recession risk, and the "free lunch" margin can vanish. And crucially, this concentrates power: only giants can run profitable ad networks, so the wave centralizes rather than spreads the spoils.

The surprising part

The inversion is the whole story: the core product increasingly exists to assemble the audience, not to serve the customer. The user isn't the customer — the advertiser is. That was always true of social media; it's now spreading to retail, banking, cars, TVs, and travel. And it's the same engine as the attention economy and the loyalty economy: the data you collect (via a feed, a loyalty card, a bank account) becomes your ad-targeting asset. Three collections, one machine.

In one sentence

Everyone's an ad business in a nutshell

Ads are near-pure margin, so every company with an audience is becoming an ad seller — turning the product you buy into the bait that assembles you, the real product, for sale to advertisers everywhere.
