Group C · The surprising new ad sellers

Connected cars & TVs — the hardware is the loss-leader; you're the product

Your TV maker makes more money watching what you watch than from selling you the TV.

The devices you buy have been turned into ad-and-data businesses. Your smart TV makes more money from ads and tracking what you watch than from the hardware. The same logic is spreading to cars, which now collect and sell huge amounts of driver data. The thing you paid for keeps watching you — and selling what it sees.

The TV that watches back

  • Roku is really an ad company — ads and platform revenue are ~70% of its total, dwarfing the hardware.
  • Vizio sells TVs at near-zero margin and profits on ads + data. Its "automatic content recognition" (ACR) watches what's on your screen and sells that. Walmart bought Vizio in 2024 (~$2.3B) mainly for the ad business.
  • Cars: automakers were caught selling driving data (speed, braking, location) to insurers (~2023–24); GM and others have floated in-car ads.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — you can't turn it off

You bought a device you thought you owned — but it watches you 24/7 and sells that data with little meaningful consent, and often no practical way to use the device without being tracked. The surveillance is built into the core product.

The surprising part

The monetization can exceed the hardware's price within months. A $500 TV might earn its maker $50–200 a year in ads and data — so the box is a loss-leader for the tracking. You're not the customer; you're the product sold to advertisers and data brokers. This is now the standard model for connected hardware — Samsung, LG, Roku, Vizio, and increasingly carmakers all see the device as a way to watch you.

In one sentence

Connected cars & TVs in a nutshell

Hardware makers discovered they earn far more watching and selling what you do with their devices than from selling the devices — so the TV and the car became surveillance-and-ad machines you paid to bring home.
