Group B · Home & auto services

Auto repair — a power play against your insurer

The body shop that fixes your fender is quietly becoming a national chain — to fight the insurance companies.

Independent auto-repair shops, collision/body shops, and quick-lube places are being bought up and consolidated. Collision repair especially: a few PE-backed giants (Caliber Collision, Crash Champions, Gerber) rolled up thousands of body shops into national chains — partly to gain leverage against the insurance companies that decide where your crashed car gets fixed.

The consolidation

13% of shops, 32% of revenue
The "big 5" collision consolidators now run ~4,000 locations — a small share of shops but a third of industry revenue. Caliber (Hellman & Friedman-backed) has 1,800+ locations and filed to IPO in 2025.

Small shops sell for 2–4× earnings; PE platforms trade at 10–14× — the multiple jump alone creates value.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The chains are caught in a squeeze — insurers wield comparable power, labor and parts costs keep rising, and PE demands cash extraction, leaving little room for real margin improvement. The independent, family-owned shop quietly disappears into funds.

The surprising part

The consolidation was partly a power play against insurers, not just cost-cutting. Insurance companies steer crash repairs to preferred shops ("direct repair programs") and dictate rates. By rolling up thousands of shops into national chains, PE aimed to get big enough to negotiate back — to say no to lowball offers. "Buy local shops and optimize them" was also "become big enough to fight the insurers."

In one sentence

Auto repair in a nutshell

PE is buying the body shops and quick-lubes that fix your car and bundling them into national chains — partly to negotiate harder with the insurers who control where the work goes.
