Group C · The quiet cash cows

Mobile-home parks — the most captive tenants in America

You own the home but rent the land — and moving a "mobile" home is nearly impossible. So the rent has no ceiling.

This is the most controversial roll-up. PE firms and big investors bought up thousands of mobile-home parks. The trap: residents usually own their home but rent the land underneath — and moving a "mobile" home actually costs $5,000–15,000+ and is often physically impossible. So they're captive, and the lot rent can rise almost without limit — on some of the poorest, most vulnerable people in America.

The captive economics

+20–50%
Documented lot-rent increases after a buyout — sometimes doubling within a decade. Parks house ~20 million residents (~8–10% of US housing), disproportionately elderly and low-income (median ~$30–35k).

Retention runs 85%+ because leaving is nearly impossible; annual rent yields on acquired parks often top 20%. John Oliver and ProPublica put the issue in the spotlight (2017–2020).

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The residents are among America's poorest and most vulnerable — elderly, on fixed incomes — and once PE ownership locks in, there's effectively no cap on rent hikes. Governments rarely step in until people have already lost their homes.

The surprising part

The "mobility" in "mobile home" is a lie. A typical double-wide costs tens of thousands to move if it can be moved at all — so rent-burdened residents literally cannot escape. That makes parks a PE gold mine precisely because the captivity is more extreme than any other real estate: even ordinary rentals let tenants leave. This roll-up extracts from the people least able to resist.

In one sentence

Mobile-home parks in a nutshell

PE bought up America's mobile-home parks because residents who own the home but not the land are the most captive tenants in the country — making rent extraction profitable on those least able to escape.
