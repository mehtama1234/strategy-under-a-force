Group C · The quiet cash cows

Self-storage — cheap to enter; a cage to leave

The teaser rate is the trap door. Once your stuff is inside, the rent only goes up.

Self-storage is a beloved investment because it's a near-perfect cash machine: cheap land, almost no staff, and tenants who won't move their stuff. Big REITs (Public Storage, Extra Space, CubeSmart) and PE rolled up thousands of independent facilities. The profit isn't from new customers — it's from raising rent on the ones already locked in.

The lock-in machine

"ECRIs" — existing customer rate increases

They lure you with a cheap teaser rate, then raise your rent 15–40% every few months once your stuff is inside and moving it is a weekend nightmare. One tenant's unit went from $45 → $75 → $220. Occupancy stays high; churn stays low; margins fatten.

~$5B / +40%
Self-storage acquisition volume in 2025, up ~40% year-over-year. Extra Space + Life Storage merged (2024) into a $12.7B, 3,600-facility portfolio.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The whole model depends on tenant inertia holding. If people finally get fed up and clear out their units, the machine stalls — but operators bet, correctly, that human laziness beats economics.

The surprising part

Self-storage sells you a cage, not a service. It profits mainly from raising rents on locked-in tenants who won't bother moving their stuff — amplified by billing tricks (switching from 30-day to 28-day cycles to squeeze 13 months of rent a year) and mailed notices you'll ignore. The business works because human inertia is stronger than economics — which is exactly why financial buyers love it.

In one sentence

Self-storage in a nutshell

Self-storage is a roll-up that extracts wealth not from service but from tenant captivity — cheap entry, relentless rate hikes, and the hassle of moving your stuff out.
