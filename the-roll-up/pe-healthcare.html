Group D · The mechanism & the cost

PE in healthcare — cost-cutting measured in deaths

When you roll up hospitals and nursing homes, the cost-cutting that makes the deal profitable shows up in the mortality data.

Private equity rolled up hospitals, ERs, and nursing homes — loaded them with debt, cut staff, extracted cash (including by selling the hospitals' own real estate and charging rent back), and the care deteriorated. This is where "the roll-up" turns from annoying to deadly, and the harm is measured in peer-reviewed studies.

The evidence

+10% / +13%
Higher patient mortality after PE buyouts — nursing homes (a study linked PE ownership to ~20,000 excess Medicare deaths, 2005–2017) and hospital ERs (a 2025 study found +13% ED mortality, ~7 more deaths per 10,000 visits). PE hospitals also had ~25% more hospital-acquired infections.
  • Steward Health Care (Cerberus-backed) collapsed into bankruptcy (2024) with ~$9B liabilities; Cerberus made ~$800M before exiting; hospitals closed, leaving communities without care.
  • The trick: sell the hospital's land to a REIT, take a dividend, then pay rent on the building you used to own — extracting cash while the hospital carries the risk.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Once a hospital's real estate is sold off, it depends on the landlord keeping rent affordable. One financial shock can force closures that leave a whole region with no emergency care — and no way back, because the buildings are owned by outside capital.

The surprising part

There's no hidden mechanism — it's a direct trade-off. The cost-cutting that makes the deal profitable (smaller staff, fewer beds, shorter stays) is what the mortality studies measure. PE investors extract real cash (~$800M on a chain that then collapsed) while the institution carries the debt and the risk — and the deaths show up in the data.

In one sentence

PE in healthcare in a nutshell

PE rolled up hospitals and nursing homes, loaded them with debt, cut staff and sold the real estate, and the result — in peer-reviewed studies — was measurably more deaths, more infections, and at least one chain (Steward) that collapsed entirely.
