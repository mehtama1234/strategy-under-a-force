The patterns

Line up everyone the roll-up touched, and one theme dominates: buy the boring essential business, keep the local name, raise the prices on people who can't leave.

Each page asks: what did the roll-up make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this business? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions repeat.

The one thing to take away

PE targets the boring, essential, captive services — vets, dentists, HVAC, funerals, storage, trailer parks — because the customer can't say no. It bundles them, keeps the local name, raises prices, and profits mostly from financial engineering, not better service. The public funds it via pensions and pays for it as customers.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Target the captive and un-shoppable

You won't shop around when your pet is dying, your AC fails, or someone just passed away. PE targets inelastic demand — where desperation or grief kills price sensitivity.

Pattern 02

Keep the local name so nobody notices

The friendly local vet, dentist, and funeral home keep their old names on purpose. You only discover the fund in New York owns them when the bill arrives.

Pattern 03

The profit is financial, not operational

Multiple arbitrage (buy at 4–6×, sell the bundle at 10–15×) plus leverage creates most of the return — value from being bigger, not from running things better.

Pattern 04

The incentive flips from need to revenue

Once a fund sets a quota, "treat what's needed" becomes "hit the number" — documented over-treatment in dental and dermatology, more procedures billed after a buyout.

Pattern 05

Raise the rent on the ones who can't leave

Self-storage hikes rent on stuff you won't move; trailer parks on homes you can't move. The profit is inertia and captivity, not new customers.

Pattern 06

Debt is the accelerant — and the fuse

Leverage amplifies returns, but a stall turns it lethal: over-loaded chains collapse (Steward, Envision, Red Lobster, Toys R Us), taking jobs and services with them.

Pattern 07

Cost-cutting can be measured in harm

The staffing cuts that make the deal profitable show up in the data — more deaths and infections at PE-owned nursing homes and ERs. No hidden mechanism; a direct trade-off.

Fourteen forces, one method

The point of the series

This is the fourteenth "strategy under a force" collection. Its signature: "buy the boring, raise the prices, financialize." It's fuelled by the same private-credit boom as "everyone's a bank," and it applies the toll-booth logic offline — control an essential service the customer can't route around. Same method every time: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group by the answer, read the patterns.
