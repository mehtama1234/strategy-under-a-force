Dental chains — "treat what's needed" became "hit the number"

When a dentist office answers to a fund with a production quota, the incentive quietly flips.

Private equity has bought up dentist offices and bundled them into big "Dental Service Organizations" (DSOs) — Aspen Dental, Heartland Dental, and others — keeping local branding. To hit PE return targets, the chains centralize the money side and set revenue quotas. The dentist provides the care; the fund sets the number.

The consolidation

~33%
Share of US dental practices under DSO ownership (2023–25), up from ~10% in 2010 — backed by KKR, Apollo, Carlyle. Prices tend to rise, not fall, after acquisition.
  • Kool Smiles scandal: a pediatric DSO chain was found billing Medicaid for unnecessary procedures on healthy children's baby teeth.
  • New dentists report pressure to hit daily production targets ($15–25k/day); financing (CareCredit, 18–30% APR) is pushed for upsold treatment.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Dentistry is a licensed profession where the dentist's duty is to the patient — but the owner's duty is now to a fund chasing 20–30% returns. That conflict creates pressure to over-treat, especially on people who can't second-guess the dentist (children, the elderly, Medicaid patients).

The surprising part

The local-branding shell game is the tell: you think you're seeing "Dr. Smith's Dental," but the treatment protocols, quotas, and financing offers come from a PE firm 2,000 miles away. The dentist provides clinical judgment; the fund sets the production quota — and over time, the quota wins. Patients rarely see the ownership structure; they just see the friendly local name.

In one sentence

Dental chains in a nutshell

Private equity bought a third of US dental practices and swapped the incentive from "treat what's needed" to "hit the revenue number" — with documented over-treatment — while patients rarely know their dentist answers to a fund.
