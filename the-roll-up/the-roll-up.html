Group E · The big picture

The roll-up — the whole force in one page

One idea ties every page together: buy the boring, raise the prices, financialize — and keep the local name.

Step back from the twelve stories and one idea explains them all. Private equity takes a fragmented industry of small owner-operators, buys them one by one, bundles them into a big chain (keeping the local names so you don't notice), standardizes, raises prices on a captive customer base, loads the chain with debt, and sells it on. Wall Street is quietly buying Main Street.

The one idea: financialize the boring essentials

The whole force in a sentence

The profit comes mostly from financial engineering (multiple arbitrage + leverage) and pricing power — not from running the businesses better — over customers who don't realize the friendly local vet, dentist, or funeral home now answers to a fund.

Where the frame holds — and where it bends

It's a fair diagnosis, not a total condemnation. Some roll-ups genuinely help small businesses with scale, tech, and capital; not all raise prices abusively; consolidation can be efficient; and owners who sell get a fair exit. The frame bends into harm when a chain is over-leveraged and squeezes captive customers — which the healthcare outcomes show is far from rare.

The scale

$3T+ / ~50%
US private-equity assets (2024), and the share of US vet practices now corporate-owned (from ~8% in 2011). Much of it is funded by ~$2.5T of private credit.

Who wins, who loses

Win

PE funds and their investors — including pension funds (teachers, firefighters).

Lose

Customers (higher prices), workers (cut hours), communities (lost services), and patients when a chain is over-leveraged.

The biggest tension

The open question

The businesses PE loves are the essential, captive, un-shoppable ones — the exact services communities can't afford to lose. Once consolidated, they're largely immune to price competition. Regulators are stirring (FTC roll-up inquiries, the UK threatening to break up vet consolidation), but enforcement targets big deals — most small-business roll-ups fly under the radar below $500M.

The surprising part

The system is self-perpetuating and funded by public money. Pension funds are the largest investors in PE roll-ups — so a teacher pays higher vet bills as a customer and gets the returns as a pension holder. And PE deliberately avoids competitive markets (restaurants, retail) where price hikes kill demand; it targets inelastic demand — you can't switch vets mid-treatment, or shop funeral homes mid-funeral. Captivity is the whole point.

In one sentence

The roll-up in a nutshell

Private equity buys fragmented essential services, consolidates them into captive-customer chains, extracts value through leverage and pricing power, and funds it with pension money — so communities pay the price while public workers' retirements profit.
