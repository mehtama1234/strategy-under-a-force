Group A · Selling belonging & connection

Experiences over things

When stuff feels hollow, a night with people you care about becomes the thing worth paying for.

A cultural shift, sharpened by loneliness and the post-COVID hunger for togetherness, moved spending toward experiences that connect people rather than goods: live music, festivals, group travel, dining out. The shared moment and the memory are the product — you're not paying for a ticket, you're paying for time with other people.

The core bet

People increasingly spend on experiences that create connection and belonging rather than objects — so the concert, the trip, and the shared event became premium products.

People cut back on things but not on togetherness

Through recent inflation, spending on furniture and appliances fell while concert tickets and group trips stayed resilient or grew. It isn't only preference — it's hunger: connection got scarce during lockdowns, so people will stretch a tight budget to get it back. Loneliness became expensive to fix, and the shared experience is the fix people chose.

The single biggest worry

Experience spending is discretionary and gets cut first in a downturn — and it can tip into status performance more than real connection, where the point becomes proof you were there rather than the time with the people you were with.

The surprising part

The counter-intuitive signal is the resilience. In an anxious economy, the rational move is to trim the fun — yet the fun that involves other people is exactly what people protected. That's the loneliness economy showing up in the spending data: the shared experience is valued because connection got rare.

In one sentence

When stuff feels hollow, a night with people you care about feels like the only thing worth paying for.
