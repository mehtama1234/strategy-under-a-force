Group C · The synthetic cure

Parasocial & fandom

For millions, a streamer they've never met is now their closest daily companion.

Lonely people increasingly get their sense of relationship from one-sided (parasocial) bonds with streamers, YouTubers, and podcast hosts, and from fandoms that feel like communities. A favorite creator feels like a friend; a fandom feels like a home. This loneliness-driven demand is the human engine beneath the creator and fan economies.

The core bet

Loneliness creates demand for the feeling of friendship, and creators, streamers, and fandoms supply a one-sided but emotionally real substitute — monetized through subs, donations, and belonging.

Fandom replaced what the town square used to give

For millions — especially isolated young adults — a streamer they've never met is now the person they feel 'understands them best,' and a fandom provides the belonging and shared values that church, the neighborhood, or the town square used to supply. The connection is real to the person feeling it, even though it flows only one way.

The single biggest worry

Parasocial bonds are one-sided and can be exploited: a creator can vanish, be exposed, or die, leaving sudden abandonment — and the hours poured into them crowd out the harder, reciprocal work of real friendship.

The surprising part

The poignant part is that the substitute is emotionally genuine even though the relationship isn't mutual. People grieve creators, defend them like family, and organize their weeks around them — a real feeling of friendship, supplied by an algorithm and a screen, standing in for the two-way bonds that got scarce.

In one sentence

Lonely people are trading effortful, reciprocal friendships for easy one-sided bonds with creators and fandoms — and paying billions for the feeling.
