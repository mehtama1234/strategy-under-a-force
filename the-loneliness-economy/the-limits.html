Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

The limits

You can't buy your way out of a disease the seller has a reason to keep you sick with.

This is the skeptical piece. The deepest tension is that connection may not be a thing you can buy: paid friendship, subscription communities, therapy apps, and AI companions can treat symptoms while leaving the real isolation intact — or worsen it. And the cures exclude the people most likely to be lonely, because the poorest can't pay, while the fixes that actually work aren't very profitable.

The core bet

The loneliness economy assumes connection can be packaged and sold, but real belonging may resist commodification — so much of the market treats the symptom, not the disease.

The real cures aren't profitable, so they're under-supplied

The interventions that actually work — free community centers, faith and civic institutions, local clubs, walkable public space — generate almost no revenue and are being defunded, while the most profitable 'cures' (AI companions, algorithmic feeds) correlate with more loneliness. The market supplies what pays, not what heals, which creates a scarcity not of connection but of free connection.

The single biggest worry

A market that profits from loneliness has little incentive to end it, and some of its best-selling products deepen it — so the spending grows while the underlying isolation doesn't shrink.

The surprising part

The blunt conclusion is that the thing being sold may not be the thing people need. Real belonging takes reciprocity, vulnerability, and stakes — hard to deliver through a screen with a payment attached. Most of the market sells relief from loneliness, not a cure for it, and the relief is priced beyond the reach of the people who need it most.

In one sentence

You cannot buy your way out of a disease the seller has a financial incentive to keep you sick with.
