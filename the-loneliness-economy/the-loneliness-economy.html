Group E · The big picture

The loneliness economy

Connection got scarce enough to become a premium product — sold by the machines that made it scarce.

Pull the whole collection together and one line runs through it. Loneliness became a public-health crisis and, immediately, a market: belonging turned scarce and valuable, so we built apps, services, and experiences to sell it back. The trap is that the same machines that isolated us — phones, feeds, dating apps, AI companions — now profit from selling the cure, with little incentive to actually end the loneliness.

The core bet

Loneliness became a public-health crisis and a market; belonging turned into a scarce, valuable thing you pay for, and the sellers of the cure often helped cause the disease.

Winners and losers

The economy sorts cleanly into who sells connection (or its substitute) and who is left still lonely.

Who ends up ahead

Social platforms and dating/AI-companion apps, mental-health and wellness firms, experience and events businesses, boutique fitness and social clubs, elder-companion services, and insurers funding connection to cut costs.

Who's exposed

The lonely who can't buy their way out, those sold substitutes that deepen isolation, and society itself when the market treats symptoms instead of causes.

The single biggest worry

Some of the biggest sellers of the cure — social media, dating apps, AI companions — profit from the isolation they help produce, so they have little incentive to truly end it. A fully connected person needs the product less, not more.

The surprising part

The note to end on is the shape of the whole thing. We didn't just lose connection — we learned to buy it back at a markup from the people who helped take it away. The same phone that isolates you sells you the app that promises to fix it; the free versions of belonging (the town square, the club, the congregation) went extinct because they couldn't be monetized, so what's left is a paid, partial substitute. Connection became a luxury good precisely because it got scarce — and the market that formed around that scarcity has a quiet stake in keeping it that way.

In one sentence

Loneliness became so common and so profitable that the same technology which isolated us now sells the connection back — at a markup.
