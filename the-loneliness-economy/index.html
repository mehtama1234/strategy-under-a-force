The Loneliness Economy

We got lonelier, so connection became something you buy — and the machines that isolated us now sell the cure.

The 45th force. Loneliness became a stated public-health crisis: the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory called it an epidemic, with isolation carrying a mortality risk likened to smoking about 15 cigarettes a day. That turned belonging into a scarce, valuable thing — and a market. People now pay for community, friendship-as-a-service, third places, therapy, live experiences, and increasingly for synthetic companions that are always available and never leave. The uncomfortable engine underneath: the biggest sellers of the cure — social platforms, dating apps, AI-companion makers — often profit from the very isolation they help create. The one idea: when connection got scarce, it became a premium product — sold, too often, by the machines that made it scarce.

A fresh force (Tier-6) Not from the Inc. 5000 taxonomy — surfaced by a Haiku discovery-scout pass for genuinely new forces. Anchored to the 2023 U.S. Surgeon General loneliness advisory and the ~half of U.S. adults who report loneliness.
The one question, asked of everyone

What did the loneliness epidemic make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? Real belonging became scarce and premium; content and synthetic company became cheap and infinite; and connection turned into a subscription. The danger: a market built on unmet connection has an incentive to keep it unmet (dating apps profit from single users), some "cures" deepen the disease (AI companions, endless feeds), and the fixes that actually work — free community — aren't very profitable, so they're under-supplied.
