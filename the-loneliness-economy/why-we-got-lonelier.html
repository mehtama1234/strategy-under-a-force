Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why we got lonelier

We demolished the places that forced us to see each other — then built phones that let us avoid it.

This is the mechanism. Loneliness didn't appear from nowhere — it's the product of tearing apart the everyday, in-person institutions where people used to connect (churches, clubs, unions, bowling leagues), replaced by a life of convenience: suburbs you drive through alone, phones you socialize through alone, and work-from-home where you sit alone. The shift was fast, and the loneliness showed up in the data after.

The core bet

Loneliness isn't random — it's the product of decades of eroding the everyday, in-person institutions where people used to connect, accelerated by phones and remote life.

We engineered loneliness through convenience

The same tools sold to connect us — Facebook, Instagram, the smartphone — became among the biggest causes of the disconnection. We chose frictionless convenience (one-tap everything, work-from-home, a 'like' instead of a call) and accidentally removed the small, in-person frictions that used to force connection. Tech didn't start the decline, but it poured gasoline on it.

The single biggest worry

The causes are baked into how we built the world — car-dependent sprawl, economics that reward solo living, technology that swaps in-person for convenient-but-hollow digital contact — so reversing it is slow and structural, which is exactly why the market rushes in to sell the cure instead.

The surprising part

The strange irony is that we solved the problem of reaching people so completely that we made connecting with them harder than ever. Frictionless contact — a text, a like, a feed — replaced the effortful kind, and it turns out the friction was the point: the awkward in-person moment was where the bond actually formed.

In one sentence

We demolished the institutions that made us see each other, built suburbs and phones that let us avoid it, and are now shocked that people raised in isolation are lonely.
