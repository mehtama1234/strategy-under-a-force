Group B · The health frontier

The mental-health boom

Distress got destigmatized and scalable — so therapy apps, teletherapy, and EAPs became a $50B+ market.

Rising loneliness, anxiety, and depression — especially post-COVID and among youth — made mental distress mainstream instead of taboo, and that let on-demand mental-health services scale fast: therapy apps (BetterHelp, Talkspace), meditation apps (Calm, Headspace), and employer benefits (Lyra, Spring Health). Demand is enormous; quality and outcomes are debated.

The core bet

Mental distress became mainstream and destigmatized, so scalable, on-demand mental-health services — apps, teletherapy, employer benefits — became a big market.

Employers buy it to protect profits, not out of kindness

Companies fund mental-health benefits because burnout, absence, and turnover cost them money — the 'wellness' boom is partly firms buying peace and lower claims. And meditation became a commercial empire not because sitting quietly is new, but because investors bet people would pay a monthly fee to be told when to breathe.

The single biggest worry

App-based therapy can be low-quality or a privacy trap — BetterHelp paid a ~$7.8M FTC fine in 2023 for sharing users' therapy-intake data with advertisers — and it treats the individual while the causes (isolation, economic stress) stay social.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable insight is that the biggest market for the cure is downstream of the disease this whole collection is about: much of the anxiety and depression being treated is loneliness by another name. The apps treat the person one session at a time, while the thing making people unwell — disconnection — is a problem no app can bill for solving.

In one sentence

A mental-health crisis created a $50B+ market for apps, teletherapy, and employer benefits — but they treat the person, not the loneliness, and employers mainly buy them to protect the bottom line.
