Group C · The synthetic cure

Dating apps

The business would break if it actually worked — so it profits while you stay single and searching.

Dating apps (Tinder, Hinge, Bumble — mostly Match Group) sit at the center of the loneliness economy with a built-in conflict: they make money while you're searching, so a happy couple who leaves is two lost customers. Growth is now stalling as users report 'dating-app fatigue,' and the model is under scrutiny.

The core bet

Dating apps sell hope and access to the search for a partner — and the perverse incentive is that they earn most from users who stay single and keep paying.

The incentive is to keep you searching, not to help you leave

If Tinder matched people perfectly and they all quit to date, revenue would vanish overnight — so the hidden incentive is to keep users hoping but not finding. Hinge's slogan is 'designed to be deleted'; the 2024 lawsuit argues that's false advertising, because the real design keeps you in the loop, paying.

The single biggest worry

The model profits from unsuccessful searching, and users are catching on — burning out, blaming the app, and leaving; Match cut 13% of staff in 2024 as Gen Z declined, and if users go, the revenue goes with them.

The surprising part

The self-defeating twist is that the product works against its own stated purpose. A business that earns from single, searching users cannot want them to succeed — and after a decade, the market's own customers figured that out and started going back to meeting in person, which is why the apps are now shrinking.

In one sentence

Dating apps profit while you're lonely and searching, not when you find someone and leave — which is why they're losing Gen Z to in-person meetups.
