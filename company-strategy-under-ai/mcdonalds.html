Group F · Old-school companies that are really tech now

McDonald's — a data company that sells burgers

The app knows you better than any cashier ever could. That's the whole point.

This is the group you suggested — companies that look old-fashioned but really run on apps, data, and AI. McDonald's is the perfect example. It has quietly become a data-and-technology business that happens to sell burgers.

The data engine hiding in the app

For 70 years, McDonald's was nearly blind to its own customers: you paid cash, walked out, and left no trace. The loyalty app changed that overnight. Every scan tells McDonald's who you are, what you order, when, and which deal pulls you back.

~175M+
Loyalty app members (heading toward a target of 250 million by 2027). It turned an anonymous cash business into a personal-data machine.
~$30B
Sales from loyalty members in a single year — and cheap, targeted deals to you replaced expensive mass advertising to everyone.

AI moving into the restaurant

  • Drive-thru voice ordering — an early test with IBM (2021) struggled with accents and noise and got shut off in 2024; McDonald's still says voice is the future and is picking a new partner.
  • A big Google Cloud deal (2023) to put AI and "edge computing" into ~43,000 restaurants — so each store can run AI locally, even on weak internet.
  • "Ask Pickles" — an AI assistant for crew that answers questions from 180,000 pages of manuals, in 32 languages.
  • Predictive maintenance — sensors that warn before the fryer (or the famously broken ice-cream machine) fails.
  • Cameras that check your order is assembled right before handoff.

Why it matters

Each restaurant becomes a mini data center — decisions happen instantly, in a business measured in seconds. The same digital menus and app let McDonald's personalize deals and prices — a discount to fill a slow afternoon, a nudge to add a drink — small percentage gains multiplied across tens of billions in sales.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Execution. Its flashiest AI project — the drive-thru voice bot — flopped publicly and got switched off. Franchisees grumble about paying for tech that doesn't clearly boost sales. And there's a privacy/pricing landmine: when a rival floated "dynamic pricing," customers heard "surge pricing" and revolted — a warning for any chain whose menu prices can move.

The surprising part

A company famous for cheap burgers is really one of the world's largest consumer-data operations. For decades the person at the counter forgot you the second you left. Today the app remembers everything — and increasingly an AI in the building, not a human, decides what to offer you and whether your order is right.

In one sentence

McDonald's in a nutshell

The burger chain became a data-and-AI company: an app that knows its customers, and AI wiring up 40,000 restaurants. The catch is pulling it off without fumbling the tech or spooking customers on price.
