Microsoft — the "sell it to businesses" bet

It went the opposite way from Amazon: partner and sell, don't build the guts.

Where Amazon builds everything itself, Microsoft made the reverse bet. Instead of building the underlying technology, it partnered for the AI and focused on what it's best at: selling to businesses.

The two moves

Microsoft's AI plan rests on two things:

  • The OpenAI partnership. Microsoft backed OpenAI (the ChatGPT company) and gets to use its AI. So it didn't have to build a top AI model from scratch.
  • Copilot. Microsoft bolts an AI assistant onto the software companies already pay for every day — Word, Excel, Outlook, Windows — and charges extra for it. Selling to businesses is Microsoft's home turf.

Its strength and its weak spot

What it's good at

Nobody sells software to big companies better than Microsoft. It already has the customers, the sales teams, and the everyday apps — so it can put AI in front of millions of paying businesses fast.

Where it's exposed

It rents its AI chips from Nvidia (expensive), and its own chip effort is behind. And it leans heavily on one partner, OpenAI — a relationship that has gotten complicated.

Its two real worries

The worry — profit and a partner

Profit: renting expensive Nvidia chips to run all this AI eats into how much money Microsoft actually keeps. Partner: so much of its AI edge runs through OpenAI that if that relationship sours, or OpenAI becomes a rival, Microsoft is more exposed than it would like.

The one-line summary

Microsoft in a sentence

It bet on selling AI to businesses through partners, rather than building the guts itself. Great at selling; weaker on owning the technology underneath. Its worries are keeping profits healthy and not depending too much on one partner.

Compare with Amazon, which made the exact opposite bet — build everything, depend on no one, but spend a fortune doing it.
