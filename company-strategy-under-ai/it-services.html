Group E · When AI replaces the workers

The IT & consulting firms — selling the very thing AI replaces

Accenture, TCS, Infosys, Cognizant, Wipro — giants whose product is people's hours, in the crosshairs of software that does those hours.

These firms are huge (each employs hundreds of thousands) and most people don't think about them. Their business is simple: rent out armies of engineers by the hour to build and run other companies' software. AI writes code and handles support — so this is the industry with the most direct target on its back.

The model AI threatens

For decades the math was: more people = more revenue. These firms hire thousands of cheap junior engineers (the wide base of a "pyramid"), bill them out, and grow by adding more bodies. If AI does the junior work — the coding, the testing, the support tickets — clients need fewer billed hours, and that whole pyramid wobbles.

The bind: they both lose and win

They lose

AI replaces the very labor they sell by the hour. Fewer hours billed = less revenue under the old model.

They win

Every company now needs help adopting AI — and pays these firms to do it. That's a big new line of work.

Which force is bigger is the open question hanging over the whole sector.

What the numbers already show

  • Headcount is quietly decoupling from revenue. The three big Indian firms (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) shed roughly 64,000 jobs in one year; Wipro's staff kept shrinking even as sales held steady. Growth no longer requires piling on people.
  • Accenture took an ~$865 million restructuring charge and said out loud it is "exiting… people where reskilling… is not a viable path" — while its AI-related bookings nearly doubled to ~$5.9 billion.
  • Cognizant says the old career ladder is becoming a "broader and shorter" pyramid, and now tracks revenue per employee rather than raw headcount.
  • Everyone is retraining frantically — Accenture has trained 550,000+ staff on AI; Cognizant is retraining a million-plus.

The tell: revenue growing while headcount flattens or shrinks. That's the linear "people = money" link breaking in real time.

The spin vs. the data

Read the executives carefully

The bosses insist AI is "expanding the market, not shrinking it" and that there will be "more jobs." Maybe. But the behavior tells a quieter story than the words: restructuring charges, flat or falling headcount, fewer junior hires per dollar of revenue, and a scramble to reskill. Watch what they do (hiring, headcount) more than what they say.

Two deeper cracks

1. Clients are clawing back the savings. Companies are reopening old contracts to add "if AI makes it cheaper, we pay less" clauses. One buyer said 10–15 people now do what used to take 40–50 offshore developers and testers. If the client keeps the savings, the firm does the same work for a smaller cheque — analysts already talk of a "$100M deal now closing at ~$80M."

2. AI eats the bottom of the pyramid — which is the training ground. The junior work AI takes over first (simple coding, testing, support tickets) is exactly where fresh graduates used to learn the craft. Entry-level hiring across the big Indian firms has fallen dramatically — by some counts around 80% from its peak. Automate away the apprenticeship and you save money today, but you starve the pipeline of tomorrow's senior engineers. (One analyst calls it the shift "from a pyramid to a diamond.")

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The decades-old "bodies for hire" model breaks — and the new AI-adoption work doesn't fully replace it. If clients need far fewer contractor-hours, or start doing AI projects themselves, revenue and profits shrink even as the firms look busy. The junior-heavy pyramid that trained the whole industry may not survive.

The surprising part

These firms are in the strangest spot in the whole collection: they sell the exact thing AI replaces (human labor by the hour), yet they also get paid to install the AI that replaces it. They're helping their own clients need fewer of them — and booking record deals doing it.

In one sentence

The sector in a nutshell

They rent out people's hours, and AI does the hours — so they're racing to sell AI-adoption help instead, retraining hundreds of thousands, and quietly growing revenue without growing headcount. Whether the new work replaces the old is the multi-hundred-billion-dollar question.
