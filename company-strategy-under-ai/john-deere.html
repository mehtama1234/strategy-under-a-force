John Deere — a robotics company on wheels

A 190-year-old tractor maker is quietly one of the biggest real-world AI companies on Earth.

Nothing looks less like a tech company than a green tractor. Yet John Deere is turning its machines into self-driving robots and selling farmers software and data on top — betting the money is increasingly in the brains, not just the steel.

The robots

Two things make Deere a genuine AI company, not a marketing one:

  • Self-driving tractors. Cameras give the machine a 360° view; a neural network reads every pixel in a split second to decide "keep going or stop." A farmer can start it and walk away. Deere has shown production-ready versions at CES.
  • "See & Spray" — cameras that spot weeds. Instead of blasting a whole field with weedkiller, cameras on the sprayer tell crop from weed and fire only on the weeds.

Average herbicide savings from See & Spray — about 8 million gallons saved in a single year. Better for the farmer's wallet and the environment.

The real shift: get paid monthly, not once

From selling steel to selling software

A tractor sells once every several years. Software sells every month. So Deere is pushing "farming as a service" — recurring subscriptions for its data platform (which already connects a million-plus machines across hundreds of millions of acres), aiming for about 10% of its revenue from software by 2030. Steady, higher-margin income that keeps flowing between tractor purchases — and makes farmers ever more dependent on Deere.

Why AI made this valuable now

Two things: farm data became worth an ongoing relationship rather than a one-time sale, and automation became a real answer to a chronic shortage of skilled farm labor. A boring, cyclical machinery company is trying to become a steady, higher-value tech company.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — who owns the tractor?

Farmers fought Deere for years over being locked out of repairing their own machines (Deere controlled the diagnostics and software). It got ugly: the government and several states sued in 2025, and in 2026 Deere settled a 10-year deal forcing it to give farmers and independent shops the same repair tools as its dealers. It's a warning shot at the whole "we own the software in your machine" model. On top of that, farm equipment sales crash in downturns — the very reason Deere wants steady subscription income.

The surprising part

A company from the 1830s deploys self-driving vehicles at a scale most robotaxi startups would envy — on farms, where there are no pedestrians to hit and a labor shortage to solve. And the punchline: this maker of heavy green steel wants to get paid monthly, like Netflix, for the software inside it.

In one sentence

John Deere in a nutshell

It turned tractors into AI robots and is shifting to selling farmers software and data by subscription. The fight is over who owns the machine and its data — and the government just made Deere open it up.
