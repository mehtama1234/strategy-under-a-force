The patterns

Look at 24 companies at once and the same handful of tensions show up again and again.

Each company page asks one question: what did AI make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this business? Line up all 24 answers and clear patterns appear. These are the ones that repeat — with the companies that show each. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

AI rarely changed what these companies sell. It changed what's scarce, who depends on whom, and where the profit sits — usually one layer underneath the product. The winners saw that shift early; the losers got hit in a place they weren't defending.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Your biggest customer is also your biggest threat

Over and over, the company paying you the most is the one working hardest to not need you. The giants that buy the most chips are building their own; the partners are becoming rivals.

Pattern 02

AI made physical things scarce again — chips, power, buildings

For years the scarce thing in tech was clever software. AI flipped it back to atoms: advanced chips, the factories and machines that make them, electricity, and data centers. Owning a physical bottleneck suddenly beat owning code.

Pattern 03

Giving AI away — or helping rivals — is a weapon, not charity

When a company hands out something expensive for free, look for who it hurts. Free models wreck rivals' ability to charge; "build your own chip" kits exist to break the market leader's grip.

MetaBroadcomAmazonGoogle
Pattern 04

The incumbent's trap: AI eats the business that pays the bills

The most dangerous spot is owning a fat, comfortable business that AI attacks directly. Some got killed, some are disrupting themselves on purpose, some are still bracing.

CheggGoogleAdobeSalesforceRedditIT firms
Pattern 05

Watch what they do, not what they say

The loudest AI stories often outrun the numbers. Someone brags about replacing staff, then rehires; a headline "AI revenue" turns out to be 2% of sales; a scary "$39B loss" is mostly accounting. Believe the behavior over the press release.

Pattern 06

You can only sell — or trust — what you can check

AI claims are only worth what your test is worth. Where results can be objectively verified (a chip design that passes, a fact you can source), value and pricing follow. Where they can't, you get hype and mistrust. (This is the whole point of the sister research site.)

Pattern 07

Everyone is quietly funding everyone

The chipmaker invests in the AI lab, which buys the chipmaker's chips. The same money loops around and can make demand look bigger and healthier than it is — and it ties everyone's fate together, so if one link wobbles, they all do.

Pattern 08

Distribution usually beats being first

Owning the customer — the phone, the feed, the app, the store — is a slower-burning but sturdier advantage than having the best model. The giants can afford to be late; the brilliant attackers still have to reach people.

Pattern 09

Old business on the outside, new data-and-software engine inside

Some of the most interesting cases look ancient — burgers, groceries, pizza, tractors — but really run on apps, data, and AI, with the profit quietly moving to ads, subscriptions, and automation.

Pattern 10

AI made humans both newly precious and newly replaceable

The same wave cuts two ways. Real human writing became a scarce asset worth licensing — while routine human labor became the first thing companies tried to automate away (sometimes too eagerly).

The question underneath all of it

If you remember one thing

Don't ask "does this company use AI?" — everyone does. Ask "what did AI make scarce, cheap, defensible, or risky for them — and are they defending the right thing?" The companies that win aren't the ones with the best AI. They're the ones who correctly spotted which part of their world just changed.
