Group B · The ones AI is eating

Adobe — trying to disrupt itself first

The smarter cousin of the Chegg story: an incumbent racing to build the AI that could kill it.

Adobe makes the software professionals use to create images, videos, and documents — Photoshop and the rest — and charges a monthly fee. "Type a sentence, get a finished image" AI should be a nightmare for that. So Adobe did the opposite of freezing: it built its own generative AI (called Firefly) into every tool, betting it can profit from the disruption instead of being killed by it.

The double-edged sword

The danger

AI makes creating an image so easy that anyone can do it for free — and Adobe's expensive tools become unnecessary.

The opportunity

Adobe bundles AI in and charges extra for it — new money on top of the subscription.

So far, the opportunity side is winning — on paper. But almost nobody believes it will last.

The strange part: great numbers, falling stock

$23.8B
Record sales, up 11% in a year. AI-linked products now drive over a third of the business — more than $5 billion.
~40% ↓
Yet the stock fell about 40% over the same stretch. The business says "charging more is working"; the stock says "free AI wins in the end."
The tension in one line

Adobe is being punished by its stock for a problem that hasn't shown up in its results. The company keeps posting records, but investors are pricing in "AI eventually makes Adobe irrelevant" as if it's already happening. (Long-time leaders also left in early 2026, which unnerved the market further.)

Adobe's clever move: "legally safe" AI

Why businesses pick Firefly

Adobe trained Firefly only on content it's allowed to use (licensed stock photos, public-domain work). That sounds boring — but it's exactly why big companies choose it. A corporate legal team can't risk AI that may have been trained on stolen images. "Legally safe" clears the lawyers, and rivals like Midjourney can't easily offer that. It's a real advantage — with businesses.

The main worry

Free AI getting "good enough" faster than Adobe can cash in. If newer models from OpenAI, Google, or Midjourney improve quickly and become good enough for most everyday users, those users just leave — before Adobe's AI revenue grows big enough to replace the lost pricing power. "Legally safe" protects the enterprise business, but it doesn't hold the casual creators who might walk.

The surprising part

The thing critics mock Adobe for — training its AI on dull licensed stock photos — is its single biggest advantage with corporations. And note the quiet catch: Firefly is getting lots of use and making real money, but it has not let Adobe raise the price of Photoshop. So the AI is defending the moat, not widening it.

In one sentence

Adobe in a nutshell

Unlike Chegg, Adobe saw the threat and built the disruptive AI itself — and it's working so far. The open question is whether "charging extra for safe AI" can outrun free AI getting good enough for everyone else.
