Group C · Where you can't prefab the physical

Skilled trades

You can't offshore or automate a burst pipe — so the rates only rise.

Hands-on repair — plumbing, electrical, HVAC, auto and appliance repair, dentistry — is inherently one-human, one-job. A plumber can only fix one leak at a time, in one basement, so the work resists productivity gains and gets pricier as wages rise. Add a shrinking, aging pool of tradespeople and the rates climb further. Ironically, as everyone chased college, the "uncool" trades became some of the most cost-disease-protected, well-paid work around.

The core bet

The fact underneath: skilled trades are geographically locked and can't be batched or offshored. As the workforce ages and fewer young people enter, prices rise faster than any productivity gain can offset — a textbook cost-disease spiral with no offshoring escape valve.

The accidental smart bet

You can't batch a plumbing leak across houses or pre-tape an electrical fault. Every job is a house call, so travel time and one-at-a-time work are baked in. As college drew people away and the trades grew scarce, the "unglamorous" path became one of the most recession-proof, well-paid careers around — often competitive with degree jobs, without the debt.

What's newly valuable
Scarce skilled hands you can't offshore or automate — pricing power that only grows as the workforce shrinks.
Why it climbs
One-human-one-job work, an aging pool, and rising licensing/insurance costs pushing out small operators.
The single biggest worry

The demographic cliff is real: apprenticeships stay unattractive while boomers retire and licensing/insurance costs climb. If the pipeline keeps shrinking, emergency calls (a burst pipe, summer HVAC) become pure scarcity pricing with no natural ceiling.

The surprising part

The trades became accidentally recession-proof and well-paid — not through glamour but its opposite. A plumber or electrician can out-earn many degree jobs without the student debt, precisely because the work can't be offshored or automated. Yet enrollment still lags, which says culture, not money, is the real barrier.

In one sentence

The trades are trapped in a cost-disease loop — one-human-one-job work, a shrinking aging workforce, no offshoring escape — so prices simply rise until they hit what homeowners can pay.
