Live performance

The original cost-disease example: a string quartet is as "productive" as in 1820.

This is where the whole idea was born. Economists William Baumol and William Bowen (1966) studied live performance and noticed something brutal: a Beethoven string quartet takes exactly four musicians and the same forty minutes it did two centuries ago — zero productivity gain in 200 years. Yet the musicians' wages must rise with the economy, so live music, theater, and opera get structurally more expensive, forever.

The core bet / core idea

The insight: a live string quartet in 1820 and 2020 are identically "unproductive" — same players, same time. If factory wages rose 20× since then, the musicians' wages rose too, or they'd quit. So the ticket that cost $1 costs ~$20 (plus inflation). Not inefficiency — mathematical inevitability.

What the numbers say

1966
Baumol & Bowen publish "Performing Arts: The Economic Dilemma," coining the idea from labor-intensive live performance where productivity is stuck.
4 players, ~40 min
A string quartet, 1820 and 2020 — identical output per hour. The purest proof that some work simply can't speed up.
$50–200+
Typical live ticket prices as orchestras, theaters, and opera companies cut performances and lean on donors and endowments to survive.
1967
Baumol's "Macroeconomics of Unbalanced Growth" formalized the math: if wages rise everywhere but productivity only rises in some sectors, the laggards' costs climb without limit.

The template for everything else

Live performance is the clean case that explains health, teaching, and care. You can't outsource a haircut, pre-tape a live show, or batch a surgery. The audience has no substitute for the real thing — and that inescapable human presence is exactly why the price rises relative to everything mass-produced.

Why it climbs
Zero productivity gain, wages that must track the economy, and demand for a live human that can't be faked.
What survives
The premium live experience — precisely because it can't be reproduced (see how recorded music only raised concert prices).
The single biggest worry

As wages rise everywhere but the arts can't speed up, they consume an ever-larger share of income — or price out ordinary people entirely, making live culture a luxury only the wealthy can afford.

The surprising part

The whole theory came from staring at one brutal fact: a string quartet is exactly as "productive" today as two centuries ago. That sameness isn't a failure of management — it's proof that some human activities simply can't be sped up, and that's precisely why they keep getting more expensive relative to everything a factory keeps making cheaper.

In one sentence

The arts prove that some work can't be traded for time — so rising wages everywhere else make it structurally more expensive, no matter how hard anyone works.
