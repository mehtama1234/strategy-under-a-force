Group A · Where a human hour is the product

Childcare

Unaffordable for parents and underpaid for workers — at the same time.

Childcare is a perfect cost-disease trap. Safe ratios — one adult per three or four infants — are fixed by law and by physical reality. You literally cannot make one worker watch forty babies. So productivity can't rise, but wages must, and the cost climbs until childcare rivals rent or college. Yet the workers are paid poorly — the money vanishes into the thin ratios, not into their pockets.

The core bet

The fact underneath: with ratios capped, there's no productivity escape — a caregiver watches the same number of babies in 2024 as in 1995. Costs must rise with wages, but there are no efficiency gains to fund good pay, so parents overpay and workers underearn at once.

What the numbers say

$10–20k+
Typical annual cost of full-time infant care — often more than in-state college tuition, and 6–8%+ of a middle family's income.
~$28,500
Median childcare-worker wage (2023) — roughly fast-food pay — with 30–40% annual turnover. The high price isn't reaching the workers.
1:3–1:4
Legal infant-to-caregiver ratios — non-negotiable for safety, and the exact reason productivity can't rise.
50%+
Share of US counties that are "childcare deserts" (no licensed care); ~40% of providers closed 2020–24. Even payers can't always find a spot.

The impossible paradox

How can something be unaffordable for families and underpaid for workers? That contradiction is the signature of cost disease. The high price isn't flowing to staff — it's absorbed by the immovable math of ratios, facilities, and compliance. Parents squeeze, workers squeeze, and the gap widens with no productivity gain to bridge it. It's also a driver of the falling birth rate — kids have become unaffordable.

Why it climbs
Fixed ratios cap productivity; wages must still rise; the cost lands on parents while workers stay poorly paid.
What's newly valuable
Reliable licensed care itself — so scarce that finding a spot can matter more than the price.
The single biggest worry

The system is collapsing from both ends: parents can't find care even when they can pay, and workers flee because the wages don't justify the load — including the cost of their own childcare.

The surprising part

Childcare is expensive and its workers are underpaid — which sounds impossible until you see the mechanism. There's no productivity gain to fund good wages, so the money disappears into thin ratios. A caregiver in 1995 watched the same number of babies as one does today; that's exactly why the price is high and the pay is low.

In one sentence

Childcare costs have outpaced inflation while worker wages lag it — the defining symptom of a service that simply can't be scaled.
