Group C · Where you can't prefab the physical

Construction & housing

Building a house barely got more efficient in 50 years.

Construction is the rare industry where measured productivity has been flat — or falling — for decades, even as manufacturing tripled. Building is still largely on-site, custom, and hand-built by skilled trades, hard to turn into a factory line. So homes keep getting more expensive to build, feeding the affordability crisis — a huge hidden driver on top of land and zoning. This is the cost-disease side of real estate.

The core bet

The fact underneath: while almost everything got cheaper to make, a house didn't — construction productivity is roughly flat since the 1970s. Labor is the stickiest cost, the work is site-specific and weather-dependent, and prefab keeps failing to scale. So the housing crisis is as much a cost-of-building problem as a land-and-zoning one.

What the numbers say

~0.5%/yr
Construction productivity growth since 1970 — versus 2%+ in manufacturing (which tripled). Building barely improved in 50 years.
+40%
Real rise in building cost per square foot since 2000 ($150–250/sq ft in 2024); labor is 25–35% and the stickiest piece.
<5%
Prefab/modular share of the US housing market — despite being "the answer" since the 1960s. On-site hand-building still dominates.
6–18 mo
Typical permitting delay before a shovel touches ground — adding 10–20% to timelines and locking in inflation on wages and materials.

Prefab keeps failing

A smartphone went from $500 to $800 with a thousand times more power; a house costs about the same in real dollars to build as in 1990. Manufacturing cracked the code — robots, supply chains, standardization — and construction never did. Prefab was "the future" in 1960, 1990, and 2015. The exception proves the rule: modular hotels work because the spec is rigid; homes stay custom because everyone wants their own design.

Why it climbs
On-site, custom, trade-heavy work resists the factory; permitting adds delay and cost.
What could break it
Genuinely industrialized building (rigid-spec modular, 3D printing) — but it hasn't scaled in 60 years of trying.
The single biggest worry

Housing affordability can't be solved by zoning alone. Even if every city upzoned tomorrow, the cost-per-unit would barely move, because the building process itself is stuck in the 1970s — a floor beneath which prices can't fall without builders taking losses.

The surprising part

While a phone got a thousand times more powerful and a car got safer and cleaner, the cost to construct a 2,000-square-foot house is about the same in real dollars as in 1990. Manufacturing industrialized; construction never did — and prefab has been "coming soon" for sixty years.

In one sentence

Construction productivity flatlined since 1970 while everything else industrialized — turning housing into a permanent cost-disease exemplar where you build homes less efficiently than your grandparents did.
