Group E · The big picture

Cost disease

The sectors that can't get more efficient are the ones eating your paycheck.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one mechanism explains all of them. Health care, college, childcare, elder care, housing, and the arts keep getting more expensive while TVs, clothes, and toys keep getting cheaper — because those services are labor-heavy and can't raise productivity, yet their wages must track the wider, productive economy. Society pays through debt, subsidy, and crowding-out. It's the hidden engine of the affordability crisis.

The one idea

The sectors that can't get more efficient are the ones eating your paycheck. Be fair, though — pure Baumol isn't the whole story. Subsidy inflation, regulation, monopoly, and bloat pile on top. Cost disease is the floor beneath the affordability crisis, not its entirety.

Winners vs losers

Winners
Productivity-scaled sectors (software, semiconductors, logistics), asset holders, and anyone who owns a productivity-immune service with captive demand and pricing power.
Losers
Middle-class families (the four essentials eat their income), public-sector workers, and anyone without assets — priced out of housing, care, and college.
The biggest tension

Baumol and the subsidy-inflation loop feed each other. Governments subsidize to make services "affordable"; providers raise prices to capture the subsidy; demand rises, capacity doesn't, prices spike further. Eventually fiscal room runs out and services get rationed (waits), cut in quality (bigger classes, rushed visits), or funded by debt. The crisis is partly Baumol's iron law, partly the feedback loop that makes it worse.

The surprising part

"Cost disease" sounds like a failure of doctors, teachers, and caregivers — but they're doing jobs that are structurally immune to efficiency gains. The disease is an artifact of the broader economy's success: because tech and manufacturing got so productive, wages rose everywhere, and a nurse can't earn far less than a programmer. A society that got richer overall experiences rising scarcity in the services that matter most to families.

In one sentence

Efficiency gains in some sectors directly create cost inflation in others — so the sectors that can't get more efficient are the ones eating your paycheck.

How this connects to the rest of the series

Cost disease is a Family III structural pattern, and it braids into several forces. Elder care and childcare are cost disease meeting the demographic cliff. Construction is the cost-disease core of the housing crisis. And accreditation and licensing (the gatekeeper tax) raise the floor further in health and education — a structural pattern stacked on a structural pattern.
