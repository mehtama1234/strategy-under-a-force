Group B · Where knowledge is hand-delivered

K-12 schools

You can't mass-produce human attention — so schooling keeps getting pricier.

Schooling is labor-intensive — one teacher, a class of kids — and class sizes can only grow so far before quality drops. Per-pupil spending has roughly doubled (inflation-adjusted) since 1970 with flat test scores. Not because schools got lazy or teachers got rich, but because education is a hand-delivered human service that can't be automated, and every attempt to replace the teacher with technology has failed.

The core bet

The fact underneath: you cannot make one teacher "more productive" without harming the teaching. So costs must rise with wages while output holds flat — and the ed-tech that promised to cut headcount instead added a new line item on top.

What the numbers say

~$3,500 → $16,000
Real per-pupil spending, 1970 to the 2020s — roughly doubled to tripled in inflation-adjusted dollars.
flat
NAEP math and reading scores — essentially unchanged since the 1980s despite the spending surge.
2–3×
Growth of non-teaching staff (aides, counselors, admin) vs teacher headcount since 1970 — absorbing ~half the budget growth.
5–10%
Share of budgets now on ed-tech hardware and software — with no reliable evidence it cut teacher demand or lifted outcomes.

Teaching was always expensive

Class sizes stayed roughly flat (23–25 students) across decades, because bigger classes hurt learning. Teacher pay actually lagged other degree-holders — yet total costs rose, because the service can't be sped up and staffing grew around it. The paradox is that schools are doing what the economics predict: labor-intensive services face the same spiral as hospitals and elder care.

Why it climbs
Human attention can't be scaled; wages track the economy; support staff and tech add cost.
The honest note
Teacher pay lagging suggests the money isn't "waste" — it's the structural cost of a human-delivered service.
The single biggest worry

If per-pupil spending must keep rising just to hold pay competitive and class sizes constant, taxpayers eventually hit a ceiling — and schools then shrink, cut services, or accept shortages that degrade quality anyway.

The surprising part

Teaching isn't broken — it's fundamentally expensive, and always was. We believed technology would fix it, and it didn't: computers and smartboards added cost without replacing the teacher, because you can't mass-produce the human attention that is the actual product.

In one sentence

K-12 costs surge not because schools are wasteful but because you can't mass-produce human attention — and every attempt to replace teachers with machines has failed.
