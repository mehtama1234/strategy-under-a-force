Group D · The mechanism & the fight

The Baumol mechanism

Why it happens — the flip side of progress, in plain English.

Here's the engine under the whole collection. Some work can't get more productive — a nurse, a teacher, or a string quartet can't serve twice as many people without cutting corners. But in a growing economy, their wages must still rise with everyone else's, or they'll quit for the jobs that got more productive. So the cost of these services climbs forever, even though the work itself didn't improve. It's not waste. It's math.

The core idea

If factory workers double their output and their wages double to match, but a string quartet can't double its output (still four players, real time), then paying those musicians the going wage means the ticket must cost twice as much. Labor-heavy services get pricier relative to manufactured goods, forever.

What the numbers say

1966
Baumol & Bowen named it, studying live performance where productivity is stuck. Baumol formalized the math in 1967 ("unbalanced growth").
the quartet test
Four players, one hour — identical in 1820 and 2020. If wages rose 20× since, the ticket rose ~20× too, purely from wage-tracking.
5% → ~18%
US health care as a share of GDP, 1980 to today; college tuition rose ~3× faster than inflation — while electronics and cars fell in real price.
captive demand
You can't pre-tape a live show, batch a surgery, or outsource a haircut — so buyers have no substitute but to pay.

Wages are one market

The key move: labor is a single market. A nurse can't earn far less than a programmer, even though her productivity ceiling is lower — or she'd switch careers. So to keep nurses, teachers, and musicians, their pay rises with the productive economy, even though their output didn't budge. The cost of their services climbs as a direct result.

What it does
Makes human-touch services rise in price relative to everything a factory keeps making cheaper.
The honest caveat
Baumol is the floor, not the whole story — subsidy inflation, regulation, and bloat pile on top (see later pages).
The single biggest worry

If cost disease runs unchecked, ordinary life — being born, educated, healed, housed, and cared for — drifts out of reach for ordinary people. You already see it: college needs debt, elder care risks bankruptcy, childcare needs two incomes.

The surprising part

Cost disease is not a bug in the economy — it's a feature of progress. The very innovations that made factories and computers hyper-productive are what force up the price of human-touch services. A richer, more automated society automatically makes care, teaching, and craft feel more expensive. It's the dark twin of abundance.

In one sentence

Cost disease is the invisible tax on human work: as the economy gets more productive and wages rise everywhere, the services that can't automate must get more expensive forever.
