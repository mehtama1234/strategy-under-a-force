Group C · Where you can't prefab the physical

Infrastructure

$2.5 billion for three subway miles — the productivity that went backward.

The US builds infrastructure far more expensively and slowly than it used to, and than peers do today. New York's Second Avenue Subway ran ~$2.5B for 3.4 miles — among the priciest on earth. California High-Speed Rail ballooned from ~$33B to $100B+ and decades late. It's labor-heavy, custom, and buried in process, litigation, and consultants — the opposite of a factory. Cost disease, plus institutional bloat.

The core bet

The fact underneath: this isn't harder geography or technology — it's that the work is labor-and-process-heavy and productivity went backward. The same country that built fast and cheap in the 1930s now needs years of review and record sums to lay a few miles of transit.

What the numbers say

~$730M/mi
NYC's Second Avenue Subway (Phase 1): ~$2.5B for 3.4 miles — many times what peer countries pay per mile.
$33B → $100B+
California High-Speed Rail — the estimate roughly tripled and slipped by decades; still no revenue service.
~13 months
Time to build the Empire State Building (1930–31), 102 stories. An equivalent today would take many years of review before ground breaks.
~$260M/mi
Tokyo's Oedo Line (1990s), deep tunnel, on time — a fraction of US costs. The US isn't building harder projects; it's building them dearer.

Process ate the productivity

The US didn't lose the ability to build fast — it lost the institutional permission. Environmental review, community hearings, litigation, and layers of consultants stretch timelines for years, and every delay locks in more wage and materials inflation, which then justifies more process. Measured per worker-hour, construction productivity is actually lower than in 1930, despite power tools and computers, because the work is fragmented and every step is inspected or renegotiated.

Why it climbs
Bespoke, labor-heavy work wrapped in process, litigation, and consultants — a vicious cycle where delay breeds more delay.
What peers show
Countries that treat it like a disciplined, repeatable process build the same tunnels for far less.
The single biggest worry

Each delay and lawsuit locks in inflation and overhead, so cost overruns spiral backward into justifications for more process — a loop where $2.5B/mile becomes the new floor, and needed transit, water, and grid projects simply don't get built.

The surprising part

America built the Empire State Building in about thirteen months in the 1930s but now takes years and record sums to lay a few subway miles. Infrastructure is a labor-and-process-heavy sector where productivity ran in reverse — the US builds like a bespoke tailor in a litigious market, while others treat it like a factory with process discipline.

In one sentence

America builds infrastructure like a custom tailor in a courtroom; everyone else runs it like a factory — which is why a few miles of subway can cost billions.
