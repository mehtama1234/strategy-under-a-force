Group A · Where a human hour is the product

Elder care

Cost disease colliding with the demographic wave, right when we need it most.

Caring for an aging person is pure human labor — feeding, bathing, helping them move, sitting with them. You can't automate it or speed it up without harming the person. And it's arriving exactly as the population ages: the 65+ share is climbing toward 22%, the 85+ group is booming. Demand explodes, the work stays slow and personal, and the cost soars. This is cost disease meeting the demographic cliff.

The core bet

The fact underneath: the one thing an aging society needs more of — a human sitting with another human — is the one thing you can't make more productive. So costs climb relentlessly, most of it falls on families or Medicaid, and a chronic caregiver shortage keeps the pressure on.

What the numbers say

$100k+
Typical annual private-pay nursing-home cost ($150k+ in high-cost states), rising 5–7% a year for a decade. Assisted living and home aides climb too.
~60%
Share of nursing-home beds funded by Medicaid — eating 4–8% of state budgets, often at rates below the actual cost of care.
~800k
Projected US caregiver shortage, with ~30% annual turnover — aides earn $18–25/hour for physically and emotionally punishing work.
→22%
US 65+ share heading toward 22% by 2050; the 85+ group (who need the most care) roughly doubling. Demand is a wave, not a blip.

Squeezed from every side

Families can't afford $100k+ a year; states can't absorb rising Medicaid costs without cutting elsewhere; and the workforce is itself aging and short-handed. Middle-class seniors spend down their assets to near-zero to qualify for Medicaid — a quarter of those needing long-term care fall into poverty doing it.

Why it climbs
Booming demand meets un-speed-up-able work; wages must rise to attract scarce aides, but families and states are already maxed.
What's newly valuable
Anyone who can supply reliable caregiving hours — the scarcest input in an aging society.
The single biggest worry

The gap between booming demand and affordable supply looks unstoppable: families can't pay out of pocket, states can't raise Medicaid rates without breaking budgets, and the caregiver workforce is aging and shrinking. The squeeze is building toward a crisis.

The surprising part

Caring work is the opposite of automation-friendly. You can't make bathing or comforting a person with dementia 50% faster without degrading it — a robot won't do. So while every other sector chases productivity, elder care is stuck: demand surges, the work stays human, workers burn out and leave, wages must rise to replace them, and the cost climbs faster than inflation, exactly when society needs it most.

In one sentence

An aging society needs the one thing that can't be automated — a human to care for a human — and its cost grows relentlessly as demand explodes and workers flee.
