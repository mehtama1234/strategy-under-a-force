The synthesis · what repeats across all 13

The patterns

Line up every sector that can't get more efficient, and one theme dominates: human-touch work gets pricier forever, because wages rise but productivity can't.

Each page asks: what did cost disease make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions repeat.

The one thing to take away

When a service can't be sped up but its workers' wages must still rise with the productive economy, its cost climbs forever — not from waste, but from math. So a richer, more automated society automatically makes care, teaching, craft, and shelter feel more expensive relative to everything a factory keeps making cheaper.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

The product is a human hour

A nurse, teacher, caregiver, or musician is the service — you can't mass-produce their attention, so it can't get cheaper.

Pattern 02

Wages rise, productivity can't

To keep workers from leaving for productive sectors, their pay must track the economy — even though their output is flat. Cost climbs as a result.

the mechanismfixed ratios
Pattern 03

Goods got cheaper; the essentials got pricier

TVs, clothes, and toys collapsed in price while health, college, and housing soared — the exact mirror image.

the squeezebig picture
Pattern 04

Technology raises the floor, not the efficiency

New scanners, ed-tech, and computers added cost without replacing the human — every prior "fix" made services do more, not cost less.

Pattern 05

You can't skip it — so you pay

Captive demand: you can't outsource a haircut, pre-tape a show, or forgo a diagnosis. No substitute means the price sticks.

crowding outthe house call
Pattern 06

Subsidy can make it worse

Money poured in (loans, Medicare) can signal providers to raise prices — chasing demand instead of relieving it.

Pattern 07

Expensive AND underpaid at once

Childcare and elder care cost a fortune yet pay workers poorly — the money vanishes into thin ratios, the signature of cost disease.

Pattern 08

Sometimes productivity goes backward

Construction and US infrastructure got less efficient over 50 years — process, litigation, and custom work outran the tools.
