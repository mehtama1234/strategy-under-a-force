Group A · Where a human hour is the product

Hospitals & clinical care

Good care is a trained human's irreplaceable time — and that time keeps getting pricier.

Health care is the textbook cost-disease sector. A nurse or doctor can't safely see four times the patients; care is human hours. So even as drugs and machines improve, the labor-heavy core keeps getting more expensive — US health spending is around 18% of GDP, roughly double its peers, and has grown faster than inflation for 40 years. And technology, oddly, tends to add cost rather than cut it.

The core bet

The core fact: the product is a licensed human's attention under time pressure. As the rest of the economy gets more productive and wages rise, hospitals must pay competitive wages to nurses and doctors — so care gets more expensive even when quality only holds flat. You can't replace a nurse with a scanner.

What the numbers say

~$4.5T
US health spending — about 18% of GDP (~$13,500 per person, 2024), roughly 2× peers like Germany and France, and rising faster than inflation for decades.
45–50%
Share of hospital costs that is labor — nursing, techs, physicians — and it tracks wage growth across the whole economy.
~450k
US nursing shortfall, with a projected ~37k physician gap by 2033 — scarcity that pushes wages up 4–6% a year in tight markets.
25–30%
Share of US health spending that is administration (vs 15–20% in peers) — a second, non-clinical layer of human cost.

Technology raises the floor

New drugs, scanners, and AI tools mostly raise the bar for what competent care looks like — so hospitals must invest to keep up, and each advance needs more specialized (pricier) staff. Unlike a chip factory where robots replaced people, hospital robots assist humans rather than replace them. A well-run hospital tends to spend more, not less, twenty years on.

What's newly valuable
Trained clinical hours — the one input you can't mass-produce — and anything that genuinely eases the labor shortage.
Why it climbs
Wages track the whole economy while the work can't be sped up; new tech adds to the bill.
The single biggest worry

Clinical wages will eventually outpace what insurers and government can sustain. If labor costs rise 4–5% a year while Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement flattens, hospitals cut staff ratios (worse care), close rural units, or shut down.

The surprising part

Technology doesn't cure cost disease in health care — it amplifies it. Every new drug or imaging tool raises the standard of care, requires more specialized training, and pulls wages higher. The thing that makes care expensive isn't waste; it's that good care is a skilled human's time, and that time gets pricier as everything else gets automated.

In one sentence

Health-care costs explode not because care is broken but because good care is a trained human's irreplaceable time — and that time gets more expensive as everything else gets cheaper.
