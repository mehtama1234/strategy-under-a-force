Higher education

Part true cost disease, part subsidy-fuelled arms race.

Teaching is labor-heavy — a professor can only reach so many students well — so the core can't get cheaper, and college costs have risen far faster than inflation for 40+ years. But there's a twist beyond pure Baumol: administrative bloat, an amenities arms race, and easy federal loans that let schools raise prices. Online learning promised to break it and mostly didn't — because what students buy is the credential and the campus, not just the lectures.

The core bet

The honest version: college is part cost disease (you can't teach faster) and part subsidy-fuelled arms race. Cheap federal loans made college "affordable" on paper, so schools stopped minimizing costs and started raising tuition to absorb the aid — competing on climbing walls and dorms, not efficiency.

What the numbers say

+1,300%
College tuition since 1980 — versus ~210% general inflation over the same 45 years. A gap far bigger than teaching wages alone can explain.
$1.7T
US student debt (2024), ~43M borrowers — the "affordable" loans that let schools keep raising prices.
1.6 : 1
Administrators per faculty member — admin staff grew faster than teaching, absorbing much of the cost growth.
~16%
Share of college cost growth that pure Baumol explains (one study) — the other ~84% is bloat, amenities, and subsidy inflation.

Why MOOCs failed

Online courses were supposed to let one professor reach millions at near-zero marginal cost. Instead they revealed the real product: students don't mainly buy lectures — they buy the credential, the campus, and the four-year sorting function. MOOCs had 90%+ dropout rates and cost a fortune to build well, and students who could get the same material free still paid full price for the diploma. This links to the gatekeeper that makes the credential matter.

Cost disease part
Teaching can't be sped up; professor wages track the economy.
The arms-race part
Cheap loans + amenities + admin bloat push tuition far past what Baumol alone would.
The single biggest worry

Debt is near $2 trillion, with tens of millions trapped in repayment. Loan-backed demand insulates schools from market pressure, so they compete on amenities and overhead instead of cost — and the debt keeps growing.

The surprising part

MOOCs were meant to break the model and instead exposed it: what students pay for isn't the lecture (available free) but the diploma's social proof and the sorting the campus provides. Building good online courses turned out harder than teaching live — proof that the human, credentialed experience is the actual product.

In one sentence

College is part cost disease (you can't teach faster) and part subsidy-fuelled arms race — cheap loans let schools raise prices instead of competing on efficiency.
