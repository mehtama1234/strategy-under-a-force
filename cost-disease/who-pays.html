Group D · The mechanism & the fight

Who pays

The few things you can't skip quietly eat your paycheck.

Because the productivity-immune sectors keep getting pricier, society pays in three ways: debt (student and medical loans), subsidy (governments pour money in, which can inflate prices further), and crowding out (these sectors swallow a bigger share of every paycheck and budget). This is the hidden engine of the middle-class "affordability crisis" — not general inflation, but runaway costs in the handful of things you can't avoid.

The core bet

The fact underneath: the affordability crisis isn't about TVs or clothes (those got cheaper). It's that the four things you can't skip — being healthy, educated, housed, and cared for — are exactly the cost-disease sectors, so they quietly take more of every paycheck each decade.

The subsidy trap

Subsidy can backfire. When government pours money into health or college — Medicare, Medicaid, student aid — it can signal to providers that they can raise prices; the money chases demand instead of relieving it, so subsidies sometimes accelerate cost disease. Once that loop locks in, the only exits are rare productivity gains or outright rationing.

Three ways we pay
Debt (loans), subsidy (taxes), and crowding-out (less left for everything else).
The one relief
Goods keep getting cheaper — which is exactly why the squeeze hides in plain sight.
The single biggest worry

Subsidy itself may be a trap: money poured in can signal providers to raise prices, so it chases demand rather than relieving it. Once locked in, the only escapes are productivity innovation (rare here) or rationing.

The surprising part

Middle-class life feels unaffordable not because TVs cost 50× more — they cost less — but because you can't opt out of the four things that cost far more. A family spent about half its income on housing, health, education, and childcare in 1960; it spends nearly 60% on the same four today. You can skip a car or a vacation, but not a diagnosis, a roof, a diploma, or a babysitter.

In one sentence

Cost disease is paid through debt, subsidy, and a shrinking discretionary life — squeezing the middle class not through general inflation but through the runaway cost of the few things no one can skip.
