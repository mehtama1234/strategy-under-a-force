Chips → foundry & tools

The chip designers you know depend on a Dutch machine-maker you don't — and it can only be made by one company on earth.

The chip world split into layers: fabless designers (Nvidia, Apple, Qualcomm) draw the chips but own no factories, and foundries like TSMC build them. But the real power moved further upstream still — to the irreplaceable toolmakers: ASML, whose machines print the circuits, and the two software firms without whose tools no chip can be designed at all.

The core bet

Being the "maker" — even a brilliant one like TSMC — isn't where the deepest moat sits. The most defensible layer is the one everyone depends on and nobody can replicate: the lithography machine, the design software, the instruction set. Value migrated to the makers of the tools the makers need.

The firm nobody's heard of holds the most power

Most people have never heard of ASML, yet it may be more indispensable than Nvidia or Apple: it makes the machine that prints the circuits everyone else depends on. Value pooled not with the maker of the chip, nor even the maker of the fab, but with the maker of the tools the fab needs — and the tools for those tools are more defensible still. That's also why chip-tool exports became a front line of geopolitics.

Who ends up rich

The upstream chokepoints — ASML, the EDA duopoly, and ARM — that decide what chips are even possible.

Who gets thin margins

Even the mighty foundries sit below the toolmakers; the assemblers and OEMs downstream keep the least.

The single biggest worry

A handful of firms — ASML, TSMC, Synopsys, Cadence, ARM — are now single points of failure for the entire global chip industry. A political restriction, a supply shock, or a technical failure at ASML would halt advanced chip production worldwide, with no substitute available.

The surprising part

The most capital-intensive, technically dazzling, visible part of the chain — actually manufacturing chips — is not where the deepest moat lives. The company that makes the one machine that prints the circuits is more irreplaceable than the company that designs the chip or the one that owns the fab.

In one sentence

In chips, profit and power moved from the fabs to the irreplaceable upstream layers — lithography machines, design-software duopolies, and instruction-set licensors — because those layers decide what can be designed and physically built at all.
