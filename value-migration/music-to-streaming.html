Group B · Cut a middleman, grow a new one

Music → streaming

Napster promised to free artists from the labels. Instead it installed a bigger gatekeeper — and the recording became free marketing.

For a moment around 2000 it looked like technology would hand power back to musicians: cut out the label, reach fans directly. What actually happened is a game of musical chairs — the label's control passed to the streaming platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube), which set the payouts. Artists now earn fractions of a cent per play and make their living somewhere else entirely.

The core bet

Disintermediation rarely ends in no middleman; it ends in a new, often bigger one. Streaming didn't free artists — it made the platform the new gatekeeper of discovery and payment, the two things that actually pay. The recording itself became a loss-leader used to sell tours and merch.

The missing middle-class musician

The touring act that once made a modest living from album sales largely vanished. Recorded music went from a primary income to a cost of doing business — you pay to record, then give it away to build an audience you monetize through concerts, merch, and sync licensing. The democratization promise crashed into a hard fact: distribution alone doesn't pay; controlling discovery and payment does.

Who ends up rich

The three or four streaming platforms that own discovery and set the payout.

Who gets thin margins

Working and mid-tier musicians, who now earn more from one concert ticket than from a million streams.

The single biggest worry

With only a handful of distribution channels setting payouts unilaterally and opaquely, artists have no negotiating power. The 2020s risk simply repeating the 2010s: a new monopoly replaces the old, and the artist is squeezed just the same.

The surprising part

A single sold vinyl record or concert ticket can be worth more to a musician than a million streams. The very tool sold as liberation — free, frictionless distribution — turned the recording into marketing and moved all the money to whoever owns the app the listener opens.

In one sentence

Cutting out the label didn't free musicians — it installed a more powerful middleman that captured the revenue while hiding it behind per-stream payments too small to live on.
