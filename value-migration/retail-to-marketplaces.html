Group B · Cut a middleman, grow a new one

Retail → marketplaces

A brand on Amazon rents access to its own customers — and if it succeeds, Amazon reads the data and launches a clone.

Amazon turned retail inside out. The brand used to own the customer relationship, the transaction data, and the shelf. Now Amazon owns all three, and the brand pays rent — fees plus advertising — to reach the very customers it once owned. The value didn't disappear; it migrated from "selling stuff" to "controlling access to the customer."

The core bet

The marketplace flipped the power: it owns the customer, the data, and the shelf, while brands pay a rising toll to sell on it. Amazon barely profits from most retail sales — it profits from sellers paying to sell, from ads, and from the cloud. The retail box is bait for the high-margin businesses around it.

Fund your rival, then fund the landlord

Because sellers have nowhere else to reach that scale, Amazon can raise fees and ad costs every year. And if a product sells well, Amazon has the sales data to clone it under its own private label — documented across chargers, storage, and kitchen goods. The successful brand ends up funding both its own competition and the platform that hosts it.

Who ends up rich

The marketplace — collecting fees, ad dollars, and data — plus its cloud and ads businesses.

Who gets thin margins

Brands and third-party sellers, doing the product work while handing the platform 30–45% and their playbook.

The single biggest worry

The squeeze only tightens. Fees and ad costs rise annually because sellers can't leave, and success invites a platform clone built on the seller's own data. The brand does the work; the platform keeps the economics — and can decide to keep even more.

The surprising part

A $1-billion brand on Amazon might hand the platform $300–400 million in fees, ads, and data value — and Amazon's operating profit on that $300M can exceed the brand's net profit on the $600M it keeps, because Amazon's costs are already spread across everything. The brand does the work; Amazon keeps the economics.

In one sentence

Amazon turned retail inside out — the platform now owns the customer, the data, and the shelf the brand used to own, charges the brand to reach them, and quietly uses the brand's own numbers to compete with it.
