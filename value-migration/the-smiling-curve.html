Group C · The new bottlenecks

The smiling curve

Draw the profit along a product's journey and it makes a smile: high at design, low at the factory, high again at brand and service.

In 1992 Acer's founder Stan Shih sketched a simple curve to explain where money lives in a product's life. Plot value from raw idea to final sale and it dips in the middle: the two ends — upstream R&D, design, and patents; downstream brand, marketing, and service — capture most of the value, while manufacturing in the middle is squeezed to almost nothing. It's why companies happily outsource the making and fight over the ideas and the customer.

The core bet

The most visible, capital-heavy, technically impressive part of the chain — actually making the thing at scale — is where the least money lives. The profit smiles at the ends: the ideas before, the brand and service after. The factory is a cost center, not a profit center.

The world's factory took the worst seat

The countries and firms that became "the world's factory" — building at enormous scale — locked themselves into the least profitable slice: volume-based, capital-heavy, labor-heavy work with no pricing power. Even as they built real scale, the profit migrated away to the design and branding hubs. Making the thing is essential and impressive; it just isn't where the money smiles.

Who ends up rich

The two ends — R&D, design, and IP upstream; brand, service, and ecosystem downstream.

Who gets thin margins

The middle — the factories doing the hardest, most visible physical work for the thinnest margin.

The single biggest worry

A whole economy can win the manufacturing race and still lose the profit race. Regions that built their strategy on being the low-cost factory have no pricing power and watch the value flow to wherever the ideas and the brand live.

The surprising part

A $1,000 phone can hold $50 of materials and $15 of assembly labor, yet the brand captures $400 in profit from the very same box. The part that looks like the real work — building it — is precisely where almost none of the money is.

In one sentence

Value in a supply chain follows a U-shaped smile — high at design and IP, low at manufacturing, high again at brand and service — which is why the profit went to California and Taiwan while the factories stayed in low-wage countries.
