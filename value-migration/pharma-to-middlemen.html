Group A · The profit leaves the maker

Pharma → the middlemen

The company that spent $2B inventing the drug keeps less of each sale than the chain that stocks the shelf.

In American medicine the money has quietly slid off the drug-maker and onto the people who sit between the pill and the patient: the pharmacy-benefit managers (PBMs) who decide what your insurance covers, the pharmacy chains that dispense, and the three giant distributors that ship almost everything. The lab took the risk and the ten years; the gatekeepers take the spread.

The core bet

Once a drug goes generic, making it is a commodity — dozens of factories can, so the price collapses 80–95%. The profit doesn't vanish; it moves to the one job that stays scarce: deciding who gets access. That job belongs to the PBMs, and they now out-earn the inventors per pill.

Access beats invention

The incentive quietly inverted. The system now pays best for saying yes — approving, stocking, dispensing — not for inventing. A pharmacy that merely shelves a generic and a PBM that merely lists it as covered can each keep more absolute profit per pill than the company that discovered the molecule. Reward flows to the gate, not the lab.

Who ends up rich

PBMs, big pharmacy chains, and the three distributors — everyone who controls access, not the molecule.

Who gets thin margins

Brand drug-makers (margins squeezed) and independent pharmacies (often dispensing generics below cost).

The single biggest worry

If inventors can no longer recoup R&D through price, the money for new drugs — especially unglamorous ones like antibiotics and rare-disease treatments — dries up. The machine gets very good at moving pills and worse at inventing them.

The surprising part

The firm that spent roughly $2 billion and ten years to create a new medicine often ends up with a thinner profit per pill than the chain that just puts it on a shelf and the PBM that just says "covered." We built a system that rewards handling the drug more than discovering it.

In one sentence

Value migrated from the lab that invents to the gatekeepers that control access — leaving drug-makers with commodity prices and the middlemen collecting a toll on every prescription.
