Group D · The mechanism & the moves

The conservation of profits

Profit behaves like water: it drains away from whatever everyone can now do cheaply and pools at the next thing that's still hard.

This is the rule underneath every page in this collection. When competition standardizes one layer of a business, the profit there doesn't disappear — it migrates to the adjacent layer that's still scarce and hard to copy. Two writers named the pattern: Clayton Christensen called it the "conservation of attractive profits," and Adrian Slywotzky wrote the book Value Migration in 1996.

The core bet

Profit flows toward the bottleneck and away from whatever just got easy. So the winner isn't whoever dominates the commoditized layer — it's whoever controls what comes next. The best financial move is often to leave your old strength behind and move upstream before competition catches you.

Effort is not the thing that pays

The uncomfortable lesson: profit doesn't reward how hard you work — it rewards how hard you are to replace. Kodak owned digital photography and couldn't profit from it. Samsung makes superb commodity chips while others captured the phone profits. Being the best in the world at a thing everyone can now do guarantees a margin near zero.

Who ends up rich

Whoever positions at the next scarce layer before competition arrives.

Who gets thin margins

Whoever defends the old layer — excellent at yesterday's bottleneck, and paid nothing for it.

The single biggest worry

You can be the best in the world at yesterday's scarce thing and still make no money. By the time it's obvious a layer has commoditized, customers have already moved what they value and rivals are already sitting upstream.

The surprising part

Two companies in the same supply chain, working exactly as hard: one earns 60% margins, the other 8% — purely because one does something scarce and the other doesn't. The profit isn't in doing the job better; it's in doing the job nobody else can do yet.

In one sentence

Profit migrates from whatever becomes standardized and cheap toward whatever is still hard to copy — so the winners are the ones who keep moving to the next bottleneck before competition arrives.
