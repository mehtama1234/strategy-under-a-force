Group A · The profit leaves the maker

Hardware → software

Two identical PCs run the same Windows on the same Intel chip — so the buyer picks the cheaper box, and the box-maker's margin dies.

The classic case is "Wintel." Through the 1990s the personal-computer market exploded, and the companies that assembled the machines — Compaq, Dell, HP — grew huge in revenue and stayed poor in profit. The money pooled in the two parts you couldn't swap out: Microsoft's Windows and Intel's chip. The same script has run again with phones, and again with AI servers.

The core bet

Whoever owns the one part that can't be substituted keeps the profit; whoever assembles all the swappable parts is trapped in a race to the bottom. It isn't about who works hardest or innovates most — it's about which layer the buyer can't shop around on.

What the numbers say

5–8% vs 30–40%
box-makers' gross margins vs Microsoft's and Intel's in the PC era — same industry, opposite economics.
90%+
Windows' share of PCs by 2000 — so OEMs had zero leverage on licensing terms.
under 10%
Dell's operating margin at its peak, vs Intel holding 25–35% in the same years.
35–45% vs 3–5%
the echo: Apple's iPhone gross margin vs assemblers like Foxconn and Samsung's hardware arm.
~70%
Nvidia's operating margin on AI chips (80%+ share) while server-makers Dell and HPE run at 5–8%.

The rule is substitutability

The split isn't driven by complexity — assembling PCs and servers takes real logistics and scale. It's driven by swappability. Any two servers with the same chip and OS are interchangeable, so buyers pick the cheapest and margin flees to whatever can't be copied: the instruction set, the operating system, the model weights. Every dollar of extra revenue the assembler chases buys a thinner slice.

Who ends up rich

Owners of the non-swappable layer — the OS, the chip architecture, the AI accelerator and its software stack.

Who gets thin margins

Assemblers and box-makers, who reinvest all their growth just to stay in a commodity game.

The single biggest worry

The value trap: assemblers must keep pouring capital into volume to stay relevant, but commoditization never stops. Eventually they either exit or shrink into pure contract manufacturers at 1–2% margins, while the platform owner gets richer without adding a single worker.

The surprising part

The echo from PCs to phones to AI servers is almost exact — because the underlying cause never changed. It's not the cleverness of the part that earns the margin; it's whether the buyer can swap it. That's why Nvidia keeps ~70% operating margin while the companies building the actual servers fight over single digits.

In one sentence

Whoever owns the one part nobody can swap out — the standard, the chip, the software layer — keeps the profit; the company that assembles everything else is left racing to the bottom.
