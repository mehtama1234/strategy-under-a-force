Group E · The big picture

Value migration

As a product matures, competition flattens the maker into a commodity — and the profit walks next door to the new bottleneck.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. A market starts with the product-maker earning fat margins. Over time competition turns the product into a commodity, and the margin migrates to a new layer — the middleman, the platform, the financer, the toolmaker, the aggregator. The company that "wins" isn't the one that makes the best product; it's the one that ends up owning the layer where profit pools next.

The core bet

Making things still creates enormous value and stays essential — the point is that value creation and profit capture come apart. A car is worth far more than it costs to build, but the financer now keeps more profit per unit than the automaker. A song creates deep cultural value, but the streaming platform captures the returns. Christensen's rule: profit pools don't grow much — they move.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

PBMs, Intel and Microsoft, Nvidia and the clouds, ASML and the EDA duopoly, Spotify, Amazon's ads and AWS, captive lenders, Apple — everyone sitting on the scarce bottleneck before or after the maker gets paid.

Losers

Compaq and the box-makers, music labels and mid-tier musicians, newspapers, brands renting their own customers, contract factories, and any maker selling a commoditized product at thin margins.

Who ends up rich

PBMs, standards-owners, chip and cloud giants, toolmakers, platforms, financiers, and aggregators — whoever controls the layer that stays scarce.

Who gets thin margins

Brilliant makers whose product got commoditized — drug inventors, box assemblers, musicians, publishers, factories — watching the profit walk next door.

The single biggest worry

Profit concentration looks like unfairness — the carmaker employs 10,000 engineers while the finance arm earns more with a small team; the musician makes the art while the platform profits from the playlist. But it's how competitive markets work: if a thing can be replicated, its profit falls toward zero, and only the unreplicable layer keeps margin. The dark pull is that makers are pushed to stop merely making and integrate into the scarce layer.

The surprising part

Value migration often looks like "the maker got worse at their job" when they actually got better. Pharma companies make better drugs than ever; they're just richer as manufacturers than as profit-keepers, because competition solved the hard problem and the pie's growth went elsewhere. That's why mature makers get run for cash, bought, broken up, or forced to integrate — there's nowhere left to grow profitably in the core.

In one sentence

As products mature, profit migrates from the maker to whoever controls the now-scarce layer — financer, platform, aggregator, standard, or middleman — because the only profit left is in the irreplaceable thing.
