The synthesis · what repeats across all 13

The patterns

Line up every corner of the reshoring push, and the same handful of mechanics repeat — all of them tracing back to one word: constraint.

Each page asks: what did reshoring-by-constraint make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions repeat.

The one thing to take away

We're not bringing factories home because it's cheaper — we're doing it because we're forced to, and it's turning out to be hard. The goal flipped from efficiency to security, so resilience is worth paying for and low cost became a liability. But the West forgot how to build: a real cost premium, a worker shortage, enormous subsidies, and a "made-at-home" product still full of Chinese parts. Doing it well is the new hard problem — and much of the "return" is really near-shoring, automation, and announcement.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Security replaced efficiency as the goal

For 30 years the rule was "cheapest wins." Shocks and geopolitics flipped it to "safest wins," and companies now pay a premium for resilience they'd never have chosen on cost.

the flipinsure, don't optimize
Pattern 02

It's a constraint, not a choice

Tariffs, export controls, security rules, and subsidy strings compel the move; policy risk replaced cost risk as the real business problem.

policycompelled
Pattern 03

Home is expensive, so "close" and "friendly" win

Most "reshoring" is really near-shoring to Mexico or friend-shoring to India and Vietnam — cheaper than home, safer than China.

near-shoringthe premium
Pattern 04

The deep supply chain stays Chinese

Reshoring moves the visible final step home while mining, refining, cells, and wafers stay routed through China — resilience theater over a fragile core.

hidden dependency
Pattern 05

The factories come back; the jobs don't

Automation is the only way expensive-home-labor plants pencil out, so reshoring returns the factory with a fraction of the promised employment.

automationno workers
Pattern 06

The bill is permanent, and someone pays

The 30-50% cost premium doesn't close with scale, so it's carried forever by taxpayers (subsidies), consumers (prices), or margins — regressively.

who payssubsidy race
Pattern 07

Announced is not built

Construction spending soared while factory employment barely moved; a lot of "reshoring" is delayed, paused, or just re-labeled goods routed through Mexico.

real or theaterdelays
Pattern 08

The shocks made lean look reckless

Five disruptions in a row turned just-in-time into just-in-case — permanent buffer stock and redundant, closer suppliers are the new baseline cost.

the shocks
