Group C · The hard part

The labor shortage

The West spent decades sending manufacturing away — and lost the workers, trades, and know-how to run modern factories.

The West spent decades sending manufacturing away, so it lost the workers, trades, and know-how to run modern factories. Now reshoring is bottlenecked by a shortage of skilled workers: TSMC's Arizona fab was delayed partly for lack of trained technicians, and it flew in Taiwanese workers, sparking friction. Studies project the US will need hundreds of thousands of manufacturing and semiconductor workers it doesn't have.

The core bet

Reshoring is constrained not by capital or government incentives, but by people. The trained technicians, process engineers, and skilled trades simply don't exist at the scale needed — a pipeline dismantled over 40 years.

What the numbers say

~500-800
Taiwanese engineers TSMC flew into Arizona (2023-24) to backfill roles US hiring couldn't fill — delaying the fab.
67,000-90,000
the projected US semiconductor-worker shortfall by 2030 (SIA); the pipeline supplies 2-3× fewer than demand.
~2M unfilled
manufacturing jobs that could go unfilled by 2030, with an aging workforce (median age ~45-47).
−40%
the shrinkage of community-college manufacturing programs since 2000; US apprenticeships run ~1/10th of Germany's rate.
$65-85k

The skills went away

The labor shortage is now the primary bottleneck. Even with unlimited subsidies, companies can't staff factories faster than they build them. If it persists, reshoring stalls, or leans on imported workers (eroding the independence rationale), or compresses skill requirements to cope — and the worst case is reshored fabs running at 70-80% capacity, destroying the economics that justified them.

Who ends up ahead

Skilled trades, staffing and training firms, and workers who can command premium wages for scarce skills.

Who's exposed

Manufacturers who can't staff their plants — and communities promised jobs a thin pipeline can't fill.

The single biggest worry

You can't build the workforce faster than the factories. Retirements alone could remove 500,000-800,000 workers in five to seven years, and rebuilding vocational training and apprenticeships is a decade-long project — so premium wages and flown-in foreign workers are stopgaps, not fixes, for a gap that will throttle capacity for years.

The surprising part

The shortage is partly self-inflicted. Decades of "college-or-bust" messaging steered young people away from manufacturing, vocational schools were defunded, and skilled trades vanished from guidance counseling. Now that fab jobs pay $100k+ in total comp, the pipeline is so dry that even a cultural reversal — rebranding manufacturing as cutting-edge — would take 10+ years to show results.

In one sentence

Reshoring is stalled not by capital or chips but by an acute shortage of skilled workers that premium wages and imported labor can't quickly fix — a gap four decades of anti-manufacturing messaging and underinvestment left nearly impossible to close in time.
