Group B · Where it's going

The subsidy war

Reshoring runs on government money — and now countries are in a bidding war to lure the same factories.

Reshoring runs on government money, and countries are now in a bidding war to lure factories: the US CHIPS Act and IRA, the EU Chips Act, Japan paying big for TSMC's Kumamoto fab, India's production-linked incentives, and China's own massive subsidies. Companies play governments off each other for the biggest handout, and economists warn of a costly race to the bottom and industrial-policy overreach.

The core bet

Governments are spending hundreds of billions to rebuild chip, battery, and manufacturing capacity at home — betting near-shoring costs less than the geopolitical risk of dependence. But it triggered a global subsidy arms race that distorts markets and shifts the cost onto taxpayers.

Overcapacity and a bidding war

The subsidy war is locking in overcapacity. A fab costs $10-20B and takes 4-5 years; if many countries subsidize construction at once, the world ends up with far more capacity than it can profitably use. When the subsidies stop, those plants either close (stranding taxpayer money) or run at a loss for years — and companies extract the subsidies during the boom, then abandon the capacity in the downturn, leaving governments holding the bag.

Who ends up ahead

Companies that extract the biggest incentives by playing governments off each other.

Who's exposed

Taxpayers funding the bidding war, and the sectors made more expensive by higher-cost domestic production.

The single biggest worry

Economists warn it's a tragedy of the commons on a $100B+ scale: the race only ends when countries run out of money. And by subsidizing production into higher-cost locations, governments inflate chip and battery prices globally — exactly when AI, EVs, and data centers need cheaper semiconductors to scale.

The surprising part

The irony is that reshoring makes strategic sectors less efficient, not more. A fab in the US or Japan costs 30-50% more to run than in Taiwan, so governments are paying to move production to higher-cost places — a protection racket dressed as strategic autonomy that slows the very industries (EVs, AI) it's meant to advance.

In one sentence

Governments are spending hundreds of billions to rebuild factories at home, triggering a bidding war where companies extract maximum subsidies and countries risk overcapacity that taxpayers fund long after the political win fades.
