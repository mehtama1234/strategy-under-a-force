Group C · The hard part

The cost premium

Making things at home costs 30-50% more — and someone always pays, through subsidies, prices, or thinner margins.

Making things at home costs more: higher wages, pricier land and energy, stricter regulation, and lost economies of scale and supplier density. A chip fab in Arizona reportedly costs much more to run than in Taiwan; US factory labor costs multiples of China's. Someone pays that premium — via subsidies (taxpayers), higher prices (consumers and inflation), or thinner margins (companies).

The core bet

Reshoring to the US, Europe, and allied nations costs significantly more than offshore production, and that gap doesn't close with time — it's a structural feature of high-wage, high-tax, strictly-regulated economies. The bet is that security and resilience justify paying a permanent premium.

What the numbers say

Permanent and regressive

The premium is permanent — it doesn't close with scale or time, because US labor, energy, and regulatory costs are structural and China's low-cost posture may persist for decades. That locks countries into subsidy dependency: if the subsidies end, manufacturing emigrates again immediately. Politicians can't reverse the subsidies without the optics of a "second exodus," so governments stay hostage to funding manufacturing indefinitely.

Who ends up ahead

Union construction and factory workers, equipment makers, and regions hosting new plants — where gains concentrate.

Who's exposed

Consumers (via inflation), taxpayers (via subsidies), and companies (via margins) — who bear the diffuse cost.

The single biggest worry

The cost premium is a ratchet: once companies accept paying 30-50% more, the math only works if a catastrophe actually happens (a Taiwan crisis, a lasting tariff regime). If tensions cool and tariffs fall, firms are stuck with expensive domestic production for a crisis that didn't come — while rivals who didn't reshore sweep the market.

The surprising part

The premium is quietly regressive: it's funded by broad inflation (everyone pays more for electronics) and tax dollars (everyone subsidizes fabs), but the gains concentrate in specific union jobs and regional real estate. A factory worker in Pennsylvania gets a $65k job; a retiree on a fixed income pays 10% more for a phone and a hearing aid. The political math works because the winners are concentrated and vocal while the losers pay diffusely and invisibly.

In one sentence

Reshoring costs 30-50% more than offshoring, and the bill — paid by consumers, taxpayers, or company margins — is permanent and regressive.
