Group E · The big picture

Reshoring by constraint

For 30 years the race was to the cheapest place on earth. Now companies come home not because they want to — because they must.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains them all. For three decades the global supply chain raced to the cheapest place on earth. That era is over — not because companies wanted to come home, but because they had no choice. Tariffs, export controls, security audits, political shocks, and hundred-billion-dollar subsidy packages made offshoring irrational or illegal. The reshoring boom isn't a triumph of manufacturing's return; it's a forced constraint companies are racing to solve.

The core bet

Solving the constraint means accepting a real cost premium, chasing skilled workers who don't exist, and still building products with Chinese inputs inside — because decoupling is harder than it sounds. Reshoring isn't a choice; it's a new landscape, and doing it well is the new hard problem.

Winners vs losers, across all thirteen

Winners

Automation and robotics vendors; Mexico and near-shoring hubs (which captured the actual capital and jobs); chipmakers and defense contractors (subsidized and tariff-protected); and skills-training firms cashing in on the labor gap.

Losers

China's low-cost assembly sites; US production-line workers (factories came home, robots came with them); small manufacturers who can't afford automation; and taxpayers funding permanent subsidies.

The single biggest worry

Reshoring was sold as security and jobs — de-risk the supply chain, buy American, employ your town. The reality is a three-way trap: security and resilience are expensive, and the only way to make reshored production competitive is to automate it to the bone, so the factories come home but the jobs don't. And the drive for independence collides with the fact that true decoupling is infeasible — a "reshored" fab still needs Chinese rare earths, a "reshored" pharma plant still needs Chinese ingredients. The constraint forces the reshoring; the economics force the automation; the politics promised the jobs. All three can't win at once.

The surprising part

The biggest reshoring story isn't the US — it's Mexico. America announced the factories; Mexico captured the actual flow of capital and jobs, because it offers a legal free-trade zone (USMCA), half-price labor, single-digit-day shipping, and no reputational cost. A "made in Mexico" component crosses the border tariff-free as North American content. It's reshoring theater on the US side, while the real economic shift happened in Monterrey and Guadalajara — Mexico quietly became the #1 source of US imports while the US celebrated its own factory boom.

In one sentence

Reshoring is not a strategy but a constraint — geopolitics and tariffs forced the factories home, automation made them viable, near-shoring to Mexico captured the jobs, and the US got the capital investment but not the employment it promised.
