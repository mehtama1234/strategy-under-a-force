Reshoring by Constraint

Bringing the factories home — not because it's cheaper, but because we're being forced to, and it's turning out to be hard.

The 35th force, and the cost side of the decoupling. For 30 years the logic was pure efficiency: make it wherever it's cheapest. Now geopolitics, shocks, and policy have flipped the calculation to resilience and security, so companies reshore, near-shore, and friend-shore even at a higher cost. But the West forgot how to build factories. The one idea: we're not bringing factories home because it's cheaper — we're doing it because we have to, and it's turning out to be hard.

The one question, asked of everyone

What did reshoring-by-constraint make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? Resilience and a secure supply became worth paying up for; low-cost efficiency became a liability; and skilled factory workers, subsidies, and control of the deep supply chain became the scarce prizes. The danger: it's expensive, short of workers, still full of Chinese parts — and a lot of it is announcement, not concrete.
