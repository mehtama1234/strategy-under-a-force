Group B · Where it's going

Near-shoring & friend-shoring

Home is too expensive, so production goes to the next-door neighbor or a friendly country instead.

Rather than fully returning to the US or Europe, much production is shifting to nearby or allied countries: Mexico (near-shoring — it overtook China as the top US trade partner), India and Vietnam (friend-shoring for electronics — Apple moving iPhone assembly to India), plus Eastern Europe and Morocco for the EU. It's cheaper than home but safer than China — the middle path.

The core bet

Production doesn't come all the way home — it goes to a trusted neighbor. You get cheaper labor and logistics than China, but with a government you trust and a supply line you can watch. It's the compromise between expensive reshoring and risky China dependence.

China games it too

Near-shoring doesn't shrink global complexity — it remaps it. Mexico's real advantage isn't cheap labor; it's the USMCA free-trade agreement no other country can match. India's iPhone share works because US policy actively favors it, not because India is inherently more efficient than China. And in a three-player twist, Chinese companies build factories inside Mexico — so Mexico gets the jobs while China keeps the margins and routes goods around the tariff wall.

Who ends up ahead

Mexico, India, Vietnam, Eastern Europe, Morocco — and the logistics and industrial-real-estate players around them.

Who's exposed

Firms that discover their "friendly" supplier is still China underneath, or get caught by rising near-shore costs.

The single biggest worry

Near-shoring trades one strategic risk for another: labor and infrastructure costs in friendly countries are rising fast, most supply-chain leaders hit tariffs in 2025, and companies can end up stuck halfway — too invested to flee to a cheaper option, too dependent on a narrow corridor to shift easily. And China isn't gone; it built factories inside Mexico, weaponizing near-shoring against the US.

The surprising part

The counterintuitive part is that near-shoring is less about production returning home and more about tariff arbitrage and legal leverage. Mexico's edge is the USMCA rulebook; the winners are those who can route goods to hit "North American content" thresholds. The competitors it was meant to avoid are already gaming it from the inside.

In one sentence

Near-shoring buys supply-chain peace through proximity and alliance, not efficiency — but it's already being gamed by the very rival it was meant to escape.
