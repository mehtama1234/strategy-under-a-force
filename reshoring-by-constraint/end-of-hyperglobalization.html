Group A · Why the tide turned

End of hyper-globalization

For 30 years the rule was "make it wherever it's cheapest." That rule quietly broke.

For three decades companies optimized ruthlessly for cost: manufacture where labor was cheapest (mostly China), ship globally, pocket the margin. Around 2008 that model hit a ceiling. Since then, firms relearned an older lesson — the cheapest supply chain is useless if it breaks, disappears when you need it, or puts your survival in the hands of a geopolitical rival.

The core bet

The calculation flipped from efficiency to security. Companies now weigh resilience, not just cost, and accept a higher price to avoid putting their whole supply base in one country — the shift from cost-optimized globalization to slower, safer "slowbalization."

Efficiency isn't coming back

The hidden cost is that hyper-efficiency is gone and probably not returning. The old math was seductive — a 20% labor saving in China swallowed small inefficiencies. But the pandemic and the 2021 shipping snarl showed a fractional saving vanishes the instant a ship is stuck in Suez or a fab is surrounded by geopolitical tension. Firms whose whole margin was built on Chinese cost are now caught between rising costs and buyers who won't absorb the hike.

Who ends up ahead

Diversified, resilient supply chains and the countries (Mexico, India, Vietnam) that absorb the shift.

Who's exposed

Companies whose entire margin depended on Chinese cost, and consumers who face higher prices.

The single biggest worry

Hyper-efficiency, once lost, is hard to rebuild — and profitability has never recovered for segments (fast fashion, consumer electronics, some auto suppliers) that can't migrate fast enough. The cheapest option is no longer the safe option, and there's no cheap substitute for the scale and density China spent 30 years building.

The surprising part

The shift isn't really economic — it's political and psychological. Pure cost would have justified staying in China with hedges. What moved the needle was Western executives concluding that a 30-year bet on deep entanglement with a geopolitical rival was a civilizational error. The CHIPS Act didn't pass because economists said it was efficient; governments decided resilience and sovereignty were worth buying.

In one sentence

The 30-year obsession with cost-optimized globalization is collapsing into a slower, costlier, politically safer world where companies pay more to avoid putting their survival in one country's hands.
