Group D · The mechanism & reality check

Constraint, not choice

Companies aren't reshoring to make money — they're doing it because laws, tariffs, and risk leave no choice.

This is the core idea. Companies aren't reshoring because it's cheaper or better — they're doing it because they're compelled to: by tariffs, export controls, national-security rules, subsidies with strings, customer and government demands, and pure risk-avoidance. The old goal was efficiency (lowest cost); the new goal is resilience and security, which costs more. Reshoring is a defensive move to avoid catastrophe, not an offensive move to make money.

The core bet

The shift from "optimize for cost" to "insure against disruption" isn't a choice — it's a constraint imposed by geopolitics and policy, and it costs companies 30-50% more. Reshoring is a compliance-and-insurance move, not a growth strategy.

Defense, not profit

The most telling reversal is that security and policy are now the cheapest justification for a move pure economics would reject. A CEO can't tell shareholders "we're paying 40% more to make chips in Ohio because it's our strategy" — that tanks the stock. But they can say "we're reshoring because CHIPS Act incentives, export-control compliance, and customer security mandates require it." The constraint flips the whole calculation: policy risk replaced cost risk as the real business problem.

Who ends up ahead

Firms that treat resilience as insurance and lock in subsidies and secure supply early.

Who's exposed

Firms that over-commit to expensive domestic production for a crisis that may not arrive.

The single biggest worry

The resilience premium is irreversible. Once companies accept paying 30-50% more, the math only pencils out if something catastrophic actually happens — a Taiwan-strait closure, a lasting tariff regime, a Chinese export ban. If tensions cool and tariffs are repealed, firms discover they've anchored themselves to expensive production for a crisis that didn't materialize, losing pricing power to rivals who waited.

The surprising part

The surprising inversion: instead of fighting for scarce wafers and cheap labor, companies now fight for CHIPS Act grants and tariff-policy certainty. Reshoring was economically insane yesterday and legally mandatory today — the same factory that would sink a stock as "strategy" is defensible as "compliance."

In one sentence

Reshoring isn't a business decision — it's a compliance move made necessary by tariffs, security rules, and geopolitical risk, framed as an insurance premium against catastrophic disruption, which makes resilience the scarce, expensive goal instead of efficiency.
