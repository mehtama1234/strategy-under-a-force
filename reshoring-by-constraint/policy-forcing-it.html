Group A · Why the tide turned

Policy is forcing it

Governments made offshoring risky and reshoring rewarded — tariffs as the stick, hundreds of billions in subsidies as the carrot.

Governments weaponized trade policy: US tariffs on Chinese goods (since 2018, expanded 2024-25), sweeping export controls on advanced chips and chipmaking tools to China (2022+), and huge subsidy laws — the CHIPS Act (~$52B for semiconductors) and the Inflation Reduction Act (~$370B+ for clean energy, EVs, batteries) — with "made in America" strings. The EU, Japan, and India followed. Stay abroad and pay tariffs; come home and pocket billions.

The core bet

Policy created a new cost calculus with sticks and carrots: tariffs and export controls make offshoring expensive or impossible, while subsidies tied to domestic production make reshoring lucrative. Companies now compete for government grants as fiercely as they once competed for cheap labor.

Strings and stranded assets

Export controls on chipmaking tools may matter more than tariffs. Tariffs are loud but negotiable; the quiet stranglehold is that China can't build advanced fabs without ASML's, Applied's, and Lam's equipment — and no foundry anywhere can upgrade without US approval. Policy flipped the lever from "make Chinese chips expensive" to "make it impossible for China to make advanced chips at all," turning reshoring from a choice into a necessity.

Who ends up ahead

Companies that capture the subsidies — chipmakers, battery makers, defense — plus domestic tool and equipment suppliers.

Who's exposed

Firms exposed to tariff reversals or to the 10-year China-expansion bans that come attached to the grants.

The single biggest worry

The reshoring bet assumes factories stay open once subsidies expire and tariffs erode. If politics shift and tariffs drop, companies have sunk $200B+ into US capacity that becomes a stranded asset when labor-cost gaps snap back. And the subsidies carry forward obligations — clawbacks, export controls, geopolitical vetoes — that lock in higher political fragility for a decade.

The surprising part

The deeper shift is that policy risk replaced cost risk as the real business problem. Reshoring was economically insane yesterday; today it's legally mandatory. Instead of fighting for cheap wafers, companies now fight for CHIPS Act grants and tariff certainty — the government, not the market, sets the map.

In one sentence

Governments stacked tariffs, export controls, and subsidy strings so high that staying offshore now costs more than coming home — while coming home locks companies into a state-dependent supply chain that dissolves if politics change.
