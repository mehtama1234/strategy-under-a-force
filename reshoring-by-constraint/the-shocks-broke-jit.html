Group A · Why the tide turned

The shocks broke just-in-time

Five disruptions in a row shattered the faith that lean, globe-spanning supply chains would absorb any hit.

A run of disruptions shattered faith in lean, globe-spanning "just-in-time" supply chains: COVID (2020-22) closed factories and caused shortages of everything from chips to toilet paper; the Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal (2021); Russia's invasion of Ukraine (2022) hit energy and grain; the chip shortage idled car plants; and Red Sea attacks (2023-24) rerouted global shipping. Companies swung from "just-in-time" to "just-in-case."

The core bet

Just-in-time worked because global shipping was stable and predictable, so companies eliminated warehouses and buffer stock. Five consecutive shocks proved disruption is now the baseline, not a rare bump — so firms accepted permanently higher inventory and more, closer suppliers as the cost of staying alive.

Disruption is the baseline now

The real terror is that no single company can fix this alone. When a Taiwanese fab goes dark or a chokepoint clogs, one firm's safety stock buys maybe a week — then everyone upstream and downstream hits the wall together. The new reality demands visibility and redundancy across the whole supply web, which is expensive, slow to build, and means living with permanently higher inventory costs.

Who ends up ahead

Firms that built resilience early — dual sourcing, buffer stock, nearer suppliers.

Who's exposed

Any company that stayed lean to cut costs — it becomes the first to stop production when the next shock hits.

The single biggest worry

A company that keeps running lean while rivals build resilience becomes the first to halt when the next shock lands — and shocks are no longer rare events. But resilience isn't free: more inventory, more suppliers, and closer-to-home production all raise permanent costs, and no one firm can secure a supply web it doesn't fully control.

The surprising part

The reshoring that followed hasn't actually saved much money yet — and often costs more. Nearshoring or reshoring means less efficient production (wage gaps, smaller scale, plants still ramping). Companies do it anyway because time-to-resilience beats unit cost: control the supply line and you can move faster and dodge a crisis before it hits. Speed and control, not price, became the new edge.

In one sentence

Just-in-time was a bet that disruptions were rare and short; five straight shocks proved they're now the baseline, so companies accepted permanently higher inventory and fragmented supply chains as the price of survival.
