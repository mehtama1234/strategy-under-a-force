Group B · Where it's going

The factory boom

Subsidies triggered a wave of giant chip and battery plants — then came the delays, the pauses, and the missing workers.

Subsidies set off a wave of giant factory announcements: TSMC's Arizona fabs (~$65B), Intel's Ohio complex (~$28B+), Samsung in Texas, plus a "battery belt" of EV and battery plants across Georgia, Tennessee, and Kentucky. US construction spending on manufacturing plants roughly doubled. But many projects face delays, cost overruns, worker shortages — and some EV plants paused as demand softened (2024-25).

The core bet

The US government poured $52B+ into rebuilding chip and battery capacity, betting reshoring means national security and tech independence. The wager assumed demand would hold through the 2020s and that builders could beat supply-chain delays and labor shortages to hit their timelines.

Delays and paused plants

The core problem is a timeline-demand mismatch: multi-year factory construction (2023-27+) collides with fast-changing demand. The AI boom favors high-end chips, not commodity fabs; EV orders softened in 2024. If demand peaks or shifts before the plants come online, the country sinks $200B+ into idle or low-utilization capacity — and Intel's fabs bring no advantage unless Intel's own chip designs stay competitive.

Who ends up ahead

Construction and equipment firms, and the swing-state regions hosting the plants.

Who's exposed

Taxpayers if plants sit idle, and communities promised jobs that automation and delays don't deliver.

The single biggest worry

Construction outran everything else: factory announcements promised 100,000+ jobs by 2025, but actual hiring lags badly, and skilled trades hit shortages at multiple sites at once. Some battery plants couldn't find workers even at $50-60/hour — labor, not capital, became the bottleneck, while the fabs' heavy automation meant the promised job counts were always lower than politicians claimed.

The surprising part

The jobs story broke badly. The plants are genuinely rising — but the headcount that was supposed to justify them keeps shrinking against reality, because modern fabs and battery lines are heavily automated by design. America is getting the factories; it isn't getting the manufacturing-belt employment it was sold.

In one sentence

America bet ~$400B that reshoring chip and battery plants would happen fast enough to lock in demand and talent — but delays, EV-demand swings, and worker shortages mean the factories land in a market that has moved on.
