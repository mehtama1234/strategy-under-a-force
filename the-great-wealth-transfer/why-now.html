Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why now

A huge generation got rich riding forty years of asset inflation — and it's aging out all at once.

This is the mechanism. Two things collided: the boomers (a huge generation born 1946–1964) are now in their 60s–80s and dying, and they accumulated more wealth than any prior generation because of four decades of rising stock and house prices. So an unusually large generation holds an unusually large pile of appreciated assets, all coming due at once.

The core bet

The transfer is this big because a huge generation got unusually rich from forty years of rising asset prices, and now that generation is aging out all at once.

It's a story about rising prices, not thrift

Boomers didn't get rich by being thriftier than their kids — they owned assets while prices doubled and tripled. A $30,000 house from 1980 is worth $600,000+ today because inflation and demand did the math, not because the house improved. The transfer is big because of forty years of asset inflation — the same inflation that priced their children out.

The single biggest worry

The very asset inflation that made boomers rich locked their kids out of home ownership, and it cuts both ways: a 30–50% drop in stocks or housing could shrink the actual transfer sharply before a dime changes hands.

The surprising part

The uncomfortable link is that the windfall and the affordability crisis are the same event seen from two sides. The rising prices that built the inheritance are the rising prices the next generation can't afford — so the transfer partly just hands back, later and unevenly, wealth the same boom took from the young in the first place.

In one sentence

A generation that got rich riding forty years of asset inflation is handing off $80T+ to children who will never see that kind of price appreciation again.
