Group C · The products & the plumbing

The tax layer

Once investing itself became free, saving on taxes became the thing worth paying for.

As fees on plain investing collapsed toward zero, wealth managers moved to sell tax optimization: direct indexing (owning the individual stocks of an index so you can harvest losses), tax-loss harvesting, and asset-location strategy. The big firms raced to buy direct-indexing providers, and 'tax alpha' became the premium product for the wealthy clients inheriting money.

The core bet

When the fee for picking investments went to zero, the new thing worth paying for is saving on taxes — so 'tax alpha' (direct indexing, loss harvesting) became the premium product for wealthy clients.

The rich pay to navigate a tax code they didn't design

Tax complexity — created by Congress — became a private-wealth feature that only firms with scale, tech, and capital can profitably deliver. The wealthier you are, the more you pay someone to optimize the government's own rules, which is exactly why it became the premium service when everything else went free.

The single biggest worry

Tax alpha mostly benefits the already-wealthy with big taxable accounts, and a rule change (tighter wash-sale rules, limits on loss harvesting) could erase the value overnight. It's also genuinely hard to deliver at scale.

The surprising part

The elegant reversal is that as the core product (investing) became free, the tax around it became the thing worth charging for. The fee didn't disappear — it moved from the stock pick to the tax return, and it landed squarely on the wealthy clients who have the taxable assets to optimize.

In one sentence

As investing itself hit zero cost, wealth firms made tax optimization — direct indexing and loss harvesting — the new scarce service worth a premium fee.
