Group B · Who manages the money

The RIA roll-up

The 'independent' advisor you trust is increasingly owned by private equity.

Independent financial-advice firms (RIAs) earn recurring fees — about 1% of your assets every year — and most are run by owners in their late 50s with no clear successor. That combination made them irresistible to private equity, which is buying hundreds of them, bundling them into big platforms, and capturing decades of sticky fees just as the wealth transfer accelerates.

The core bet

Financial-advice firms earn recurring fees on assets and are run by aging owners, so bundle hundreds into big platforms and capture the wealth transfer's fee stream.

Advice priced like software

PE treats advice like a SaaS business because the 1%-of-assets fee is so predictable — high upfront valuation, sticky revenue, minimal churn if service holds. The 'independent' label persists, but the economics are now corporate, and the advisor who promised never to be owned by a big firm increasingly answers to a fund.

The single biggest worry

As funds bid against each other, multiples hit records (9–15x), so the math needs strong fee growth to work. And the same heirs-fire-the-advisor risk hangs over it: if inherited clients don't stay and add money, or younger advisors leave with their books after a deal, returns sink fast.

The surprising part

The quiet shift is in what 'independent' means. The advisor you chose precisely because they weren't a big bank is now, more often than not, part of a private-equity platform — bundled with hundreds of peers, valued like a subscription, and betting on the wealth transfer's fees.

In one sentence

Private equity is bundling hundreds of aging independent advisors into platforms to capture the trillions their clients will hand off — milking a sticky 1% fee that behaves like a subscription.
