Group B · Who manages the money

Trust & estate

A predictable tax cut set off a gifting stampede among the rich — while most families die with no plan at all.

Moving large wealth to heirs means trusts, estate planning, and tax strategy — a professional industry of estate attorneys, trust companies, and tax planners. The federal estate-tax exemption is historically high but was scheduled to roughly halve, creating urgency. The bigger and more complex the transfer, the more valuable the people and tools that move wealth down without losing it to taxes.

The core bet

The bigger the transfer and the more complex the tax rules, the more valuable the professionals and tools that move wealth down efficiently — and looming tax changes create urgency.

A scheduled law change created a stampede

A predictable tax sunset set off years of gifting and trust restructuring in advance among the wealthy — a rare case where a known future rule reshaped behavior now, and enriched estate attorneys and trust firms in the process.

The single biggest worry

Tax law is political and can change again — today's strategy can become tomorrow's closed door. And most families' plans are inadequate or missing entirely, so wealth, intent, and control routinely get lost to taxes, fees, or family conflict.

The surprising part

The revealing contrast: the machinery is elaborate and lucrative, yet most Americans (60–70%) die without any plan at all. So the estate-planning industry mainly serves the rich — the very people whose wealth is already most likely to survive the handoff intact.

In one sentence

A predictable estate-tax cut triggered a wealth-transfer gifting surge that made estate lawyers indispensable to the rich, while most families die with no plan at all.
