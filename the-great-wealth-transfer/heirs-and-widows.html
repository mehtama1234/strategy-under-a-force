Group A · The handover itself

Heirs & widows

The money moves to women first — and to younger heirs — none of whom feel loyal to the old advisor.

In the transfer, money usually moves 'horizontally' to surviving spouses (mostly women, who live longer) before it moves 'vertically' to Gen X and millennial heirs. Both groups tend to switch advisors — studies show most heirs and most widows fire the deceased's advisor. So the real race isn't to win the person who has the money today; it's to win whoever will own it next.

The core bet

The money is moving to different people than held it — women and younger heirs — who feel no loyalty to the old advisor. So the race is to win the next owner of the money, not the current one.

The wealth transfer is a stealth transfer to women

Before it flows down to kids, the money sits with widows — the largest, shortest-tenure ownership cohort in the whole transfer, and the one most likely to leave. The reason usually isn't age or wealth; it's exclusion: the advisor built the relationship with the husband and never brought the wife in, so she has no reason to stay.

The single biggest worry

Advisors built entire businesses on aging clients whose heirs and widows will mostly leave — so their most profitable relationships are, quietly, their most fragile. Reaching younger and female heirs takes a different, more digital, more inclusive approach than the one that won the parents.

The surprising part

The unsettling truth for a wealth manager is that their biggest risk isn't the market — it's their best clients dying and the money walking out the door. An industry built on client lifetime value is watching its most valuable clients reach the end of their lives with the next owner already halfway out.

In one sentence

The transfer moves from aging men to their widows and adult children — none loyal to the old advisor — forcing wealth managers to win the relationship again from zero every time money changes hands.
