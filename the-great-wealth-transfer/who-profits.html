Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

The individual advisor is the most replaceable link — the platform is the one that can't be fired.

This is the follow-the-money piece. The durable winners are the ones holding the platform and the assets when wealth transfers: the big custodians and asset managers (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, BlackRock), the PE-backed RIA aggregators, the PE-owned insurers, and the private-market managers moving into wealth. The individual advisor may lose the client; the platform keeps the money.

The core bet

The money pools with whoever owns the platform and the relationship as wealth transfers — custodians, aggregators, asset managers, insurers — not the individual advisor who may get fired.

Most of the money never really leaves the institutions

Firms are already swapping human advisors for robo and simpler models, because the advisor is replaceable and the platform is not. And most of the $84T doesn't actually move to families in any deep sense — it moves from one institution's ledger to another's, a reshuffling of who holds the assets and collects the rent.

The single biggest worry

Concentration and conflict are the same problem: when one firm advises you, custodies your assets, and sells you products, the fee conflicts multiply — and as advisor headcount shrinks, the platforms' leverage over pricing grows.

The surprising part

The clarifying insight is that the wealth transfer is mostly a transfer of fees, not of who ultimately controls the money. The front-line advisor is the fragile link; the custodian and asset manager underneath are close to unkillable, and they keep collecting whether or not the advisor survives the handoff.

In one sentence

The wealth transfer shifts who collects the fees, not who has the money — and the winners are the platforms that own the relationship and the ledger, not the advisors on the front line.
