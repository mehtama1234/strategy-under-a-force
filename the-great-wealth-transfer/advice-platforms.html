Group B · Who manages the money

Advice platforms

The 'robo revolution' didn't disrupt the giants — it became a feature they bolted on and won with.

To reach younger and smaller-balance heirs cheaply, the industry built robo-advisors (Betterment, Wealthfront) and human-plus-digital hybrids (Vanguard, Schwab). The idea: automate enough to serve profitably the heirs that human advisors can't afford — and keep them when they inherit. But the story ended with the incumbents winning by copying the startups.

The core bet

Serving smaller, younger clients profitably needs cheap automated advice, so robo and hybrid platforms are the tool to capture the heirs human advisors can't afford to serve.

What the numbers say

~$1T
Global robo-advisor assets by the mid-2020s, from near zero in 2010.
0.25% vs 1%
Robo fee versus a typical human-advisor fee — the cost gap that made small accounts viable.
$30–50B
Assets at each of the biggest pure-play robos (Betterment, Wealthfront).
hundreds of $B
Assets in incumbent hybrids (Vanguard, Schwab) — dwarfing the pure robos.
2022
Wealthfront's ~$1.4B sale to UBS was announced, then scrapped — a sign pure-robo economics were tightening.

The incumbents copied it and kept the relationship

Pure robos proved the model worked but barely made money at 0.25% fees. Then Vanguard, Schwab, and Fidelity built their own hybrids, undercut on price using their existing client base, and absorbed the market. Automation made advice cheap — but the money still flowed to whoever already owned the client relationship.

The single biggest worry

Pure robos struggled to be profitable and mostly got acquired or faded; thin fees make the unit economics fragile, and incumbents with a captive client base could always offer the same thing cheaper.

The surprising part

The lesson is that the disruption became a feature. The robo 'revolution' didn't topple the giants — it handed them a cheaper way to serve the next generation, and the startups that proved the idea largely disappeared into the balance sheets of the firms they meant to disrupt.

In one sentence

Robo-advisors were the tool to capture young heirs cheaply, but the incumbents who copied them won by owning the client relationship itself.
