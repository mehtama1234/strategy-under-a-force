Group E · The big picture

You are the product

Whoever captures and fuses the most data controls attention, credit, and increasingly governance.

Pull the thirteen pages together and one shift explains all of them. Companies you think sell search, social, cars, TVs, or credit cards are primarily collecting and monetizing data about you — profiling your behavior, location, health, and face to sell to advertisers, lenders, insurers, and governments, or to score you for pricing and access. The bait is the service; the product is you.

The one idea

You are not the customer — you are the product. Be fair, though: it's partly true and partly overstated. You are also a user, and some data use genuinely funds free, useful services. The problem is scale, opacity, and the lack of real choice — you don't know what's collected, who buys it, or how it's used to score you.

The whole force in numbers

1890s
Credit bureaus predate the internet — Equifax traces to the 1890s. Data-as-product is over a century old; tech just scaled it.
~$208B
Google's ad revenue (2023, ~81% of the total); Meta's ~$114B (~98% ads). Neither charges you, because your attention and data are the product.
~$10B
The hit to Meta from Apple's 2021 tracking toggle — proof the whole model sits on permissions a platform can revoke.
30–50B
Photos Clearview scraped to sell face-search to ~3,000 police forces — the state became a top customer of surveillance-data.
$20M
GM's earnings from selling driving data — your own car scoring you for insurers. Sensors everywhere became informants.
2023–24
California's Delete Act and the FTC's surveillance-pricing probe — the backlash arriving, unevenly.

Winners vs losers

Winners
Google, Meta, Amazon (first-party data + devices), the credit bureaus and brokers, government-surveillance vendors, and AI labs trained on scraped data.
Losers
Third-party ad networks (cookie death), indie publishers, and users — priced, scored, and tracked with little choice or recourse.
The biggest tension

The infrastructure is entangled with genuine utility — Gmail and Search are excellent and would cost money without the ad subsidy. But scale and opacity crossed a line: you don't know what's collected, who buys it, or how it feeds scoring systems that can deny you credit, jobs, or insurance. The "choice" is illusory because every major platform runs the same way.

The surprising part

Regulation is moving, but into a two-speed world: Europe and California enforce deletion and consent, while the US has no federal privacy law, so most data capture stays legal. And the biggest shock — law enforcement is one of the largest buyers. Clearview and location brokers let police bypass warrants, turning commercial surveillance infrastructure into a state-surveillance backbone the Constitution nominally forbids.

In one sentence

Data became the moat — whoever captures the most behavioral data, fuses it with scoring systems, and owns the first-party relationship controls attention, credit, and increasingly governance itself.

How this connects to the rest of the series

This is the darker root of a whole family. It's the collection engine beneath the ad economy and the loyalty economy — the same data, sold three ways. It underlies the fight for your minutes in the attention economy, and the shopper-data weapon in private label. Across all of them: the thing you use is the bait; the record of you using it is the business.
