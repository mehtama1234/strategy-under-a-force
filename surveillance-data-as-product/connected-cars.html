Group C · Sensors everywhere

Connected cars

Your own car is the informant — and it reports against you.

Modern cars collect a huge amount of data — location, driving behavior, even in-cabin details — and automakers have quietly sold or shared it. GM was caught passing drivers' hard-braking and speeding data to brokers who fed it to insurers, raising real people's premiums without their clear knowledge. Privacy researchers rate cars the worst product category they've ever reviewed.

The core bet

The bet is that the car can quietly rate how you drive and sell that score. GM proved it: it collected hard braking, speeding, and late-night trips via OnStar, then sold the data to brokers who passed it to insurers — often without drivers realizing their car was the informant.

What the numbers say

March 2024
A New York Times investigation exposed GM sharing driving behavior with brokers (LexisNexis/Verisk); drivers found rate hikes tied to data they never knowingly shared.
$20M
Roughly what GM earned selling driving data (2020–24). One driver found a 640-trip file on himself at a broker.
25/25
Mozilla (2023) tested 25 car brands — all failed on privacy. Cars can collect location, health, and even inferred intimate data.
21M+
US drivers now in telematics programs (tracking via app or vehicle), growing fast — and regulators have started penalizing the practice (GM consent order, 2025).

Safety feature or informant?

Drivers thought they were opting into safety — OnStar promised crash response and theft alerts. Buried in the fine print was that every hard brake, every speeding event, every midnight drive was collected, scored, and sold. Once the "risk score" reaches an insurer, there's no appeal: the broker owns the narrative, the insurer owns the rate.

What's risky for you
A driving score you can't see or contest, built from your own car, that can raise premiums for years.
What's valuable for them
A rich behavioral feed automakers can monetize and insurers will pay for.
The single biggest worry

Once your insurer has a driving "risk score," there's no appeal — one hard-braking event logged by your car can follow you for years, visible to underwriters before you even apply.

The surprising part

Most drivers didn't remember signing up for any of it. The shock isn't just that the car collects data — it's that the car reports against you: it isn't your device, it's the insurer's witness. A few taps of the brake, and your own vehicle quietly testifies to raise your rates.

In one sentence

Automakers and insurers built a shadow credit score out of your driving data, and most people found out only when their premiums jumped with no explanation.
