Group D · The scoring machine & the fight

Government & biometrics

The state became the biggest customer of the surveillance-data business.

A booming market sells surveillance and data analysis to governments. Palantir builds data-integration platforms for police, immigration, and militaries. Clearview AI scraped tens of billions of photos from the web to build a facial-recognition search used by thousands of police departments. This is data-as-product sold to the state — turning "you are the product" into a tool of government.

The core bet

The bet is that surveillance itself is a sellable commodity — and the government is the biggest buyer. Scrape or integrate personal data, build a searchable tool to identify and track anyone, and sell agencies the ability to use it. The customer is the state; the product is control.

What the numbers say

30–50B
Photos Clearview AI scraped from social media (2012–20) — without consent — to build a face-search engine anyone with access can query.
~3,000
US police departments that have used Clearview to search faces — available to local cops, not just federal agencies.
$50M+
Clearview's US settlement (2025), plus fines in the UK, Australia, and EU — yet the scraped databases persist.
$100B+
Palantir's valuation, on big contracts with ICE, the FBI, defense, and intelligence — the data-analysis backbone of state surveillance.

Commercial surveillance, government spine

The twist on the ad-tech model: Clearview didn't sell to advertisers — it sold government access. Police can search a face against billions of scraped photos with no warrant, no judicial oversight, and no notice to the person. Private companies did the collecting; the state became the customer, quietly outsourcing mass surveillance to firms that profit from it.

What's at stake
Warrantless face searches, immigration tracking, and false positives — mass surveillance with little oversight.
What's valuable for them
Long, lucrative government contracts for the ability to identify and track anyone.
The single biggest worry

Police can search anyone's face against billions of scraped photos — no warrant, no oversight, no notice — creating a mass-surveillance system that governments effectively rent from private companies the Constitution nominally constrains.

The surprising part

Clearview flipped the ad-tech playbook. Instead of selling influence to advertisers, it sold control to the state: a startup scraped billions of citizens' public photos (the "free product" of social media), built a surveillance tool, and sold governments a backdoor to it — turning every social-media selfie into a potential police-database ID.

In one sentence

A startup scraped billions of public faces into a searchable database police can query without warrants, then sold governments a backdoor to mass surveillance — the ultimate customer for data-as-product is the state.
