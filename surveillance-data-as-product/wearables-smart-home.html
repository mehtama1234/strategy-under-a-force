Group C · Sensors everywhere

Wearables & smart home

Cameras and microphones you paid to install in your own home.

Smart-home and wearable devices put always-on sensors inside your home and on your body. Amazon's Ring built a vast private camera network and shared footage with thousands of police departments; Alexa kept voice recordings, including children's. Google bought Fitbit for its health data. Millions of people voluntarily installed the surveillance — and paid for it.

The core bet

The bet is that people will pay to put a camera and microphone in their own home — and that the resulting video, voice, and health stream becomes a data asset. It can be shared with police, fed to ad profiles, or (with health data) used to price insurance.

What the numbers say

~2,000
Police departments Amazon Ring partnered with — officers requested doorbell footage in thousands of cases, sometimes without warrants or notifying owners.
$25M
FTC penalty (2023) over Ring privacy failures and Alexa keeping children's voice recordings even after parents asked for deletion.
~$2.1B
What Google paid for Fitbit (2021) — regulators worried it would merge your health and fitness data into its ad profile of you.
indefinitely
Amazon kept some Alexa recordings without clear limits, and contractors could access clips — the FTC forced deletion of historical child voice data.

Scope creep

Once a camera is in your home and wired to law enforcement, its use rarely stays where it started. Footage requested for a package theft can extend to trespassing, protests, or profiling a neighborhood. And a Fitbit-style device sets up a future where insurers price you on the sleep and heart-rate data you volunteered — the sensor you bought becomes the underwriter's tool.

What's risky for you
Home video and voice in a police-accessible network; health data feeding ad profiles or insurance pricing.
What's valuable for them
An intimate, always-on stream from inside homes and off bodies — bought and paid for by the surveilled.
The single biggest worry

Once a camera is in your home and tied to police, scope creep is near-inevitable — from catching a thief to tracking protesters. And merging fitness data into ad and insurance systems means the device you bought for health could be used to price you.

The surprising part

People paid to install their own surveillance — and kept buying even after the police-access and voice-retention stories broke. The $25M penalty was a rounding error for Amazon, and Ring sales rose anyway. Either the risk isn't widely understood, or the convenience simply feels worth it.

In one sentence

Consumers bought their own surveillance gear and were surprised to learn the footage belonged to a police network and an ad machine, not to them.
