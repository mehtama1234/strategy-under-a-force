Group B · You are the product

The "free" app exhaust

The flashlight app is a data-collection front.

Countless free apps — flashlights, weather, games, period trackers, fitness counters, kids' apps — exist mainly to harvest data. They ask for contacts, location, and usage, then sell it or feed ad networks through embedded code (SDKs). The advertised feature is often just the excuse to get the permissions. "If it's free, you're the product" is the literal business model.

The core bet

The bet is that a simple, useful-looking feature is a Trojan horse for permissions. The flashlight or forecast is the gateway; the contacts and location it collects are the real product, sold behind the scenes to brokers and ad networks.

What the numbers say

2021–24
FTC actions hit period-tracker Flo, weather apps, and others for sharing location/health data without meaningful consent — settlements in the tens of millions.
post-Roe
After 2022, period-tracking apps drew scrutiny — health/location data could be sold or subpoenaed, turning a tracker into a legal risk.
X-Mode
Location-harvesting SDKs embedded in hundreds of free apps (fitness, weather, games) sold precise location to brokers, funds, and even defense contractors.
COPPA
Kids' apps repeatedly broke child-privacy law by embedding tracking SDKs without parental consent — vacuuming device IDs and usage.

Permission creep

The most invasive harvesters are often the most mundane. A free flashlight can collect more precise location history than a maps app, because users grant camera and location access without reading the fine print and never suspect a simple utility is a data operation. The app maker may barely profit — the money goes to the ad networks and brokers in between.

What's risky for you
Health, location, and contact data linked to your identity via device fingerprints — then sold to insurers, police, or worse.
What's valuable for them
Cheap, high-volume data from millions of trusting users who think they downloaded a utility.
The single biggest worry

Health and location data from a "harmless" tracker can be linked to you and sold to insurers, employers, or law enforcement — turning a period tracker or fitness app into a surveillance tool with real legal and safety consequences.

The surprising part

The feature is the disguise. A free flashlight or weather app can be a data-collection business wearing a utility's clothes — and kids' apps are among the worst offenders, quietly harvesting device identifiers and usage without parents knowing. The download felt free; the payment was you.

In one sentence

Free apps are surveillance businesses disguised as utilities — the feature is the bait, and your contacts and location are the product sold behind it.
