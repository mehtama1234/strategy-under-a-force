Group C · The machinery

Why claims stay low

The profit doesn't come from denying claims — it comes from the product not breaking.

This is the margin math. Warranties are so profitable because of the 'loss ratio' — the share of premiums paid back as claims — which is far lower than real insurance. Regular insurance runs 60–80%; service contracts often run 40–50% or lower, so most of what you pay is margin. Claims stay low because products rarely fail in the window, people forget they have coverage, deductibles apply, and claims can be denied.

The core bet

A warranty is profitable because far more comes in as fees than goes out as claims — the 'loss ratio' is low, so most of your payment is margin.

The seller is betting on something that probably won't happen

If a laptop has a 2% failure rate in year one, the warranty seller pockets almost all the premium — so warranties are most profitable on reliable products. It's the opposite of health insurance, where the insurer knows someone will get sick; here the seller wins quietly when nobody breaks anything.

The single biggest worry

The low payout is exactly what draws consumer-protection heat: a warranty that returns 40–50 cents on the dollar while real insurance returns 60–80 looks — and legally is — a worse deal.

The surprising part

The clarifying insight is that denial is a lever, not the engine. The bulk of the profit comes from the product simply not failing and from people forgetting they're covered — so the business quietly roots for durability and inattention, not for breakdowns it has to pay for.

In one sentence

Warranties make money by charging you to insure something unlikely to fail in a short window, paying back less than real insurance when it does, and relying on you to forget you have it.
