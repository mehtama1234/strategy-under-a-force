Group A · The register upsell

Extended warranties

The store makes more profit on the $150 warranty you'll never use than on the $1,000 TV it covers.

When you buy a TV or laptop, the store pushes an extended warranty. These are famously high-margin: the retailer keeps a large share because claims come in far below the premium charged. For years, extended warranties were a big chunk of the actual operating profit at electronics retailers — sometimes more than the razor-thin margin on the product itself.

The core bet

Sell a protection plan priced far above the claims it will ever pay, and the plan becomes pure high-margin profit — often more than the product it covers.

On one sale, the warranty beats the product

A $900 TV at a 20% margin nets the store ~$180; a $150 warranty at a 65% margin nets ~$97 — but the customer thinks they bought insurance, and the store has more incentive to push the plan than to move the TV. On plenty of transactions, the add-on is worth more than the thing it protects.

The single biggest worry

Consumer advocates and regulators call extended warranties a bad deal, and the model depends on customers overestimating how often electronics fail — an assumption transparency erodes.

The surprising part

The reveal is a role reversal at the register: the product is the bait and the plan is the catch. A retailer whose hardware barely makes money leans on the fear of failure to sell a high-margin promise — so the sales script pushes the warranty harder than the television.

In one sentence

Retailers make more profit from a $150 warranty you'll probably never use than from the $1,000 appliance it covers.
