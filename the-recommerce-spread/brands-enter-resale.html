Group C · Why it works now

Brands enter resale

If your product gets resold anyway, run the resale yourself — and keep the margin and the data.

Brands launched their own resale programs — Patagonia's Worn Wear, Lululemon Like New, REI Used Gear, Levi's SecondHand — and a 'recommerce-as-a-service' industry (Trove, Archive, Recurate) quietly powers branded resale so the brand captures the second sale instead of losing it to a third party. It lets brands control their secondhand market, keep the customer and the data, and claim sustainability.

The core bet

If your products get resold anyway, run the resale yourself — capture that second sale, keep the customer in your ecosystem, and get a sustainability halo.

It looks like cannibalizing yourself — but it captures the relationship

A used Patagonia jacket at $60 seems to undercut a $120 new one, but most secondhand buyers would never pay full price anyway — so the real prize is that the brand now owns the relationship and the data, and can upsell later. And almost nobody realizes the resale site is powered by Trove or Archive; the brand's name hides the infrastructure.

The single biggest worry

Brand resale can cannibalize new sales, is operationally messy (processing, authenticating, managing inventory across channels), and the sustainability marketing can be thin window-dressing if little material is actually diverted from waste.

The surprising part

The strategic logic is the surprise: a brand reselling its own used goods competes with itself on paper but wins on substance — it keeps the margin that a StockX or Poshmark would have taken, keeps the customer inside its walls, and gets a green halo, all from a channel that a white-label provider runs invisibly under its logo.

In one sentence

Brands are building their own resale so they capture the secondhand margin and the customer data instead of handing both to a third-party marketplace.
