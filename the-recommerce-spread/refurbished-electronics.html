Group A · The resale marketplaces

Refurbished electronics

The phone in your drawer is a $200 asset — unlocked by grading standards, not by better repair.

Used and refurbished electronics — phones, laptops, tablets — became a big recommerce category. Back Market is a marketplace connecting refurbishers to buyers; Apple's certified-refurbished store and carrier trade-ins feed the loop. It's driven by high new-device prices, sustainability, and, above all, the grading and warranty standards that make a stranger's used phone buyable.

The core bet

Expensive devices have a long second life, so a marketplace that certifies and resells refurbished electronics can take a cut while offering buyers a cheaper, greener option.

Grading standards, not repair, unlocked the market

Refurbishment marketplaces didn't invent the second life of a phone — they built the trust. Standardized grading (battery health, screen condition) plus a warranty turned an 'unknown used phone' into a 'Grade A refurb,' and that's what convinced people to buy a stranger's device. The trick isn't fixing devices; it's grading them so the buyer believes 'Excellent' really means excellent.

The single biggest worry

Refurbishers depend on a steady flow of devices and parts, but Apple and Samsung control the parts supply and can make independent repair hard — and if the makers decide trade-in-to-landfill beats trade-in-to-reseller, the loop tightens.

The surprising part

The overlooked scale is in everyone's junk drawer. A dead phone is worth $100–200 to a refurbisher, and across hundreds of millions of households that's a $20–40B pool of untapped supply — sitting idle only because, until grading standards arrived, no stranger trusted it enough to buy.

In one sentence

A ~$50B market opened not by making phones last longer but by building grading and warranty standards that let strangers confidently buy a used device at a big discount.
