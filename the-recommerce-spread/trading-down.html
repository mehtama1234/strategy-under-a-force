Group C · Why it works now

Trading down

When new gets expensive, resale value quietly becomes a feature you pay extra for upfront.

The other big tailwind is affordability: inflation and high prices push people to buy used to save money, and rising new-item prices make resale attractive. Increasingly, a product's resale value is itself a selling point — you buy an iPhone or a Toyota partly because it holds value — and 'cost of ownership' thinking makes recommerce a permanent part of the purchase decision.

The core bet

When new is expensive, used gets attractive — so recommerce grows in tough times, and products that hold their resale value gain an edge.

Recommerce is one of the few businesses that grows in a recession

A product's resale value became a hidden feature you pay for at purchase — a brand that holds value can charge more new, because buyers know they'll recover some of it. And recessions feed the model: hard times see 15–20% more used goods listed, so sellers keep the system stocked while buyers hunt bargains, and both sides win when money is tight.

The single biggest worry

The demand can work against sellers: in downturns more people sell at once, flooding the market and depressing prices — and the whole appeal depends on the gap between used and new, which vanishes if new prices fall.

The surprising part

The subtle shift is that 'what you get back when you sell' became part of why people buy in the first place. Resale value moved from an afterthought to a spec — priced in upfront — turning recommerce from a bargain-bin fallback into a factor that shapes which new things people choose at all.

In one sentence

When new things get expensive, people buy used to save — and a product's resale value becomes part of why they bought it, making recommerce thrive precisely when times are hard.
