Group A · The resale marketplaces

Fashion resale

Your closet is free inventory — but the winners are the platforms that never touch the clothes.

Secondhand clothing went mainstream through peer-to-peer marketplaces (Poshmark, Depop, Vinted), managed consignment (ThredUp), and luxury authentication (The RealReal). Each takes a cut of every resold garment. But the economics split sharply: handling cheap used clothes at scale is brutal, and the platforms that touch the item lose money while the ones that don't get rich.

The core bet

People's closets are free inventory, so a platform that connects sellers and buyers of used clothes and takes a fee can ride the secondhand boom — cheaper and greener than new.

The lightest model wins; the value-added one loses

Peer-to-peer platforms (Vinted, Poshmark) never touch the item — the seller ships to the buyer, and the platform just skims a fee — so they turn a profit. ThredUp buys or consigns, then cleans, photographs, and ships each garment, and that last-mile cost is the killer: below ~$15 an item, it loses money. Doing more work made the business worse.

The single biggest worry

The unit economics of handling cheap used clothes are punishing — ThredUp and The RealReal lost money for years even at scale — and fast fashion (Shein, Temu) sells new so cheaply that 'used' isn't obviously cheaper or greener.

The surprising part

The counter-intuitive lesson is that touching the product is the trap. The platform that adds the most 'value' — grading, cleaning, guaranteeing — is the one that bleeds cash, while the one that does almost nothing but take a cut prints money. In resale, labor is the enemy of margin.

In one sentence

Fashion resale works for platforms that never touch the clothes and fails for those that do, because handling cheap used garments at scale costs more than the fee.
