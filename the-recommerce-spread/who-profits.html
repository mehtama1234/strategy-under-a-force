Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

The profitable recommerce firms do the least work — and in used cars, the profit is the loan.

This is the follow-the-money piece. Recommerce profit is uneven: the asset-light platforms that never touch the item win big (Vinted profitable, Poshmark sold for $1.6B, eBay), the big-ticket spread-plus-financing players win (CarMax — the profit is the loan), off-price wins (TJX, high margins), while the asset-heavy managed resellers lose money for years (ThredUp, The RealReal). The sellers get some cash and closet space; the platforms take the spread.

The core bet

The money pools with whoever takes a cut without holding the item, whoever adds financing to the spread, and whoever buys mistakes cheap — not the labor-heavy managed resellers.

Doing the least is the winning strategy

The most profitable recommerce firms do almost nothing: Vinted and Poshmark take a fee and walk; eBay just takes a cut. Meanwhile the firms that 'add value' by guaranteeing authenticity and handling logistics burn cash every year. And in used cars, the profitable players aren't really selling cars — they're packaging loans. The value isn't the goods; it's the fee, the financing, or the cheap supply.

The single biggest worry

Margins are thin and can be cyclical or fraud-prone, and the labor-heavy managed versions may simply never make money — even at $1B+ in revenue, ThredUp and RealReal can't get the unit economics to work.

The surprising part

The clean statement is that in resale, effort is inversely correlated with profit. The platform that touches nothing and merely charges a toll wins; the one that inspects, cleans, and guarantees loses; and the biggest 'used-car' profits are a lending business in disguise. Follow the money and it flows away from the goods and toward the fee and the loan.

In one sentence

Recommerce profits flow to whoever avoids touching the inventory, adds a loan to the spread, or buys mistakes in bulk — not to the companies that try to add labor and trust.
