Group A · The resale marketplaces

Sneakers & streetwear

The real product isn't the shoe — it's a 'certified real' stamp that's worth paying for.

Limited-edition sneakers and streetwear became an investable resale market, and StockX and GOAT built marketplaces where buyers and sellers trade used and deadstock pairs. The platforms don't own inventory; they authenticate each pair (verify it's not a fake) and take a fee. The scarce, valuable thing they sell isn't footwear — it's proof the shoe is real.

The core bet

The supply (used/limited sneakers) is other people's stuff, so the business is the fee on reselling them — and the scarce thing you provide is proof they're real.

You're buying the platform's credibility, not the shoe

A used pair with a StockX or GOAT 'certified authentic' tag is worth more than the identical pair from a stranger online, because you're buying the platform's ability to catch fakes and its guarantee if something's wrong. The fee is the price of sleeping at night — the authentication is the moat, and the shoe is almost incidental.

The single biggest worry

The whole model rests on the authentication being trustworthy, and it isn't unbreakable — Nike's suit alleged fakes flowed through 'authenticated' inventory, and every counterfeit that slips through chips at the one thing customers are paying for.

The surprising part

The reveal is that the sneaker is the wrapper and trust is the product. Two physically identical shoes fetch different prices depending on who vouches for them — so the business isn't footwear at all, it's a verification service that happens to ship shoes.

In one sentence

Sneaker resale became a ~$10B market where the platform's 'certified real' stamp is worth more than the shoe — and fakes are the thing that can break it.
