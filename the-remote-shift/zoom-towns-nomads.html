Group A · The remote dividend

Zoom towns & nomads — big-city salaries poured into small towns

Freed from the commute, high earners moved somewhere cheaper and nicer — and brought their salaries with them.

Once you no longer had to live near the office, why pay big-city rent? High earners moved to cheaper, nicer places — mountain towns like Bozeman and Boise, or low-tax metros like Austin and Miami. But their big-city salaries came with them, colliding with small-town wages and wrecking local affordability in a year or two.

The boomtowns

+40%
Bozeman, Montana home prices in a single year (2020–21) as remote workers arrived from higher-cost cities. Boise's median home jumped from ~$285k to ~$420k.
  • Austin grew ~2.3% a year; its median price roughly doubled toward $500k+, as tech workers earning $120–150k met a local median wage nearer $50k.
  • Digital nomad visas: 50+ countries launched them (2020–23) — Barbados, Portugal, Croatia, Estonia — competing to attract remote workers.
  • Airbnb pivoted hard toward long-term stays as nomads settled in.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — a new gentrification

A remote worker earning $120k moves to a town where locals earn $45k and outbids everyone on housing. This isn't slow, investment-driven gentrification — it's wage arbitrage collapsing a local market in under two years, faster than anyone can build.

The surprising part

Countries thought they were importing a glamorous "global nomad community." What they actually did was export high salaries into places with low wages. When a US remote worker earning $3k/month lands in a country where locals earn $800, rents inflate instantly and locals can't compete with foreign income in their own town. The nomad visa wasn't cultural exchange so much as wage-dumping dressed up as talent policy — countries treating remote workers like a natural resource to be mined.

In one sentence

Zoom towns & nomads in a nutshell

Remote work let high earners take big-city salaries to cheaper towns and countries — a windfall for them, but a fast, brutal affordability crisis for the locals they outbid.
