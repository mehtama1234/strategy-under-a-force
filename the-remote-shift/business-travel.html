Group B · Built on the commute

Business travel — video calls killed the routine trip

The total dollars came back. The most profitable kind of trip did not.

Once everyone was fluent in video calls, the routine sales visit and the internal team fly-in became a lot harder to justify — "this could have been a video call." Total business-travel spending eventually recovered, but the mix changed, and the change hurt airlines where it counts most.

Recovered dollars, changed shape

~17–18%
Share of corporate trips companies say video calls now replace (2023–24) — a structural, not temporary, shift. Overall business-travel spending still climbed back toward 2019 levels by 2024.

But the recovery leaned on conferences and "bleisure" (adding leisure days to a work trip) rather than the old base of high-frequency routine trips.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the subsidy is gone

Business travelers pay the expensive, last-minute fares that quietly subsidize everyone's cheap coach seat. Lose a chunk of that dense, predictable, high-margin flying and airlines lose their most reliable profit — leaving a more volatile, event-driven, leisure-dependent model in its place.

The surprising part

The dollars bounced back, but the composition inverted. The old model was a steady base of routine short-haul business trips, topped with a few big conferences. The new one flips that: airlines now sell premium leisure and bleisure as the anchor, and conferences are the bonus. So the passengers airlines cater to, and the way they price, quietly reorganized around vacationers who'll splurge on a nice seat — not the road warrior who flew every week.

In one sentence

Business travel in a nutshell

The remote shift didn't kill business-travel spending, but it killed the high-frequency, high-margin routine trip that subsidized everyone's cheap seat — forcing airlines to rebuild around premium leisure instead.
