Group C · Work, reorganized

Global talent & hiring — geography stopped binding who you hire

If the job is remote, a company can hire from anywhere — and you compete with everyone.

Once work is remote, geography stops binding hiring. A company can hire the right person wherever they live — and, for the first time in decades, a knowledge worker's biggest career advantage (living in an expensive tech hub) partly dissolved. The talent pool went global overnight, and so did the competition for every job.

The plumbing that made it possible

$12B
Peak valuation of Deel (2022), up from ~$300M a year earlier — one of the "employer of record" firms that lets a company legally hire and pay someone in another country. Deel and Remote.com reached 100+ countries.
  • Remote job listings jumped from ~2% of postings (2019) to ~25% (2023) in tech, finance, and marketing.
  • The pay fight: should you pay by the worker's location or by the role? A San Francisco engineer at $180–220k vs. an equally good one abroad at $70–100k.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — permanent wage pressure

If talent is truly global, a company can nearly always find someone cheaper who can do the job — so knowledge workers face steady downward pressure on pay, with no geographic buffer left, only their actual skill.

The surprising part

Remote work didn't liberate workers evenly — it stratified them. Big-name firms (Google, Stripe) can now hire the best from anywhere; smaller firms compete on wage alone. Meanwhile the worker who once thought "I'll move to New York for the tech job" found the job was no longer tied to the city — and neither was the salary. A plumber still can't be outsourced. But a programmer in Ohio now competes head-to-head with one in Bangalore, on equal footing, for the first time in 30 years.

In one sentence

Global talent & hiring in a nutshell

Remote work dissolved geography as a career moat — making talent truly global but pay locally volatile, so companies can hire anywhere and workers now compete with everyone.
