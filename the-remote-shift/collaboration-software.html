Group A · The remote dividend

Collaboration software — the tools became the office overnight

Zoom, Slack, and Teams went from nice-to-have to the workplace itself in weeks. Then the emergency ended.

When offices shut in March 2020, every knowledge worker became a remote worker who suddenly needed to video-call, chat, and share files. The software that could do it at massive scale went from a nice-to-have to a survival tool overnight. Zoom, Slack, and Microsoft Teams became the office — and then discovered that being essential isn't the same as being safe.

The overnight boom

10M → 300M
Zoom's daily meeting participants, in about two months (spring 2020). It became a verb. Microsoft Teams passed ~300 million users; Slack roughly 8× its daily users.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — essential isn't defensible

Zoom's stock peaked around $559 in late 2021, then crashed ~65% within 18 months. The problem: once the hard part (great video at scale) was solved, "good enough" video from Microsoft — bundled free into Office — was hard to beat. Being indispensable to remote work didn't make it a durable business.

The surprising part

The twist isn't that remote work slowed — it's that the tools worked too well and became utilities. Zoom solved video conferencing so completely that it turned into a commodity feature, and a giant (Microsoft) simply gave an equivalent away for free inside a suite everyone already paid for. Slack's team-chat network effect survived better, but Teams still spread by default. The tools brilliantly proved remote work was possible — and mostly failed to turn that into defensible revenue.

In one sentence

Collaboration software in a nutshell

The tools became the office overnight in 2020 — then learned that being essential to remote work wasn't a moat, as normalization cooled demand and Microsoft bundled the same features free.
