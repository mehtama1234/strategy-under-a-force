Group B · Built on the commute

Office towers — half-empty buildings meet expensive debt

The biggest single loser of the remote shift — and its crash arrives in slow motion.

Remote work permanently shrank how much office space companies need — attendance fell, and firms cut floor space on renewal. That flooded the market with emptiness, right as the cheap loans that built these towers came due at much higher rates. The result is a slow, grinding crisis in downtown commercial real estate.

The numbers

~20%
US office vacancy hit a record ~20% by end-2024 (versus a healthy 5–8%). Office values have fallen roughly 37% since 2019; 600+ buildings sold at 50%+ discounts.
~$1.3T
Office loans coming due, peaking around 2027 — refinancing at ~6.5–7% on buildings worth far less. Much of this debt sits with regional banks (see the cheap-money collection).

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the refinancing wall

When the 2027 wave of loans has to refinance at high rates on properties worth ~37% less, many simply can't — owners walk away, lenders foreclose, and losses land on the (often regional) banks that hold them.

The surprising part

It's a crash in slow motion, not a single bad day. Long leases keep the rent flowing — a company keeps paying for a floor it isn't using until its 10-year lease expires, which hides the losses and spreads the pain across years. And the obvious fix — "just convert the towers to apartments" — turns out to be impossible for most of them: office floors are too deep (no windows in the middle), the plumbing is wrong, and conversion can cost more than the building is worth. So you get a decade-long zombie walk: office is dead, but it keeps paying rent and taking up space.

In one sentence

Office towers in a nutshell

Half-empty at record vacancy, worth ~37% less, and carrying expensive debt they can't refinance, downtown office towers are in a slow-motion crash — and most can't be converted to housing to escape it.
