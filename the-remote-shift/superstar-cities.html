Superstar cities — the first crack in 40 years of coastal concentration

For four decades, talent and money piled into a few expensive coastal cities. Remote work partly reversed it.

For 40 years, talent and wealth concentrated relentlessly in a handful of expensive "superstar" coastal cities — San Francisco and New York above all. Remote work cracked that lock. When you could do the job from anywhere, people (and some companies) moved to cheaper, sunnier, lower-tax places — and took their incomes with them.

The dispersal

−$484M
Hit to San Francisco's business tax haul in one year (2023) from remote work — about a third of one key tax. Its property-transfer tax fell to a 12-year low.
  • The Sun Belt boomed: Austin employment up ~17% since 2020; Nashville, Phoenix, and Miami among the top destinations for company relocations.
  • The wealth spread too: emerging hubs like Scottsdale and West Palm Beach grew ~10× faster than traditional coastal cities over the past decade.
  • Movers took their taxes, denting the budgets of the high-cost cities they left.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Coastal superstar cities face structural tax-base erosion. Once workers have tasted mobility, a return-to-office mandate can push them to resettle remotely and take their tax dollars for good — a fragile position for a city that depends on a concentrated, high-income base.

The surprising part

This is the first real crack since the 1980s in the "winner-take-all city" story — highly educated workers no longer need to live in SF or NY to do tech or finance work. But the reversal is partial and uneven. Tech's 2025–26 return-to-office mandates are pulling some talent back, and New York's rental vacancy stays tight despite its cost — younger workers still choose coastal cities for the amenities and networks. The magnet got weaker, not broken: the wealthy coast is still wealthy, just visibly less inevitable.

In one sentence

Superstar cities in a nutshell

Remote work cracked the 40-year monopoly of expensive coastal superstar cities by letting talent and wealth disperse to cheaper Sun Belt places — a real reversal, though return-to-office is now pulling some of it back.
