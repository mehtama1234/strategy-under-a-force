Group C · The debt bombs

Zombie companies — the reckoning cheap money postponed

Thousands of firms don't earn enough to cover their interest. Cheap money kept them alive.

A "zombie" company doesn't make enough profit to cover even the interest on its debt. It should have failed years ago — but near-zero rates let it keep rolling over its loans cheaply and limping on. Higher rates threaten to finish the job.

Why they're now in danger

Cheap money was a hidden life-support machine. As long as a barely-alive company could refinance at ~4%, it survived. Now, when that old debt matures, it must refinance at painful rates — or default.

~17%
Of listed companies in rich economies are now counted as "zombies" — up from about 4% in the late 1980s. That's roughly 2,000 US public firms.
$3T+
Corporate debt due to be refinanced in the coming years — the "maturity wall." Corporate bankruptcies already hit their highest since 2010.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

A slow avalanche of defaults and bankruptcies as tranches of cheap debt mature and can't be rolled over cheaply. Companies cut jobs, sell assets at fire-sale prices, or fold. It's the "economic cleansing" that cheap money had postponed for 15 years — now overdue and arriving.

The surprising part

Cheap money wasn't just a nice tailwind — it was a hidden subsidy that kept thousands of unviable companies on life support, hiding the rot in the real economy. Higher rates aren't really a "shock" to these firms; they're the reckoning that was always overdue. The end of cheap money didn't create the weakness — it just stopped hiding it.

In one sentence

Zombie companies in a nutshell

Near-zero rates kept thousands of firms that can't cover their own interest alive; higher rates and a wall of maturing debt are now forcing the wave of failures that cheap money postponed.
