The patterns

Line up everyone the rate shock touched, and one theme dominates: cheap money was a hidden subsidy for risk — and its end revealed who was swimming naked.

Each page asks one question: what did the end of cheap money make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions keep showing up. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

Buffett said it best: "only when the tide goes out do you learn who's been swimming naked." Cheap money was the tide. For 15 years it lifted everything — zombies, speculation, leverage, profitless growth — and hid who was weak. Higher rates pulled the tide out. Almost nothing here is a new problem; it's an old weakness suddenly made visible.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

Being paid to be patient is back

For 15 years, holding cash or earning a steady profit felt like a mistake — you were "missing out." Higher rates flipped that: a safe 5% now rewards the patient and the profitable. The boring winners of this whole collection were simply the ones with cash and real earnings.

Pattern 02

Gravity switched back on — far-off payoffs got crushed

When money is free, a maybe-someday payoff looks great. When money pays 5% now, a promise of profit years away is worth much less. So the market repriced anything whose value lived in the distant future — money-losing tech, speculative bets, "growth at any cost."

Pattern 03

The "genius" was really just leverage

Some businesses looked brilliant for a decade. Strip away cheap borrowed money and the brilliance fades — it was a leveraged bet on rates staying low, not operational skill. When the borrowing got expensive, the magic disappeared.

private equitySPACs
Pattern 04

The "safe" thing became the dangerous thing

The cruelest twist. A bank buys the safest asset there is — government bonds — and higher rates turn them into hidden losses that kill it. A homeowner's cheap 3% mortgage, the smartest move they ever made, becomes the golden handcuff that traps them.

regional banks & SVBhousing
Pattern 05

The bomb goes off at refinancing

Higher rates don't hurt right away — old cheap debt keeps its old rate. The damage lands when that debt matures and has to be rolled over at today's rates. That "maturity wall" is the fuse under offices, zombie firms, and even the government's own budget.

Pattern 06

Higher rates froze things instead of crashing them

Everyone expected a crash. Often they got a freeze. Homeowners stopped selling, so housing seized up and prices rose. Private equity couldn't sell its companies, so cash to investors dried up. A frozen market can hurt as much as a falling one — it just hurts more quietly.

housingprivate equity
Pattern 07

The biggest borrower of all was the government

Everyone watched companies and households pile on cheap debt. The largest borrower was the US Treasury — and its interest bill tripled past the entire defense budget. The end of cheap money's single biggest casualty isn't a company. It's the nation's balance sheet.

the interest bill
Pattern 08

The end didn't cause the weakness — it revealed it

Zombie firms couldn't cover their interest for years; cheap money just hid it. Profitless companies were always burning cash; free money just funded it. Higher rates aren't the disease — they're the light switch that finally showed the rot that was always there.

Four forces, one method

The point of doing all four

This is the fourth collection built the same way — after AI, Ozempic, and the decoupling. Each force has its own signature: AI's is "you can only trust what you can verify," Ozempic's is "one force, many industries," the decoupling's is "the policy backfires and the walls leak," and cheap money's is "the tide went out and revealed who was swimming naked." But the method is identical: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group companies by their answer, and read the patterns. Point it at anything big and the stories fall out.
