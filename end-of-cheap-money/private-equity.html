Group C · The debt bombs

Private equity — the "genius" that was a bet on cheap debt

Buy companies with borrowed money, sell them higher. Both halves broke when rates rose.

Private equity firms buy companies using lots of borrowed money, spruce them up, and sell them for a profit. It looked like financial genius for 15 years. Higher rates exposed how much of it was simply a bet on cheap debt.

Both halves of the model broke

  • Buying got expensive. The cost of the debt PE runs on roughly doubled (from ~4% to ~8%). Leveraged buyouts stopped penciling out.
  • Selling got impossible. With deals frozen and IPOs dead, firms can't sell the companies they own — so they can't return cash to their investors. The "exit drought."
~$2.3T
"Dry powder" — money raised but sitting unused because deals don't work. Meanwhile 30,000+ unsold companies (~$4 trillion) pile up.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — pensions can't get paid back

The whole model needs cheap debt and easy exits — both gone. Payouts to investors fell to the lowest in over a decade, so pension funds and endowments aren't getting their money back. Firms are now borrowing more (NAV loans, ~$94B in a year) just to fund payouts — debt to pay debt.

The surprising part

For years PE managers were celebrated as masters of value creation. Higher rates revealed the uncomfortable truth: much of that "value" was a leveraged bet on cheap money and rising markets, not operational genius. Pension funds are waking up to the fact that their "diversification" into private equity was really a highly leveraged, hard-to-exit bet on rates staying low — and they're stuck in it.

In one sentence

Private equity in a nutshell

Higher rates made its debt expensive and its exits impossible, freezing the model and starving investors of cash — revealing that its "genius" leaned heavily on money being free.
