Group E · The big picture

The end of cheap money — the whole force in one page

For 15 years, money was almost free. Then it wasn't. That one change reshuffled everything.

From 2009 to 2021, interest rates sat near zero. Borrowing was almost free. Then, in 18 months, the US central bank raised rates from near-zero to over 5% — the fastest jump in 40 years. This page is the whole force in one place: what changed, and why it touched every company in this collection.

The one idea: money got a price again

Buffett's gravity

Warren Buffett calls interest rates "gravity" for the value of everything. When rates are near zero, gravity is weak — money floats toward anything that might pay off someday: money-losing startups, crypto, far-off profits, risky bets. When rates rise, gravity turns back on. Suddenly a safe 5% in the bank beats a maybe-someday payoff, and money rushes back to things that pay cash now.

Who won, who lost

Won

Savers, banks, insurers, and anyone sitting on cash or earning profit today. Being paid to be patient is a reward that vanished for 15 years and came back.

Lost

Speculation, money-losing growth, heavy borrowers, and long, far-off bets — anything that only worked because money was free.

The hidden subsidy nobody named

Here's the thread running through every page in this collection: cheap money was a giant hidden subsidy for risk-taking. It quietly propped up zombie companies, profitless tech, leveraged buyouts, SPACs, and speculative property. Nobody called it a subsidy because it was invisible — it was just "the way things are." Ending it didn't cause the weakness in all these places; it simply stopped hiding it.

How much rates rose in about 18 months (2022–23) — the fastest tightening in four decades. Then "higher for longer."

The surprising part

The astonishing thing is how little had to change to reshuffle so much. The central bank didn't invent a new technology or pass a new law. It just made borrowing cost something again. That single price — the cost of money — turns out to sit underneath banks, startups, offices, houses, pensions, and the government's own budget. Change it, and the whole board rearranges within months.

The open question

What we don't know yet

Is this the new normal, or a detour? If rates stay high, the discipline (and the pain — defaults, frozen markets, a trillion-dollar government interest bill) is permanent. If they drift back down, some of the old behavior returns. Everything in this collection hangs on that one unanswered question.

The end of cheap money in a nutshell
