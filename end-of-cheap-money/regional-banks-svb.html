Group C · The debt bombs

Regional banks & SVB — killed by the safest asset

Silicon Valley Bank did the "safe" thing — buy government bonds. Higher rates made that fatal.

Not every bank won from higher rates (see big banks). Some got destroyed. Silicon Valley Bank took its depositors' cash and parked it in long-term government bonds when rates were near zero. When rates jumped, those bonds lost value — and when depositors realized it, the bank died in 48 hours.

The mechanism, in plain terms

Why safe bonds became a bomb

A bond paying 3% is worth a lot less once new bonds pay 5% — why buy the old one at full price? So SVB's pile of "safe" bonds was quietly underwater. It had to sell some at a big loss to raise cash, which spooked its depositors — who then yanked $42 billion in a single day through their phones. A bank can be technically solvent but still die if everyone wants their money at once.

48 hours
From trouble to seizure (March 2023). Signature Bank failed the same day; First Republic ($229B) followed weeks later.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Many regional banks still hold underwater bonds (the US banking system had ~$600B+ of these paper losses) and heavy commercial-real-estate loans. And deposits are no longer "sticky" — a rumor on social media or an app tap can drain a bank in hours, not days.

The surprising part

The cruel paradox: the "safest" asset a bank can own — government bonds — became the thing that killed it, because rising rates make even safe bonds lose value. And the speed was new: in 2008, bank runs took months; SVB's took 8 hours, because depositors could move money by app and coordinate by group chat. It was the first true internet-speed bank run — rules built for 1990s banking couldn't keep up.

In one sentence

Regional banks & SVB in a nutshell

Higher rates turned banks' "safe" government bonds into hidden losses, and in the app era a spooked crowd drained SVB in hours — proof the same force that enriched big banks could kill smaller ones.
