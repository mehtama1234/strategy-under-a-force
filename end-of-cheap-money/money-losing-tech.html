Group B · Built on free money

Money-losing tech — "grow at all costs" died overnight

When money was free, burning cash for growth was genius. When it wasn't, Wall Street wanted profit — now.

In the free-money years, investors rewarded tech companies for growing at all costs — grab users and market share, worry about profit later. Higher rates flipped that rule to its opposite: show me the money now.

The great cost-cut

When rates rose, investors stopped tolerating losses. The result was a wave of tech layoffs: in a single month (January 2023), Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta announced roughly 50,000 cuts between them; the year totaled over a quarter of a million tech jobs.

Meta's stock gain in 2023 — driven not by a new product but by its "Year of Efficiency" layoffs and cost cuts. Wall Street cheered discipline over dreams.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Cheaper capital funded experimentation and moonshots — the risky, far-off bets that occasionally become the next big thing. When every dollar has to justify itself now, some of that dies in the cradle. The discipline can go too far and quietly strangle the next breakthrough.

The surprising part

The companies that got huge by burning cash got their biggest stock rewards from cutting it. Zuckerberg went from "grow no matter what" to efficiency hero, and Meta's value tripled off layoffs, not launches. The narrative flipped completely: growth-at-all-costs became the liability, and — as one way to put it — profit became the shiniest new product.

In one sentence

Money-losing tech in a nutshell

Higher rates ended the "grow at any cost" era and forced profitability, triggering mass layoffs — and rewarding the companies that cut hardest, with the risk that discipline smothers the next moonshot.
