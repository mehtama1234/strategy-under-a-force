Group A · Cash is king again

Big banks — higher rates handed them a goldmine

The thing that hurt almost everyone else was a pure gift to the big banks.

Banks make money on a simple gap: they charge more on loans than they pay you on deposits. For years of near-zero rates, that gap was squeezed thin. When rates jumped, the gap blew wide open — and JPMorgan and the big banks minted record profits.

The windfall

$49.6B
JPMorgan's record profit in 2023 (then $58.5B in 2024) — as higher rates lifted what it earned on loans faster than what it paid savers.

Why: when rates rose, banks quickly charged more on loans but were slow to raise what they paid depositors — so the gap (their "net interest income") ballooned.

The main worry — the party's ending

The single biggest risk

Three clouds: savers are now demanding higher rates on their deposits (squeezing the gap back); banks face commercial-real-estate loan losses (empty offices — see the debt-bombs pages); and now that the Fed is cutting rates again, the fat margin shrinks. The 18-month goldmine is closing.

The surprising part

Higher rates crushed almost everyone — mortgages, startups, offices, stocks. For the big banks, they were the opposite: a straight gift. The exact same force that broke Silicon Valley Bank (see next door) enriched JPMorgan — the difference was simply who was big and diversified enough to ride it, and who was caught holding the wrong bet.

In one sentence

Big banks in a nutshell

Higher rates widened the gap between what they charge and what they pay into record profits — a rare pure winner of the end of cheap money, now facing a shrinking margin as rates fall again.
