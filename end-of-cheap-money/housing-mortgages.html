Group D · The government & housing

Housing — the market that froze instead of crashing

Everyone expected higher rates to crash home prices. They did the opposite.

Mortgage rates jumped from about 3% to about 7%. Everyone braced for a price crash. Instead the market froze — and prices went up. Here's the strange mechanism behind it.

The "lock-in effect"

Why nobody sells

Millions of people locked in a 3% mortgage during the cheap-money years. To move, they'd have to give up that 3% loan and take a 7% one — often doubling their monthly payment for the same house. So they just… stay put. That dries up the supply of homes for sale.

~30-year low
Existing-home sales fell to roughly 4 million a year — the slowest since the mid-1990s. The lock-in effect alone blocked well over a million sales.

The surprising result

With almost no homes for sale, the few buyers left still competed over scarce supply — so prices rose about 5.7% instead of falling. Higher rates hurt affordability, but the supply freeze more than made up for it. A crash needs forced sellers; lock-in meant there were almost none.

How homebuilders adapted

New-home builders (like D.R. Horton and Lennar) had a clever escape. Since resales were frozen, buyers came to them — and they used their cash to "buy down" the mortgage rate for customers (a 3-2-1 buydown), effectively paying part of the interest. It cost about $47,000 per house, and around 73% of buyers took it. Builders quietly became mortgage subsidizers to keep selling.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

A permanently stuck market: high prices and high rates together lock out young and first-time buyers, freezing a generation out of ownership. It only truly thaws if rates fall a lot — and "higher for longer" says don't count on it soon.

In one sentence

Housing in a nutshell

Higher rates didn't crash home prices — they froze the market, because owners clinging to 3% loans stopped selling, choking supply and pushing prices up even as buying got unaffordable.
