Group A · Cash is king again

Insurers & Berkshire — the mocked cash pile became a money machine

For years critics said Buffett's giant cash hoard was a drag. Then rates rose.

Some companies sit on mountains of cash — insurers hold "float" (premiums collected before claims are paid), and Berkshire Hathaway famously hoards tens of billions. For 15 years that cash earned almost nothing. Higher rates flipped it into a money machine.

The turn

~$380B+
Berkshire's cash pile hit a record — and at ~4–5% it throws off well over $15 billion a year in safe interest, roughly double what it earned before rates rose.
$89B
Record investment income for US property-casualty insurers in a single year — as premiums finally get reinvested into bonds that actually pay.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

If rates fall again, the easy interest income shrinks. And a giant cash pile is also a quiet signal that Buffett sees few things worth buying at today's prices — patience, not opportunity. Insurers also still face catastrophe claims and losses on the old low-rate bonds they're stuck with.

The surprising part

Patience literally paid

For years, critics mocked Buffett's cash hoard as a sign he'd lost his touch — "why sit on all that dead money?" The moment rates rose, that "dead money" started printing billions. He'd been waiting for exactly this: a world where not buying anything was as profitable as buying. The most-criticized position became the winning one.

In one sentence

Insurers & Berkshire in a nutshell

Higher rates turned huge cash and insurance float from a drag into a money machine — vindicating the patient and the cash-rich, at least until rates fall again.
