Group C · The China side

Shein & Temu — a giant built on a loophole that just closed

Their whole edge was a customs technicality. The government deleted it.

Shein and Temu built multibillion-dollar empires shipping ultra-cheap clothes and gadgets straight from Chinese factories to your door. The secret wasn't just low prices — it was a customs rule called "de minimis."

The loophole

Why they were unbeatable

A US rule let packages worth under $800 enter duty-free. Shein and Temu shipped tiny parcels directly to shoppers — no US warehouses, no import taxes, no middlemen. That's how a dress could cost a few dollars. It wasn't a clever business moat; it was regulatory arbitrage — profiting from a rule that could vanish.

Then it vanished

In 2025 the US ended the duty-free rule for China, then for the whole world, and piled on tariffs. Overnight, the cost advantage evaporated.

−52%
Temu's US daily users dropped by half. Shein's US sales fell, it swung to a loss, and its planned stock listing was derailed.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the edge is gone for good

Cheap + duty-free was the business. Now they have to build US warehouses, use local sellers, and pay tariffs — a slower, costlier model that may never be as profitable. Their biggest market turned hostile almost overnight.

The surprising part

A $10-billion-plus industry rested on a single customs technicality. Two of the world's biggest retailers had almost zero resilience to tariffs, because their whole model — the speed, the price, the scale — depended on one rule that was always politically vulnerable. When the government finally closed it, they nearly collapsed in America. That's not a moat; it's a loophole.

In one sentence

Shein & Temu in a nutshell

Their unbeatable cheapness came from a duty-free loophole, not a real advantage — so when the US closed it, half their US users vanished and the model broke.
