The patterns

Line up everyone the US–China split touched, and one theme dominates: the policy keeps backfiring, and the walls leak.

Each page asks one question: what did the great unwinding of cheap trade make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company or country? Thirteen answers later, the same tensions keep showing up. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

Tariffs and controls are blunt tools aimed at a moving target. Again and again they hit the wrong company, get dodged, or backfire — because global supply chains don't cut cleanly. This whole collection is really a study in unintended consequences.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

The tariff boomerang — it hits the wrong target

Meant to punish China, the tariffs keep landing on the countries goods moved to (India got a 50% tariff), or on industries that can't move at all. The policy trips over its own goal.

IndiaMexicoNike
Pattern 02

If you can't ship in, build inside the walls

Block the imports and the maker just builds a factory inside your borders. BYD is opening plants in the countries walling it out; Chinese firms used Mexico as a backdoor. The wall relocates the thing it was meant to stop.

BYDMexico
Pattern 03

Cutting them off makes them scrappy — the backfire

Deny a rival the best tools and they get creative. Export controls meant to slow China's AI helped produce cheap, home-grown AI (DeepSeek) and sped up its drive for self-reliance.

Alibaba & Chinese tech
Pattern 04

The efficiency that built you becomes the liability

The tight, cheap, China-centric machine that made a company great is exactly what decoupling punishes. Yesterday's masterstroke is today's exposure.

Applebig-box retail
Pattern 05

You can't bring it home if there's no home

Tariffs are meant to reshore production — but if there's no domestic industry to move to (no US sneaker factories), or it costs far more (chips), the goods can't actually come back. You just tax them abroad.

NikeUS chipmaking
Pattern 06

A business built on a loophole is a business built on sand

When your edge is a rule rather than a real advantage, it vanishes when the rule does. A duty-free customs loophole was the whole model — until the government deleted it.

Shein & Temu
Pattern 07

The quiet physical chokepoint beats the headline

Everyone watches tariffs and chips, but the sharpest weapon was a few obscure minerals. Whoever controls the unglamorous middle step — processing — holds more leverage than the loud fights suggest.

rare earths
Pattern 08

Chaos pays — for the pick-and-shovel players

Some businesses don't care who wins; they profit from the churn itself. Shipping earns more from disruption than calm; the electrical-gear makers boom on all the rebuilding.

shippingthe electricians
Pattern 09

Security costs money — and the walls leak anyway

Decoupling buys resilience with higher prices and duplicated supply chains. But true separation is nearly impossible: block the front door and trade just reroutes through a third country. Expensive and leaky.

Three forces, one method

The point of doing all three

This is the third collection built the same way — after AI and Ozempic. Each force has its own signature: AI's is "you can only trust what you can verify," Ozempic's is "one force, many industries," and the decoupling's is "the policy backfires and the walls leak." But the method is identical: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group companies by their answer, and read the patterns. Point it at anything big and the stories fall out.
