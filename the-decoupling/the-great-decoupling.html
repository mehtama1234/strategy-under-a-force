Group E · The money & the country

The great decoupling — ending cheap trade, and who pays

The whole reshuffle in one place: a deliberate trade of lower prices for more security.

Step back from the individual companies and here's the big picture. For 30 years, the rule was simple: make it wherever it's cheapest — usually China. The US and China are now deliberately untangling their economies for security reasons, and everyone pays a price for the reshuffle.

The shift

From "cheapest is best" to "secure is best"

The new goal is "friend-shoring" — make things at home or with trusted partners, even if it costs more. The tools: tariffs (as high as 145% on some Chinese goods), export controls (no advanced chips to China), and subsidies (tens of billions for chips and clean energy). It's bipartisan in the US — and China is doing the mirror image, pushing hard for self-reliance.

Winners and losers

  • Winners: "connector" countries in the middle — Vietnam, Mexico, India — and industries being brought home.
  • Losers: shoppers (higher prices), efficiency (duplicated supply chains), and companies caught between the two giants.

The main tension

Security costs money

This is the honest trade-off at the heart of it all: resilience isn't free. Decoupling means higher prices, two of everything, and slower growth — in exchange for being less dependent on a rival. It is, quite deliberately, a little bit inflationary. You're buying insurance, and insurance has a premium.

The surprising part

Two twists. First, full separation is nearly impossible — block direct China–US trade and the goods just reroute through Vietnam or Mexico (America's reliance on Chinese-made value barely fell). It's less a divorce than a reshuffle. Second, China is also "winning" in its own way: the controls meant to slow it down accelerated its push to make its own chips and AI. Both sides are paying to build walls, and the walls are leakier than they look.

In one sentence

The great decoupling in a nutshell

The world is trading 30 years of cheap trade for security — accepting higher prices and duplicated supply chains to depend less on a rival — but true separation is nearly impossible, so it's really a costly, leaky reshuffle.
