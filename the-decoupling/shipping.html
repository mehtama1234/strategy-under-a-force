Shipping — the industry that profits from the chaos

You'd think decoupling would hurt the companies that carry globalization. The opposite happened.

Container ships carry about 97% of the world's traded goods, so companies like Maersk live and die on globalization. You'd expect tariffs and decoupling — which shrink trade — to hurt them. Strangely, the chaos has been good business.

A wild few years

  • Red Sea attacks forced ships to detour all the way around Africa — spiking rates several times over.
  • Tariff rushes: when a big tariff is announced, importers scramble to ship everything before the deadline — cramming the whole import calendar into a few frantic weeks and sending rates soaring, then crashing.
  • Route reshuffle: less China–US traffic, more via Vietnam, Mexico, and elsewhere — the map of trade is being redrawn.

The strange result

Volume down, revenue up

Here's the inversion: China–US shipping volumes fell about 30%, yet freight rates stayed well above normal — so Maersk cut its volume forecast and raised its profit outlook in the same breath. When trade is chaotic and unpredictable, no carrier can meet the sudden surges at normal prices — and prices spike. Chaos pays more than calm.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Whipsaw volatility meets a glut of new ships. Carriers ordered huge amounts of new capacity right as demand fragments — so if the chaos ever settles into a calm, shrinking, over-supplied market, the party ends fast. They're betting disruption keeps paying.

The surprising part

Shipping's business model quietly became disruption itself, not efficiency. The industry built to move goods smoothly now profits most when things go wrong — reroutes, tariff stampedes, scheduling chaos. Decoupling and disorder, once seen as a threat to shipping, now guarantee its profits.

In one sentence

Shipping in a nutshell

It carries globalization, yet profits from globalization breaking — tariffs, war, and reroutes spike freight rates so much that volumes can fall while revenue rises. The risk: a flood of new ships if the chaos ever calms.
