Group A · Reshoring winners

US chipmaking — $52 billion to build fabs at home

Depending on Taiwan for the world's best chips looked too risky. Fixing that is slow and expensive.

Almost all the world's most advanced computer chips are made in Taiwan — an island China claims, in an earthquake zone. The US decided that dependence is a national-security risk, and is spending tens of billions to build chip factories on American soil.

The bet

$52B
Government subsidies (the "CHIPS Act") to lure chipmakers to build in the US. TSMC, Intel, Micron, and Samsung all took the money.
~$165B
TSMC's planned US investment (Arizona) — a huge vote of confidence. Some American-made chips are now rolling off the line.

The catch: it costs more, and it's hard

The single biggest risk

Making chips in the US costs roughly 10–50% more than in Taiwan and takes longer to build. Execution is hard: Intel, the biggest bet, delayed its Ohio plant by years. And the very best chips will still come from Taiwan for years to come — so the dependence isn't going away soon.

The surprising part

The government bought a piece of Intel

In 2025, the US government took a ~10% ownership stake in Intel — roughly $9 billion. That's almost unheard of: the state becoming a shareholder in a struggling private tech company. It signals how badly the US wants home-grown chips — and how shaky its national champion is. If Intel stumbles, taxpayers are directly on the hook.

The deeper worry

The whole plan assumes subsidies are a temporary bridge — that US factories will eventually be cheap enough to stand on their own. If they never are, the country is locked into paying forever just to make chips at home. Taiwan's fabs are profitable without help; America's may not be.

In one sentence

US chipmaking in a nutshell

A national-security bet worth tens of billions to stop depending on Taiwan — real factories are rising, but they cost more, Intel is stumbling, and the leading edge stays in Taiwan for years.
