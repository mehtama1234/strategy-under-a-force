Group A · Losing the drinkers

Big beer

The default American drink is in a slow, structural decline.

For a century, mass-market beer was the safest bet in the drinks business. Every generation of young adults picked up a can and kept drinking for life. That escalator has stopped. Americans now drink less beer per person than at almost any point in modern record, and the giants built on "everyone drinks" are managing a shrinking market, not a growing one.

The core bet

Big beer bet that beer was a permanent habit — that volume would always grow with the population. Instead, per-person drinking is near a multi-decade low, younger people are starting later or not at all, and the industry's answer is to sell less liquid for more money and to chase whatever the sober-curious drinker will buy instead.

What the numbers say

−26%
The drop in Bud Light US sales in a single week in April 2023 after a boycott. It never recovered — Bud Light lost its spot as America's #1 beer to Modelo Especial, and parent AB InBev shed an estimated $1B+ in US sales.
~30-yr low
US alcohol consumed per person has fallen back toward levels not seen in three decades. Beer, the biggest category, takes the biggest hit in raw volume.
54%
Share of US adults who say they drink at all in 2025 (Gallup) — roughly a 90-year low. The pool of drinkers itself is shrinking, not just how much each one drinks.

How they're responding

The giants are pushing in three directions at once: premium (charge more per can), non-alcoholic (AB InBev wants a big chunk of sales to be no- or low-alcohol), and beyond beer (seltzers, canned cocktails, even stakes in cannabis). The honest problem: none of these is growing fast enough to fully replace the plain lager that's fading.

What's newly valuable
Non-alcoholic beer (the fastest-growing corner of the shelf), premium and imported brands, and canned formats that feel lighter.
What's draining
Cheap domestic lager, the "beer with the guys" ritual, and the assumption that each new generation refills the room.
The single biggest worry

If the decline is generational — young people simply drinking less for life — then it doesn't bounce back when they hit their 30s and 40s the way lighter youth drinking used to. The low baseline may stick, which turns a soft patch into a permanent shrink.

The surprising part

The world's biggest beer makers are now racing to sell you beer without the beer. AB InBev has said it wants a large share of its volume to be no- or low-alcohol — the maker of Budweiser openly planning for a future where a lot of its "beer" has no buzz in it at all. That's the tell: they believe the decline is real.

In one sentence

Big beer isn't losing to a better beer — it's losing to a generation that drinks less of everything, and its own executives are quietly betting the future on the non-alcoholic version of their own product.
