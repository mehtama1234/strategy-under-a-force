Group D · The industry's response

Hard seltzer & RTD

Lighter, canned, lower-calorie — the industry chasing the health-minded drinker.

Faced with drinkers who want fewer calories and less commitment, the industry's first big answer was hard seltzer — and then ready-to-drink (RTD) canned cocktails. These formats fit the moment: light, portable, calorie-counted, easy to have just one. But the hard-seltzer story is also a warning about how fast these trends can turn.

The core bet

The bet is that even a drinking-less generation will buy alcohol if it feels light and healthy-ish — a 100-calorie seltzer or a single canned cocktail instead of a heavy beer or a full bottle. RTD is now the one corner of alcohol still growing, so the whole industry is piling in.

What the numbers say

+160%
How fast hard seltzer grew at its 2020 peak, led by White Claw, to about a $4.1B US category. For a moment it looked like the future of drinking.
$102M
The writedown Boston Beer took on unsold Truly seltzer as the boom cracked — a vivid lesson that these formats boom and bust fast.
only gainer
RTD was roughly the only alcohol category still growing in 2025 (~+2% volume, +4% value) while beer, wine, and spirits all fell. The industry's lifeboat is a can.

The pivot inside the pivot

When plain seltzer cooled, the money moved to RTD canned cocktails — a real margarita or vodka soda in a can, from spirits brands. It's the same logic (light, portable, controlled) but with a spirit's price and margin. Every major spirits company now has a canned-cocktail line.

What's newly valuable
Low-calorie, single-serve, grab-and-go formats; the RTD canned cocktail; anything that fits "I'll just have one."
What's risky
These are fashion categories. Seltzer's crash showed how a format can go from savior to writedown in two years.
The single biggest worry

RTD is a format, not a fix. It can win share within a shrinking market, but a lighter can still contains alcohol — so it doesn't answer the deeper forces (cancer warnings, GLP-1, wellness) pulling people away from drinking entirely.

The surprising part

The industry's most successful response to "people are drinking less" was to sell them a drink that feels like drinking less — fewer calories, one can, a number on the label. It's less a new product than a new packaging of restraint. And even that only slows the decline; it doesn't reverse it.

In one sentence

Hard seltzer and canned cocktails are alcohol repackaged for people who want to drink less — a smart way to win a shrinking market, not a way to grow it again.
