Group D · The industry's response

Premiumization

Drink less, but pay more — the industry's main growth strategy for 20 years.

For two decades, the drinks industry had one answer to "people are drinking less": get them to spend more per drink. The logic is clean — one $50 bottle of tequila replaces six $5 beers, so revenue holds even as volume falls. It worked brilliantly for years. Then, around 2024, it hit a wall.

The core bet

Premiumization is the wager that drinkers will trade quantity for quality — fewer, better, more expensive drinks. It powered a celebrity-spirits gold rush and propped up the whole industry through the early decline. The problem: you can't premiumize your way past a generation that isn't drinking at all.

What the numbers say

$1B
What Diageo paid for George Clooney's Casamigos tequila in 2017 — the deal that launched the celebrity-premium boom and proved this strategy could scale fast.
−1.1%
US spirits revenue in 2024 — the first decline since 2004. The top shelf fell hardest: the $100+ category down 8.5%, the $50–$100 tier down 4.3%.
−4%
Total alcohol value across leading markets in 2025 — falling faster than volume (−2%). That's the exact opposite of what premiumization promises.
the pivot
By 2025, majors including Diageo quietly abandoned pure premium and moved toward "evenly weighted" portfolios and cheap, strong RTDs — an admission the trade-up had stalled.

Why it stalled

Premiumization assumed a stable pool of drinkers who'd simply spend more. But the pool is shrinking, and in a squeezed economy even the remaining drinkers stopped reaching for the top shelf. When both the number of drinkers and their willingness to splurge fall together, "charge more per drink" runs out of room.

What still works
Genuine luxury and story-driven brands for a smaller, wealthy core; the top of tequila and agave; anything a collector wants.
What broke
The mass "trade-up," the assumption that margin can forever replace volume, and the celebrity-brand flood now chasing fewer buyers.
The single biggest worry

Premiumization can't offset generational non-drinking. Even if every remaining drinker trades up in quality, there are fewer drinkers each year to carry those premium margins — and there's no price high enough to sell to someone who's decided not to drink.

The surprising part

The industry's honest bet was never really "drink better" — it was "spend more so we can worry less about volume." That trick bought twenty years and a billion-dollar celebrity-tequila boom. But 2024 revealed the ceiling: when top-shelf sales fall faster than everything else, the growth engine of the last two decades is the first thing to stall.

In one sentence

Premiumization worked until it didn't — you can charge a shrinking group of drinkers more, right up until they stop drinking, and then there's nothing left to premiumize.
