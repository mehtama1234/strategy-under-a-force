Group A · Losing the drinkers

Wine

A Boomer drink with no younger generation to replace it.

Wine is in worse shape than beer or spirits, and the reason is simple: it was built on one generation. Baby Boomers were the wine cohort, and as they age, drink less, and pass on, no younger group has picked up the habit at the same rate. The result is a glut so severe that growers on two continents are literally ripping vines out of the ground.

The core bet

The wine business assumed each generation would grow into wine the way Boomers did — trading up from beer in their 30s and 40s. That trade-up isn't happening. Demand is falling faster than supply can shrink, leaving too much wine and too few drinkers, and the "healthy glass of red" story that propped it up has quietly collapsed.

What the numbers say

208M
Hectoliters of wine the world drank in 2025 — the fourth straight yearly fall, and the lowest in roughly 60 years. It was ~227M in 2022: about a 14% drop since 2018 (OIV).
38,000
Acres of California vineyard pulled up between Oct 2024 and Aug 2025 — about 7% of the state's vines, with no government payment. Pure oversupply (USDA). Napa −7%, Lodi −10%, Fresno −18%.
27,461
Hectares torn out in France by June 2025. The EU paid growers about €4,000/hectare (~€110M total) to rip up vines; about 1,300 growers are quitting entirely (FranceAgriMer).
53M hl
Record unsold wine sitting in Italian warehouses in May 2025 — close to a full year of the country's own drinking, stuck in inventory.

The demographic trap

US wine drinkers fell by roughly 9 million to 76 million between 2023 and 2025, and Boomers slid from 32% to 26% of them. Gen Z's monthly wine occasions are down about 34% since 2019. There is no cohort behind the Boomers pulling the same weight — which is why this reads as structural, not a bad vintage.

What's draining
Mass-market wine, the "bottle with dinner" ritual, and the vineyards themselves — being cut down as an asset that no longer pays.
What survives
Premium and story-driven bottles, and regions that can shrink to fit a smaller, older, wealthier customer base.
The single biggest worry

Wine is squeezed from both ends at once: demand keeps falling while inventories still hit records. Growers can rip out vines for years and still not catch down to a shrinking, aging customer base — the definition of genuine structural oversupply.

The surprising part

Wine's edge was the "responsible, healthy" drink — the glass of red that was supposedly good for your heart. That story came from the 1990s "French Paradox" and resveratrol hype, and one of its key researchers was later found guilty of 145 counts of data fabrication. Health bodies have since reversed entirely: the WHO's "no safe level" (Jan 2023) and the American Heart Association dropping any heart-health claim (2025). The very image that made wine feel virtuous is now the thing working against it hardest.

In one sentence

Wine rode one aging generation and one health myth to the top, and now that both are gone, the world has far more wine than drinkers — so the vines are coming out of the ground.
