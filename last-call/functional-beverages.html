Group B · The sober economy

Functional beverages

From a drink that's bad for you to one that claims to be good for you.

If the sober generation isn't reaching for a beer, what is in their hand? Increasingly it's a "functional" drink — a soda that claims to help your gut, a sparkling water with adaptogens for calm, an electrolyte or protein drink. These flip the entire premise of alcohol: instead of a treat that costs you something, they're sold as a treat that gives you something back.

The core bet

The bet is that the social drink of the future is a wellness product. The same money and moment that used to go to a cocktail now goes to a prebiotic soda or a calming tonic — something that feels like a reward but reads as self-care.

What the numbers say

~$2B
What PepsiCo paid for Poppi in 2025 — a prebiotic "gut-health" soda barely a few years old. When a giant pays that for a functional-soda startup, the category has arrived.
Olipop
The other big prebiotic-soda brand, valued around $1.85B in 2025 — built on the pitch of soda's fun with "3 grams of sugar" and fiber. Old soda giants are now chasing the format they were losing to.
Adaptogens
"Calm," "focus," and mood drinks with ingredients like ashwagandha and L-theanine are a fast-growing shelf — sold explicitly as an alcohol alternative for winding down.

Why it fits the moment

A generation that reframed not drinking as a health choice needs something to drink instead — and it wants that something to be on-brand with the wellness identity. Functional drinks are perfectly aimed: they're the anti-alcohol, a beverage you can post about that signals you're taking care of yourself.

What's newly valuable
The "better-for-you" beverage shelf, the wellness story on the can, and any drink that owns the after-work or party moment without alcohol.
What's shaky
Many health claims are vague or thin, and regulators (and lawsuits) are starting to test whether a "gut-health soda" really does what the label implies.
The single biggest worry

The category runs on health claims that are often loosely supported. If regulators crack down on "functional" marketing — or a big lawsuit sticks — the premium these drinks charge could evaporate, and they'd be back to competing as expensive soda.

The surprising part

Soda spent decades as the villain of the health conversation. Now a reinvented, low-sugar, "good-for-your-gut" version of soda is one of the drinks replacing alcohol on wellness grounds. The bad-for-you drink of the 2000s became the virtuous drink of the 2020s by changing what's inside and the story on the outside.

In one sentence

The sober generation still wants something in its hand — and it's paying a premium for drinks that promise to give health back instead of taking it away.
