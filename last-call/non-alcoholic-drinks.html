Group B · The sober economy

Non-alcoholic drinks

Sell the ritual — the cold can, the fancy glass — minus the booze.

The clearest winner from the decline of drinking is the drink that looks and feels like alcohol but has none in it. Non-alcoholic beer, wine, and spirits let people keep the ritual — the toast, the after-work beer, the thing in your hand at a party — without the hangover, the calories, or the health worry. It's the fastest-growing corner of the entire drinks shelf.

The core bet

The bet is that people didn't only want alcohol — they wanted the ritual alcohol came wrapped in. Give them a grown-up drink that fits the social moment, and many will happily skip the buzz. The category is small but growing fast, and the big brewers are pouring money in.

What the numbers say

+32%
Roughly how fast US non-alcoholic beer sales grew year over year (recent data), while regular beer slid. It's the standout growth story in a shrinking market.
~$800M
Valuation of Athletic Brewing, a non-alcoholic-only brewer founded in 2017 that came from nowhere to lead the US NA beer category — proof this is a real business, not a novelty.
0.0 > original
In the UK, Guinness 0.0 — the no-alcohol version — has at times outsold expectations dramatically as the brand's fastest grower, a sign the sober option can rival the original.

Why the big players care

For a giant like AB InBev or Heineken, non-alcoholic beer is a rare growth lane and it often carries higher margins — no alcohol tax, and shoppers will pay full price for a "premium" zero. That's why Budweiser, Heineken, and Guinness all now push a 0.0. The sober drink isn't a threat to them; it's the lifeboat.

What's newly valuable
A credible grown-up drink for the sober moment; brands built NA-first; the higher margin of a tax-free "premium" zero.
The catch
Taste still lags for wine and spirits especially, and much of the volume is existing drinkers swapping some occasions, not a brand-new market.
The single biggest worry

Non-alcoholic beer is convincing; NA wine and spirits often are not. If the taste gap doesn't close, the category stays a beer-shaped niche rather than a full replacement for the drinks cabinet — and the math (a few percent of a shrinking market) never fully offsets the decline of the real thing.

The surprising part

The people making the best non-alcoholic beer are, increasingly, the same giant brewers whose regular beer is fading. They're competing with their own core product on purpose — because they'd rather sell you a Budweiser Zero than lose you to a soda or a seltzer entirely.

In one sentence

The sober drink wins by keeping everything about alcohol except the alcohol — and the brewers losing the old market are the ones racing hardest to build the new one.
