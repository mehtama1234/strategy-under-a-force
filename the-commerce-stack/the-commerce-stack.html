Group E · The big picture

The commerce stack

The pipes got rich; the brands got squeezed — because starting a store stopped being scarce.

Pull the whole collection together and one line runs through it. Starting an online store became nearly free, so the store stopped being the scarce thing. The money moved away from the merchants and toward the picks-and-shovels everyone still needs — the software to run it (Shopify), the payments to process it (Stripe, the card networks), the ads to find customers (Meta and Google), and the shipping to deliver it (3PLs and Amazon). The arms dealers win while most brands get squeezed.

The core bet

Starting a store became free so the store stopped being scarce; the money moved to the tools, the ads, and the shipping — and the arms dealers win while most brands get squeezed.

Winners and losers

The stack sorts cleanly into who collects the tolls and who pays them.

Who ends up ahead

The arms dealers — Shopify, Stripe and the card networks, Meta and Google, Amazon, the 3PLs, and the app-makers — who collect a fee on every sale or a subscription whether the brand profits or not.

Who's exposed

Most DTC brands, squeezed between rising ad costs and Amazon's gravity; the aggregators that imploded; and anyone who mistook cheap ads for a moat.

The single biggest worry

The infrastructure needs healthy merchants — Shopify needs profitable brands, Stripe needs volume, Meta needs spending customers — yet the merchant is the layer with the thinnest margin. The system taxes every part of the sale before the brand sees a cent, so on many sales the brand is the only stakeholder losing money while everyone else collects a percentage.

The surprising part

The note to end on is the paradox at the core: making it easy for everyone to sell made selling worthless and the tools priceless. When supply exploded, competition for attention collapsed brand margins, and the real cost of a sale became the advertising to get found — not the product or the store. The margin migrated from the merchant to the infrastructure, and on many sales a brand now keeps the least of anyone in the chain: less than the processor, less than the shipper, less than the ad platform. The scarce thing was never the store. It was being seen, getting shipped, and getting paid — and someone else owns all three.

In one sentence

When starting a store became free, the money stopped flowing to merchants and started flowing to whoever charged rent for the tools to sell — Shopify, Stripe, Meta, Google, Amazon, and the shippers who together tax most of a brand's revenue.
