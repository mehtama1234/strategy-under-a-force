The Commerce Stack

Starting a store became free — so the money moved to the tools, the ads, and the shipping.

The 44th force. E-commerce exploded and launching an online store went from hard to trivial — Shopify, a supplier, some ads, and you're live in an afternoon. But when starting a store became free, the store stopped being the scarce thing. The scarce things became getting seen (customer acquisition, taxed by Meta and Google), getting the product to the door (logistics, judged against Amazon Prime), and getting paid (the payment toll). So the picks-and-shovels — Shopify, Stripe, the ad duopoly, the 3PLs, the marketing apps — capture the reliable margin, while most direct-to-consumer brands get squeezed. The one idea: the arms dealers of e-commerce win; most of the soldiers don't.

Seeded by the Inc. 5000 data This is the commerce half of "Creator & Commerce," the single biggest fast-growing theme by count — 867 companies, ~$32.8B represented — packed with DTC brands, Shopify-ecosystem apps, payments, fulfillment, and marketing-tech firms. This collection follows that money.
The one question, asked of everyone

What did the commerce stack make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly worth money? Starting a store became cheap and abundant, so the store lost its value; traffic, logistics, and payments became the scarce chokepoints worth a fortune; and the safest profit turned out to be the toll, not the brand. The danger: when the tolls (ads + payments + shipping + apps) exceed the margin, the brands can't survive — and the arms dealers are left squeezing a base they need healthy.
