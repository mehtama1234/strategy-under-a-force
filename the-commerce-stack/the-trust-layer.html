Group B · The two things that got scarce

The trust layer

In a sea of unknown brands, a five-star average is worth more than the product's actual quality.

An unknown online brand needs to prove it's legit, so a whole layer of trust-tech exists: reviews and ratings (Yotpo, Okendo, Trustpilot, Amazon reviews), user-generated content, and trust badges. Reviews drive conversion, so collecting and displaying social proof became a paid service — and a battleground against fakes.

The core bet

Shoppers won't buy from a brand they don't trust, so software that collects and displays social proof — reviews, ratings, UGC — sells into every store. Trust became a product.

Trust itself became a SaaS category

Because shoppers won't buy from strangers, the software that manufactures credibility — collecting reviews, displaying stars, surfacing customer photos — became a multi-billion-dollar category. Stores now optimize for review count and rating before durability, because the first customer won't buy without the score, and the score is a software artifact.

The single biggest worry

Fake and AI-generated reviews are corroding the whole system — shoppers increasingly distrust five-star averages on unknown brands, inviting regulation and leaving honest sellers caught between compliance costs and gamed competitors.

The surprising part

The inversion is striking: in a market flooded with unknown brands, a credible star rating became worth more than the product behind it. The score, not the quality, closes the first sale — which is why 'trust' turned from a reputation you earn over years into a service you buy by the month.

In one sentence

Reviews became a multi-billion-dollar SaaS layer because shoppers won't buy from strangers — and faking that trust is so profitable it's now a running battle with regulators.
