Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Why selling got commoditized

Making it free for everyone to sell made selling worthless — and the tools priceless.

This is the mechanism. Starting an online store went from hard (custom code, warehouses, retail deals) to trivial (Shopify + a supplier + ads) in a decade. When the barrier to entry collapses, everyone floods in, the product gets easy to copy, and margins get competed away — so profit flees the store itself and pools in the scarce chokepoints everyone still needs: traffic, logistics, payments. It's a textbook case of value migration.

The core bet

When starting a store became nearly free, the store stopped being scarce — so profit migrated to the few things every store still can't avoid: getting seen, getting shipped, getting paid.

The barrier to entry fell, but the barrier to being seen didn't

Opening a store became free, but getting visibility and transactions did not — so those got owned by whoever sells access: Shopify on software, Meta and Google on traffic, Amazon and Stripe on logistics and money. The barrier moved from starting up to standing out, and the toll-collectors captured the new bottleneck.

The single biggest worry

If the store has no moat — no brand, no unique product, just a resold item — the seller isn't a business, they're a price-taker: squeezed by suppliers, the ad auction, the processor, and the marketplace all at once, none of whom they can negotiate with.

The surprising part

The deep point is that 'democratizing' commerce didn't democratize profit — it inverted it. Making stores free made them worthless and made the platforms indispensable. Shopify won not by selling stores but by being the store everyone must use; the same is true of the ad duopoly, Amazon, and the card networks. The winners sell access, not products.

In one sentence

When opening a store became free, selling became worthless and the only profit left went to whoever controls traffic, shipping, and money — the chokepoints everyone still depends on.
