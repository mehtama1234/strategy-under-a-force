Group D · The mechanism & the tensions

Who profits

On many DTC sales, the brand keeps the least of anyone in the chain.

This is the follow-the-money piece. The durable profit goes not to most brands but to the 'arms dealers': Shopify (software + payments), Stripe and the card networks (payments), Meta and Google (the ads that acquire customers), Amazon (marketplace fees + fulfillment + ads), the 3PLs (shipping), and the app-makers. Each takes a cut of every sale, and together they can consume the majority of a brand's revenue.

The core bet

The reliable money is in the tolls every merchant must pay — software, payments, ads, shipping — not in the brands themselves, most of which get squeezed.

What the numbers say

~30% / ~3%
Rough shares of a DTC sale taken by ads / payments — before shipping and platform fees.
10–15%
Shipping's share of a typical order; add platform/marketplace fees and the brand's sliver shrinks fast.
~$8B
Shopify's 2024 revenue; Amazon seller-services (~$140B) plus its ads (~$50B) dwarf it.
~65%
Meta and Google's combined share of global digital ad spend — they price every customer.
~$1.4T
Stripe's payment volume — the arms dealers move trillions while brands fight over crumbs.

The DTC 'revolution' just swapped one landlord for several

A brand that built a Shopify store and bought Instagram ads never escaped dependency — it swapped a wholesale buyer (Target) for a new set of landlords (Meta, Shopify, Stripe, Amazon fulfillment). Billions went into brands; trillions went through the arms dealers. The safest e-commerce bet was never a product — it was the software, the ad network, or the payment rail.

The single biggest worry

The chokepoint owners — the ad duopoly, Amazon, the card networks — hold pricing power over the brands and no incentive to keep it gentle, so brand margins thin not because brands are inefficient but because the toll keeps rising in lockstep.

The surprising part

The clean restatement is that e-commerce didn't disrupt retail — it moved the profit from store owners to the companies that run the digital pipes and ad auctions. Add up the cuts and on many sales the brand keeps the least of anyone: less than the payment processor, less than the 3PL, less than the ad platform.

In one sentence

E-commerce didn't disrupt retail — it moved the profit from store owners to the companies that run the pipes and the ad auctions.
