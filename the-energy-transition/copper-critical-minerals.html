Group A · The picks & shovels

Copper & minerals — electrify everything, and you need a mountain of it

The transition is really a mining story. And you can't open a mine in a hurry.

Every part of the electric future runs on metal. An electric car uses about 3.5× the copper of a gas car; wind turbines, solar farms, and grid wires are all copper-hungry; batteries need lithium, nickel, and cobalt. The catch: a new mine takes 10–20 years to open. So the boring mining industry quietly became the choke point.

The demand wall vs. the supply wall

Demand explodes

The world mines ~20 million tonnes of copper a year. Clean energy alone could need 30+ million tonnes a year by 2040. Lithium demand could grow many times over.

Supply crawls

A new copper mine takes 10–20 years from discovery to first metal; permitting alone can be 5–10 years. And ore grades keep falling, so you dig more rock for less metal.

~80 kg
Copper in a typical EV, versus ~20 kg in a gas car. Multiply that across a billion vehicles, plus the grids and turbines to power them.

The lithium head-fake

Lithium's price crashed about 80% — from ~$64,000 a tonne in late 2022 to ~$12,000–15,000 in 2024. That looked like the shortage was over. It wasn't. The crash exposed the real bottleneck: not digging the mineral out, but refining it — and China controls most of that step (~85% of lithium refining).

The main worry

The single biggest risk

If mines and refineries can't scale by ~2030–2035, the whole transition slows down — not because of physics or policy, but because there simply isn't enough metal. A 5–10 year permitting delay quietly becomes a 20-year delay for the electric future.

The surprising part

Two twists. First, old EV batteries are becoming mines — recycling now recovers 90%+ of the lithium and cobalt inside them, so a worn-out car turns into raw material. Second, and more important: the real power isn't in the ground, it's in the refining. China doesn't have the world's copper or lithium deposits — it has the factories that process them. The lithium price crash didn't signal success; it revealed that whoever controls refining controls the transition.

In one sentence

Copper & minerals in a nutshell

Electrifying everything needs far more copper and battery metal than the world mines today, and because mines take 20 years to open and China controls refining, geology and geopolitics — not technology — became the real limit on the transition.
