Utilities & the grid — sleepy monopolies became the center of everything

For decades US power demand barely grew. Then three things hit at once, and the grid became the bottleneck.

Electric utilities were the most boring business in the world: a regulated monopoly selling a flat amount of power, growing 0–1% a year. The energy transition — plus AI — blew that up. Suddenly demand is surging, and the old, slow grid can't keep up. Utilities went from a backwater to the thing everyone is waiting on.

Why demand suddenly surged

Three waves arrived at the same time: AI data centers (which run 24/7 and eat gigawatts), electrifying cars, heating, and factories (every EV and heat pump plugs into the grid), and reshored manufacturing. After decades of flat demand, forecasters now expect US electricity use to jump 15–20% by 2030.

2,600+ GW
Power projects stuck in the "interconnection queue" waiting to plug into the grid (2024) — about 8× the historical backlog. The typical wait is 7–10 years.

Why the grid can't keep up

  • Building is slow. A big new power line takes 5–7 years just for permits, then 2–3 more to build. A single town can kill a billion-dollar project in court.
  • The gear is scarce. The US needs ~10,000 large transformers a year but builds ~2,000; ~40% of the fleet is over 30 years old. (More on this on the grid-equipment page.)
  • The money is flowing anyway. Utilities' 5-year investment plans jumped ~40–50% since 2020, toward roughly $2 trillion.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Permitting and local opposition — not money or engineering — are now the real limit. If the gap between demand and shovel-ready capacity keeps widening, you get strained grids and the risk of blackouts, and economic growth (data centers, factories) drifts to wherever the grid can actually connect them, not where the talent or customers are.

The surprising part

For the first time in a century, utilities are competing for customers. A data center or factory picking a location sets off a bidding war — fast connection, cheap rates, even subsidies. The sleepy monopoly with captive customers and no growth has flipped into the scarce resource everyone is fighting over. And the twist for the transition: the customer utilities most love isn't a solar farm — it's a data center, a big, steady, round-the-clock load that behaves nothing like the on-and-off nature of wind and sun.

In one sentence

Utilities & the grid in a nutshell

Electrifying everything (plus AI) turned decades of flat power demand into a surge, and the grid's decade-long build times made grid access — not fuel — the scarcest thing in the whole transition.
