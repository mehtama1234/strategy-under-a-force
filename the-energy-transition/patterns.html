The patterns

Line up everyone the transition touched, and one theme dominates: energy is turning from a fuel you rent forever into a machine you build once — and that flips who gets rich.

Each page asks one question: what did the energy transition make scarce, cheap, safe, risky, or newly valuable for this company? Twelve answers later, the same tensions keep showing up. Click any name to jump to its page.

The one thing to take away

Follow the money. When energy stops being fuel you buy forever and becomes a machine you build once, value drains away from whoever sold the fuel (oil, petrostates, gas stations) and pools around whoever builds the machines (China's factories, grid-gear makers) and mines the materials (copper, lithium). Almost every story here is a variation on that one move.

The recurring patterns

Pattern 01

The picks-and-shovels win, not the visionaries

In a gold rush, the reliable money is in shovels, not gold. The transition's steadiest winners aren't the flashy solar or EV firms — they're the boring companies that sell to everyone, whoever wins: the grid, the copper miners, the transformer makers.

Pattern 02

Cheap is not the same as profitable — the commodity trap

Solar and batteries got 90% cheaper, and EVs went mainstream. But once a technology works at scale and prices collapse, there's no edge left to defend — so the makers get crushed on razor margins even as the technology wins. The savings flow to consumers and utilities, not inventors.

Pattern 03

Yesterday's crown jewel becomes today's dead weight

The exact thing that made an incumbent great is what the transition punishes. A century of engine mastery turns worthless in an EV. A gas station's fuel pumps kill its foot traffic. Oil reserves risk becoming assets no one will buy.

legacy automakersgas stationsoil majors
Pattern 04

The bottleneck moved from the flashy part to the boring part

Everyone watches solar panels and car designs. The real chokepoints are unglamorous: the transformer, the transmission line, the refining step. Whoever controls the dull middle of the chain holds more power than the headline players.

grid equipmentthe gridrefining
Pattern 05

We swapped oil dependence for China dependence

The transition was meant to free the West from Middle-East oil. Instead it created a new dependence — on Chinese factories for panels, batteries, and EVs, and on Chinese refining for the minerals. A shift from fuel to machines that China quietly won.

China's gripbatteriesminerals
Pattern 06

Going green got punished; staying dirty got rewarded (for now)

The cruelest short-run twist. BP's climate pivot tanked its stock; Exxon's oil focus made record profits. The market kept rewarding the fuel-sellers and punishing the switchers — so even Europe's majors walked their promises back.

oil majors
Pattern 07

Cheap energy becomes the incumbent's weapon against its own obsolescence

The Gulf states' one great asset — dirt-cheap power — is exactly what they're using to build the post-oil economy (AI data centers) that's meant to replace oil. The old advantage funds the escape from the old advantage.

Five forces, one method

The point of doing all five

This is the fifth collection built the same way — after AI, Ozempic, the decoupling, and the end of cheap money. Each force has its own signature: AI's is "you can only trust what you can verify," Ozempic's is "one force, many industries," the decoupling's is "the policy backfires and the walls leak," cheap money's is "the tide went out and revealed who was swimming naked," and the transition's is "a fuel you rent forever becomes a machine you build once." But the method is identical: pick a force, ask what it made scarce/cheap/risky/valuable, group companies by their answer, and read the patterns. Point it at anything big and the stories fall out.
