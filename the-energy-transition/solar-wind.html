Group C · The believers

Solar & wind — the technology won; the makers went broke

Solar is now the cheapest electricity in history. That did not make it a good business.

Solar is the transition's biggest success story. Panel prices fell about 90% over the 2010s, and solar is now the cheapest source of electricity ever, in most of the world. But here's the hard lesson: being cheap is not the same as being profitable. The companies that make the panels keep going bankrupt.

The success

−89%
Fall in solar panel prices, 2010 to 2024 (roughly $2/watt to $0.20/watt). New solar now undercuts new coal, gas, and nuclear in most markets.

The trap: cheap ≠ profitable

Solar makers

China built ~80–85% of the world's panels and flooded the market. Panels became a cutthroat commodity; the industry lost tens of billions in 2024–25 and 50+ Chinese makers went bankrupt.

Wind makers

Offshore wind had a brutal run: Ørsted and Siemens Gamesa took billions in losses (2023–24) from inflation, higher rates, and faulty parts. ~3 GW of US projects were cancelled.

The main worry

The single biggest risk — the hidden bill

The sun sets and the wind drops, so cheap solar and wind need expensive extras: batteries, grid upgrades, and backup. Those costs can rise faster than panel prices fall. So the story shifts from "cheap power" to "cheap power plus a big grid bill" — which squeezes everyone from the generator to the utility.

The surprising part

This is a textbook commodity trap. The technology worked too well: once you prove it at scale and prices collapse, there's no advantage left to defend, and the makers get crushed on razor-thin margins. The winners aren't the panel companies — they're the utilities and consumers who pocket the cheap power. The people who built the miracle mostly went broke building it.

In one sentence

Solar & wind in a nutshell

Solar and wind became the cheapest power on Earth, but that very cheapness turned them into a razor-margin commodity — so the technology won while the companies making it went bankrupt, and the savings flowed to utilities and consumers instead.
