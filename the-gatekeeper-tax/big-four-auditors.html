Group A · Capital gatekeepers

The Big Four auditors

Only four firms are allowed to vouch that a public company is telling the truth.

Every public company must have its books signed off by an auditor — and effectively only four firms (Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG) can do it for large caps. It's a government-mandated seal of approval, and the firms are paid by the very company they're scrutinizing. Big frauds keep slipping through, yet instead of breaking up, the club shrank from eight firms to four and got more entrenched.

The core bet

The bet is that the audit stamp is legally required and only a tiny club can supply it. That makes an audit a compliance tax every public company must pay — to one of four firms that also sell them lucrative consulting, the exact conflict that has enabled the biggest frauds.

What the numbers say

~51%
Share of SEC registrants audited by the Big Four — and 100% of the FTSE 100. For large companies there is effectively no one else.
2001
Enron / Arthur Andersen — the auditor billed ~$1M/week and missed (or blessed) years of fraud. Andersen collapsed, taking the Big Five to the Big Four.
2020
Wirecard / EY — €1.9B in "cash" that never existed, missed across ~10 years of audits. EY drew a small fine and a German ban; investors lost billions.
75%+
Share of Big Four revenue now from consulting — often sold to the same clients they audit. The conflict that caused Enron is bigger than ever.

The club shrank instead of breaking

You'd expect repeated failures — Enron, Carillion, Wirecard — to force a breakup. The opposite happened: the Big Eight consolidated to the Big Four, concentration rose, and consulting revenue exploded. Regulators probe the conflict but have never forced a clean split. Fines land on individual cases; the oligopoly is untouched.

What's newly valuable
The mandatory seal, the four-firm scarcity, and a captive base to cross-sell consulting into.
What's broken
Independence — you can't rigorously audit a client you're also earning consulting fees from.
The single biggest worry

Major frauds slip through repeatedly, yet the failed auditors face fines trivial against their scale. The system punishes individual cases but never the oligopoly — and if any of the four collapsed (as Andersen did), the world would be down to three.

The surprising part

After each failure the Big Four tightened their grip rather than losing it. The frauds that broke Enron and Wirecard flowed from the exact conflict — auditing a client you also consult for — that now generates three-quarters of the firms' revenue. Regulators know, probe, and do nothing structural.

In one sentence

The only four firms allowed to certify that public companies are honest are paid by those companies to also consult for them — an unbreakable oligopoly that has survived its own frauds.
