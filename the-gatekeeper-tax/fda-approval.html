Group B · Health gatekeepers

FDA approval

No drug reaches the market without the permission slip — and the slip is the moat.

No drug reaches US patients without FDA approval — the ultimate permission slip. It protects people, which is real and valuable. But the same gate also builds pharma's biggest moat: approval costs on the order of $1–2B+ and takes a decade, which only giants can afford. And the drug industry funds roughly half the FDA's drug-review budget through "user fees" — so the gatekeeper is partly paid by the gated.

The core bet

The bet — mostly an accident of good intentions — is that the cost and time of the permission slip is so high it doubles as a barrier. Rigorous review keeps patients safe and keeps small challengers out, entrenching whoever can afford the decade-long, billion-dollar climb.

What the numbers say

$1–2.6B
Cost to bring one drug through FDA approval (2009–18 estimates) — cancer/immune drugs at the high end. Only big pharma can absorb that.
10–15 yrs
Typical timeline from research to approval. There is no legal shortcut, even when doctors and makers agree.
~50–77%
Share of the FDA's drug-review budget funded by industry user fees (PDUFA, since 1992) — the regulated pay the regulator, reauthorized every five years.
citizen petitions
Brand makers file petitions (mostly denied) that still delay cheaper generics by months — gaming the gate to extend a monopoly.

Safety and moat are the same thing

The gate's strength (credible, rigorous review) and the moat's height (cost plus time) are inseparable. Approval is expensive because it must be careful — and that carefulness is exactly what blocks small entrants. So the thing that keeps you safe also keeps the incumbents comfortable.

The real value
Patient safety and trust — a genuine public good the gate provides.
The moat
A billion-dollar, decade-long barrier only incumbents can clear, partly funded by those incumbents.
The single biggest worry

If most of the FDA's review budget comes from the companies it regulates, there's a soft conflict — pressure to keep approvals (and fees) flowing, and to preserve a high, incumbent-friendly gate. Not corruption, but capture by design.

The surprising part

The FDA is partly funded by the very industry it polices — by law, not scandal. Pharma pays around half the agency's drug-review budget through user fees, and still games the gate with petitions to delay generics. The permission slip that protects patients is also the drug industry's most durable moat.

In one sentence

The decade-long, billion-dollar FDA approval both protects patients and entrenches pharma — a gate so tall only incumbents can climb it, funded in part by those same incumbents.
