Group A · Capital gatekeepers

Credit-rating agencies

They neither issue nor buy bonds — but almost nothing sells without their stamp.

Three private companies — S&P, Moody's, and Fitch — hold a government-blessed veto over who can borrow and at what price. They don't lend and they don't invest; they just rate, and the issuer pays them for the rating. It's the purest gatekeeper: rent collected on the right to borrow. They blessed the toxic bonds behind 2008 and came out more entrenched.

The core bet

The bet is that a rating is a mandatory permission slip. Investors won't touch an unrated bond and regulations bake ratings into the rules, so every issuer must pay for a stamp — from the very companies they're grading. The gate, not the judgment, is the business.

What the numbers say

~$130T
Global rated debt the Big Three sit astride. S&P and Moody's alone are roughly a 70% duopoly; Fitch is a distant third.
95%+
Share of mortgage-backed securities the agencies stamped AAA before 2008 — the bonds that blew up the world. They settled (S&P ~$1.4B, Moody's ~$864M) and kept the franchise.
1975
The SEC's NRSRO rule locked in their status — a regulatory moat that took decades to crack even slightly. New entrants still can't win trust or rule-book inclusion.
issuer-pays
The issuer hires and pays the rater — who wants a high grade. The investor, who needs an honest one, pays only indirectly. The conflict is the business model.

Failure made them stronger

After the worst call in financial history, no executive was jailed and profits recovered within a few years — because the crisis made the permission slip more mandatory. Banks now lean harder on rated debt for their own capital rules, so the gate is baked into the cost of capital for every borrower on earth.

What's newly valuable
The mandatory stamp, the NRSRO moat, and rent on the cost of capital itself.
What's broken
Accuracy — the raters are paid whether or not they're right, so there's little incentive to be right.
The single biggest worry

There's no structural fix, because the gatekeeper is paid by the issuer (who wants a high rating), not the investor (who needs a true one) — and the franchise is unbreakable even after a multi-trillion-dollar failure.

The surprising part

After rating the bonds that erased trillions and cost millions their homes, the Big Three faced no criminal liability, kept their executives, and grew — because regulation tightened bank capital rules, which made rated debt more essential. The crisis didn't punish the gate; it widened it.

In one sentence

Credit-rating agencies are a government-blessed private monopoly that collects rent on $130T of debt, is paid by the very issuers it grades, and survived blowing the biggest call in history because the system needs the stamp more than it needs it to be right.
