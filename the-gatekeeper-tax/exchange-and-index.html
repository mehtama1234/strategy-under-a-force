Group A · Capital gatekeepers

Exchange & index inclusion

A private committee steers trillions of index dollars — and answers to almost no one.

Two layers gatekeep public companies. Stock exchanges set who may list. But the more powerful gate is index inclusion: a private committee at S&P (or MSCI, FTSE) decides who gets into the S&P 500 — and because trillions in passive funds must buy whatever's in the index, inclusion is worth tens of billions and exclusion can crater a stock. A handful of people quietly direct where the market's money flows.

The core bet

The bet is that passive money has to follow the index — so whoever sets the index is a kingmaker. It's the gatekeeper of gatekeepers: not a regulator, not the market, but a private committee whose "in or out" decision mechanically moves trillions. This ties directly to passive investing.

What the numbers say

$58B
Passive funds were forced to buy roughly $58B of Tesla stock on its Dec 2020 S&P 500 inclusion; the stock jumped ~70% between announcement and entry — on index mechanics, not fundamentals.
$13T
Passive money that must buy whatever the S&P committee includes (~$20T is indexed to the S&P 500). The committee's choice is a buy order for trillions.
discretion
Passing every quantitative test does not guarantee entry — the committee has "almost total discretion" and no duty to explain a rejection.
unregulated
Unlike fund managers, index providers aren't SEC-regulated as advisers and owe investors no fiduciary duty — while earning $1B+ a year in licensing fees.

The "automatic" market isn't

Index funds are sold as rule-based and automatic. In truth they're dependent on the discretionary decisions of an unregulated private committee. Inclusion is the closest thing to an official endorsement in modern markets — and the body granting it is accountable to almost no one, faces obvious conflicts, and needn't justify its calls.

What's newly valuable
The power to direct passive flows, the licensing fees, and a kingmaker role bigger than most regulators.
What's fragile
Opacity and conflict — a closed-room decision can rocket or crater a company with no appeal.
The single biggest worry

A trillion-dollar decision made by 10–20 people in a private room can move a company's value with no market test, public debate, or accountability — a hidden kingmaker steering capital more forcefully than the Fed.

The surprising part

The "passive" revolution handed enormous active power to a tiny committee. Retail investors think they own the whole market automatically; really they own whatever an unregulated private body decides to include — a gatekeeper that earns billions from the very conflicts that bias its choices.

In one sentence

The S&P 500 index committee is a private, unregulated, unaccountable gatekeeper that directs trillions of dollars with discretion greater than most regulators.
