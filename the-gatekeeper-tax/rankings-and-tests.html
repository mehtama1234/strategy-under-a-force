Group C · Credential gatekeepers

Rankings & tests

A magazine and a "nonprofit" quietly gate opportunity — and monetize the anxiety.

Two private gatekeepers sit over college. A weekly magazine, US News & World Report, became the de-facto judge of university quality — and schools reshape billions in spending to game its formula. Meanwhile the College Board (a "nonprofit") runs the SAT and AP exams, charging tens of millions of students to sit the tests that gate admission, earning over a billion dollars a year. The gatekeepers of merit are private businesses.

The core bet

The bet is that prestige and admission need a scorekeeper — and whoever holds the scorecard can charge for it. US News monetizes rankings; the College Board and ETS monetize the exams. Both tax the anxiety around getting in, one from schools, one from students.

What the numbers say

1983
US News began ranking colleges — now a magazine's opaque formula, built on self-reported (and gameable) data, dictates how universities spend billions.
$1.2B+
College Board's annual revenue (a "nonprofit"), with a ~$140M surplus in one year — administering the SAT to 2M+ students at $68+ each, plus AP exams.
$185–238
What the grad-school gates cost per sitting — LSAT ~$238, GRE ~$220, TOEFL ~$185. ETS runs ~50M exams a year across 180 countries.
2022
Yale and Harvard law quit the US News law rankings — the first big revolt against being ruled by a magazine's formula.

Nonprofit in name

The gatekeepers are tax-exempt 501(c)(3)s that behave like highly profitable corporations — a nine-figure surplus here, 50 million exams there, the details buried in filings few read. Schools comply with the rankings even when they resent them, because prestige — not learning — is what students are really paying for.

What they claim to provide
A common yardstick — comparability for students and a signal of quality.
What they actually do
Monetize a private scorecard and a mandatory exam, rewarding gaming over genuine outcomes.
The single biggest worry

A private magazine's opaque, gameable formula steers university strategy more than educational outcomes do — while "nonprofit" testers charge tens of millions of students for the exams that gate their futures.

The surprising part

A weekly magazine quietly dictates how universities behave and spend, and a "nonprofit" runs a billion-dollar business charging students to sit the tests that decide their futures. Even Yale and Harvard couldn't ignore the rankings until they publicly walked away — proof of how much power a private scorecard can hold.

In one sentence

Private gatekeepers — a magazine deciding who deserves prestige, a "nonprofit" charging to sit the entrance exams — monetize anxiety about merit while hiding behind tax-exempt status.
