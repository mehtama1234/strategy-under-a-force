Group C · Credential gatekeepers

Professional licensing

About 1 in 5 US jobs now needs the guild's permission — mostly to limit supply.

To work in roughly a fifth of American jobs you need a government license, granted by boards usually run by people already in the trade — a guild protecting its own. Some gates are sensible (surgeons). Many aren't (florists, hair-braiders, interior designers) and mainly limit supply to keep incumbents' prices up. Licensing raises consumer prices with little proven gain in safety or quality.

The core bet

The bet is that requiring a license — set by the incumbents — restricts who can compete, which props up prices and wages for those already inside. It's a supply-control tax dressed as consumer protection.

What the numbers say

~22%
Share of US jobs needing an occupational license (2024) — up from ~5% in the 1950s. A roughly fourfold rise in 70 years.
+15%
Roughly how much licensing raises consumer prices for those services — an estimated $200B+ a year in excess spending.
~20%
Drop in foreign-born employment in a licensed occupation; military spouses face far higher relocation costs because licenses don't transfer across states.
little safety gain
Studies (incl. 2026 reviews) find "little evidence" licensing improves quality or safety — customers pick on price and reputation, not license status.

The self-dealing loop

Boards set their own entry rules with minimal oversight — a perfect loop: incumbents vote to raise the barriers (more required hours, higher fees, no reciprocity) to protect their turf, not consumers. And the gates spread to the harmless jobs (hair-braiding, florists) far more than to the dangerous ones.

Where it's justified
Genuinely high-risk work (surgery, aviation) — real safety gates.
Where it isn't
Low-risk trades where hours and fees balloon for no safety gain, blocking the poor, immigrants, and people with records.
The single biggest worry

Licensing boards set their own entrance rules with little oversight — a self-dealing loop where incumbents raise barriers to protect their turf, not consumers' safety, and pay almost no penalty for it.

The surprising part

You'd expect licensing to guard the dangerous jobs. Instead it spreads to florists and interior designers, where training requirements can balloon to years and thousands of dollars for no proven safety gain. A homeowner pays extra for a license they never asked for that doesn't predict quality — a guild tax on everyday work.

In one sentence

Licensing is a guild tax: it moves ~$200B a year from consumers to licensed workers and blocks the poor and immigrants — with no clear improvement in safety or quality.
