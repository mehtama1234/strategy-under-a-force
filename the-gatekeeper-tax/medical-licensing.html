Group B · Health gatekeepers

Medical licensing

America's doctor shortage is largely by design.

To practice medicine you must pass a chain of gates — med school, the residency match, board certification, state licensing. The tightest gate is the number of residency slots, frozen at roughly 1997 levels because they're tied to a capped pool of Medicare funding. The population grew by 75+ million; the pipeline barely moved. The scarcity that keeps doctor pay high and patients waiting is manufactured, not natural.

The core bet

The bet, baked into a 1997 law, is that limiting the pipeline keeps the profession scarce and well-paid. Cap the residency slots and you cap how many new doctors can ever exist — a supply throttle the guild has little incentive to loosen.

What the numbers say

1997
The Balanced Budget Act froze Medicare-funded residency slots at ~1996 levels — fearing a doctor surplus. The cap has held for ~29 years.
~98,000
Residency slots the pipeline stalled at, even as the US population grew 75M+. The bottleneck is the slots, not the applicants.
86k–124k
Projected US physician shortage by the mid-2030s (AAMC/HRSA). 83M+ Americans already live in primary-care shortage areas.
2,400+
Qualified applicants left with no residency slot in 2025; foreign-trained doctors are ~27–35% less likely to match and often must redo residency to practice.

A policy trap

The cap is hard to lift because it needs Congress, and physicians benefit from the scarcity, so the lobby has little reason to push. The result: the US has far fewer practicing physicians per capita than Germany or Sweden — not because Americans can't train doctors, but because policy froze the pipeline three decades ago.

Who it protects
Incumbent physicians — scarcity supports wages and status.
Who it costs
Patients (waits, shortages), and would-be and foreign-trained doctors shut out by the gate.
The single biggest worry

The cap is a durable trap: fixing it takes Congressional action, but the group that benefits — the profession — has little incentive to lobby for it. Every year it holds, access degrades further.

The surprising part

Congress capped residencies in 1997 to prevent an imagined surplus. It produced the exact opposite — a manufactured shortage that has persisted for a generation because the guild quietly benefits from it. The scarcity that keeps physician pay high is legislative, not a fact of nature.

In one sentence

America's doctor shortage is a policy artifact — a 1997 freeze on residency funding, kept in place because scarcity serves the very people the gate lets through.
