Group D · The mechanism & the fight

The revolving door

The gates hold because the rule-makers and the ruled are the same people.

Why do gatekeeper tolls almost never fall? Because the people who set the rules are often employed by — or soon will be — the industries they regulate. Pharma execs move to and from the FDA; generals join defense contractors; bank regulators join the banks. Industry writes the rules that raise the drawbridge behind itself. This is "regulatory capture," and it's the engine under the whole collection.

The core bet

The bet — really an equilibrium, not a plot — is that a regulator who will one day work for the industry has weak incentive to police it hard. So the gate isn't neutral: it's shaped by the very people it's supposed to check, and every new "safety" rule doubles as a barrier only incumbents can afford.

What the numbers say

9 of 10
FDA commissioners (2006–2019) who went to work for pharma after leaving — while the agency draws roughly half its drug-review budget from the industry.
672
Former Pentagon officials working as lobbyists, board members, or execs at the top 20 defense contractors (2022) — 91% as registered lobbyists.
~800
Times ex-SEC staff (2006–2010) advised or lobbied the agency they'd just left — some within days of departure.
1971
Economist George Stigler showed that regulation tends to be captured by the industry and designed for its benefit — and the pattern has only strengthened since.

Every safety rule is also a moat

New compliance rules filter out upstart competitors but rarely hurt incumbents — who helped write them. So each "safety cycle" makes the gate stronger, not weaker, and the people who designed it just got hired by the biggest firm inside. The barrier grows with every reform.

Who benefits
Incumbents (a higher wall) and the gatekeepers (more to enforce, more fees).
The honest caveat
Some expertise flow is legitimate — you want regulators who understand the industry. The problem is the incentive it creates.
The single biggest worry

Compliance costs from new rules filter out challengers but never harm the incumbents who wrote them — so the gate grows stronger with each safety cycle, and the rule-writer's next employer is the biggest firm behind it.

The surprising part

Stigler's fifty-year-old insight has strengthened, not faded. The commissioner-to-pharma exodus isn't a scandal — it's the expected career path. The revolving door isn't corruption; it's the normal equilibrium. Every regulator quietly knows: push the industry too hard, and there's no job waiting on the other side.

In one sentence

Regulators who will someday work for the industry they police have weak reason to police hard — so they write rules that raise the barrier for everyone else.
