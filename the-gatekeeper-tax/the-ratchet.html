Group D · The mechanism & the fight

The ratchet

The toll only rises — and the gate keeps failing at its one job.

Two linked truths. First, gatekeeper tolls only ratchet up: once mandatory, a rater or board or certifier can raise fees and pile on requirements with little pushback, because you have no choice. Second, the gates repeatedly fail at their stated purpose — and when they do, the gatekeeper rarely pays; the public does. The permission slip is sold as protection, yet the biggest disasters sailed right through it.

The core bet

The ratchet works because gatekeepers earn money twice — once from the fee, and once from failure. Compliance is mandatory, so tolls climb; and because the gatekeeper is paid whether or not it's right, there's little incentive to actually catch the fraud or the flaw.

The gates that failed anyway

2008
Rating agencies stamped AAA on hundreds of billions in junk mortgage bonds. Fines came years later; the public lost trillions and their homes.
Enron / Wirecard
Auditors signed off on Enron (2001) and Wirecard's €1.9B of nonexistent cash (2020). Shareholders lost tens of billions; the auditors paid comparatively little.
346 dead
The Boeing 737 MAX passed FAA certification — using Boeing's own engineers as delegated certifiers — then crashed twice (2018–19).
$2T+
Estimated annual US regulatory-compliance cost, climbing for years — the toll rises across the economy regardless of whether the gates work.

Paid whether or not it's right

Vioxx was FDA-approved before being pulled after tens of thousands of heart attacks. Each disaster carried the gatekeeper's seal — "AAA means safe," "audited means honest," "certified means airworthy" — and each seal was wrong. Yet the fees keep flowing and the tolls keep rising, because the gatekeeper's income doesn't depend on being correct.

The moral hazard
Barriers protect incumbents and boost gatekeeper revenue; failure carries almost no penalty. Profit from the fee, profit from the failure.
The honest caveat
Most of the time the gates do work quietly — we only see the failures. The problem is the lack of accountability when they don't.
The single biggest worry

Gatekeepers are structurally rewarded to raise tolls and complexity but suffer almost nothing when their core mission fails — the moral hazard is near-total: they profit from fees and from failure alike.

The surprising part

Every major disaster of the past 25 years — 2008, Enron, Wirecard, Vioxx, the 737 MAX — sailed straight through the gate, with the gatekeeper's seal on it. The toll kept rising while the protection it promised kept failing, because the gatekeeper collects the fee whether or not the call is right.

In one sentence

The ratchet holds because gatekeepers earn twice — from the fee and from the failure — while bearing almost none of the cost when they miss.
